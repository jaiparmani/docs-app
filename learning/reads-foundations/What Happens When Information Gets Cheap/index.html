    What Happens When Information Gets Cheap¶

    6 min read

    Before movable type reached Europe in the mid-fifteenth century, producing a book meant a person copying it out by hand, for months. Books were correspondingly rare and expensive, and the set of people who could participate in producing written ideas was tiny — largely institutional, largely religious, largely constrained by who could afford the labour.

    Then the cost per copy collapsed. Not to zero, but by an order of magnitude that changed who could publish and what was worth publishing at all.

    What followed is the closest thing history offers to a controlled experiment in what happens when the cost of copying information falls off a cliff. It's worth studying carefully, and mostly not for the reasons people usually cite.

    What actually changed, and what didn't¶

    The first correction: literacy did not transform overnight. Most people in Europe remained unable to read for a long time afterwards. The printing press did not immediately create a reading public.

    What it changed was the economics of production. When a book required a year of skilled copying, only works with guaranteed institutional demand justified the investment. When a print run could produce hundreds of copies from one typesetting, suddenly it made economic sense to publish things with smaller, more speculative, more contentious audiences.

    This is the shift that matters. Cheap copying didn't primarily give more people access to existing books. It changed which ideas were economically viable to circulate at all — and that turned out to be the more consequential effect by a wide margin.

    The upheaval nobody planned¶

    The Reformation is the standard example, and it's a good one as long as it's told carefully.

    Luther's arguments were not novel in the sense of being unprecedented — reformist critiques had circulated before and been contained. What differed was distribution. Short, cheap, vernacular pamphlets could be printed quickly, in volume, and spread faster than institutional authorities could respond. An argument could now outrun the mechanisms built to suppress it.

    The important qualifier: printing did not cause the Reformation in any simple sense. The theological disputes, political rivalries, and grievances were real and pre-existing. What printing changed was the speed and scale at which a dissenting position could reach an audience and become a coordinated movement rather than a local grievance. Technology altered the dynamics of a conflict that had its own independent causes — which is usually how technology works, and almost never how it gets described.

    The consequences were not gentle. The century that followed included some of the most violent religious conflict in European history. Contemporaries experienced this period as genuinely destabilising, and they were right to.

    Both things at once¶

    This is the part usually skipped, and it's the most important part.

    The same technology that enabled the scientific revolution also enabled decades of religious warfare and a substantial expansion of witch-hunting. Printed manuals for identifying and prosecuting witches circulated widely and effectively — the same cheap-distribution mechanism that spread reformist theology and scientific observation also spread instructions for persecution, to a far larger audience than manuscript ever could have.

    Print enabled reliable accumulation of knowledge: a researcher could reference a specific edition and page and trust that another reader elsewhere held an identical copy, which manuscript copying — with its accumulated errors — could never guarantee. It enabled standardised vernacular languages, which shaped the formation of national identities. It enabled published astronomical tables, anatomical drawings, and reproducible descriptions of experiments.

    And it enabled pamphlet wars, forged documents, mass-produced denunciations, and rumour at speeds no prior society had faced.

    The technology did not have a moral valence. It lowered the cost of moving information, and both the best and worst things humans wanted to circulate moved more cheaply.

    Second-order effects arrive slowly and unrecognisably¶

    Perhaps the most useful pattern here is about timing.

    The immediate effect of cheap printing was fairly boring: existing texts became cheaper. The transformative effects — new intellectual communities, standardised languages, a scientific culture built on citation and reproducibility, new political dynamics — unfolded across generations, and mostly took forms nobody at the outset would have predicted.

    Nobody in 1470 wrote down "this will eventually enable a scientific method dependent on precise citation of identical editions." That effect required decades of accumulated practice before anyone could see it as a thing that had happened. Meanwhile, the effects contemporaries were worried about were often the wrong ones.

    This should induce real humility about predicting technological consequences. The people closest to a new technology are frequently wrong about its important effects, not because they're unintelligent, but because the important effects emerge from interaction with institutions and practices that haven't been invented yet.

    Drawing the parallel carefully¶

    The internet and now generative AI represent further collapses in the cost of producing and copying information, and the temptation to map the printing press directly onto them is strong. It's worth doing, but with discipline.

    What the historical pattern genuinely supports:

    Dropping the cost of information production has large effects that are slow, indirect, and mixed. Both the empowering and corrosive uses scale together, because the underlying capability is neutral. The most consequential changes come from shifts in who can produce and distribute, not from broader access to what already existed. And existing institutions — built around the previous cost structure — come under strain, sometimes productively and sometimes destructively.

    What it does not support: any specific prediction. The printing press analogy tells you to expect surprise, disruption, and mixed outcomes over decades. It does not tell you which outcomes. Anyone using the analogy to predict a particular future is using it as decoration for a conclusion they held already.

    There's also a real disanalogy worth flagging: printing lowered the cost of copying information that a human had produced. Generative systems lower the cost of producing it. Whether that's a difference of degree or of kind is genuinely unresolved, and it's the more interesting question.

    The thing worth carrying¶

    The lesson isn't optimism or pessimism. It's a specific correction to how technological change is usually discussed.

    When the cost of something collapses, the question worth asking is not "what will people do more of?" It's "what becomes economically viable that wasn't before, and who can now participate who couldn't?" The answers to those questions drive the second-order effects, and the second-order effects are where the history actually happens.

    The people living through the first century of print did not experience it as an information revolution. They experienced it as a period of unusual religious violence, political instability, and a bewildering amount of printed nonsense — alongside, largely unremarked at the time, the quiet construction of the intellectual infrastructure that made modern science possible.

    Both were the same event. It just took a few hundred years to see it that way.
