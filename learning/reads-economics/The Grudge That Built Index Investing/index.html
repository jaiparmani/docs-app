    The Grudge That Built Index Investing¶

    7 min read

    The most consequential idea in modern personal finance did not arrive as a triumphant insight. It arrived as the consolation prize of a man who had just been fired from the job he expected to hold for life.

    Jack Bogle joined Wellington Management as a young man and spent his twenties and thirties climbing it, eventually running the place. Wellington was a conservative shop built around a balanced fund — stocks and bonds, sober allocation, the kind of product that looked increasingly boring as the 1960s went on. That decade produced what the industry later called the go-go years: a bull market in which individual fund managers became famous, aggressive growth funds attracted enormous inflows, and a firm selling prudence looked like a firm selling nothing. Bogle, watching money walk out the door toward flashier competitors, did the thing that ambitious executives do when the market seems to have changed permanently. He bought the trend. Wellington merged with a Boston firm whose young managers ran one of the era's hot performance funds, and Bogle handed a meaningful share of the company to people whose entire edge was that the market had recently gone up. He spent the rest of his life describing that merger publicly as the worst mistake of his career.

    Then the market went down. The bear market of 1973 and 1974 was brutal and, for high-beta growth funds, close to annihilating. Performance collapsed, assets collapsed, and the partners Bogle had brought in now had enough combined voting power to remove him. In 1974 he was fired from the company he had run.

    The Loophole in His Own Firing¶

    What happened next depended on an obscure feature of how mutual funds are legally organized, and this is the part of the story that usually gets skipped in favor of the index fund.

    A mutual fund is not simply a product owned by an asset manager. It is its own legal entity — a corporation or trust with its own board of directors, which then hires a management company to run it. In practice this distinction is often theater; the fund's board is friendly to the manager and renews the contract every year without much drama. But it is a real distinction, and Bogle noticed that while Wellington Management had fired him, the boards of the Wellington funds were separate bodies where he still had standing.

    He went to those boards with a proposal: let the funds themselves establish a company, owned by the funds, to handle their own administration. The funds' shareholders would own that company. Since the shareholders already owned the funds, the entity would have no outside owners to enrich, which meant it had no reason to charge more than its own operating costs. It would run at cost, more or less permanently, by construction.

    They agreed, narrowly and in stages, and the entity began operating in 1975 under a name Bogle borrowed from one of Nelson's ships: Vanguard.

    This structure is the actual invention. A conventional asset manager has two sets of owners with interests pointing in opposite directions — the shareholders of the management company, who benefit from higher fees, and the investors in the funds, who benefit from lower ones. Vanguard collapsed those two groups into one. There was no longer anybody standing to profit from charging more.

    Bogle's Folly¶

    The index fund followed in 1976 and, at first, mostly as a way to do something. Vanguard had been permitted to administer funds but not to manage them, so it needed a fund that required no manager. A fund that simply bought the constituents of a broad market index in their market proportions and then did essentially nothing qualified. First Index Investment Trust — later renamed the Vanguard 500 Index Fund — went to market with the underwriters aiming to raise something like $150 million. It brought in roughly $11 million, a fraction so small that the original plan of actually replicating the index had to be scaled back.

    The reception in the industry ranged from amused to hostile. It got nicknamed Bogle's Folly. A poster circulated at the time framed the whole idea as un-American — why would anyone settle for average? The objection sounds ridiculous now, but it contains the misunderstanding that made the fund necessary.

    Why It Is Arithmetic and Not a Theory¶

    The strongest argument for indexing has nothing to do with whether markets are efficient, whether managers are smart, or whether prices reflect all available information. William Sharpe laid it out in a short piece called "The Arithmetic of Active Management," and the argument is close to a tautology, which is exactly its power.

    Divide every dollar invested in a market into two buckets: passive dollars, which hold every security in proportion to its market weight, and active dollars, which do anything else. The passive bucket, by construction, earns the market return before costs. But the two buckets together are the market. So the active bucket, taken as a whole, must also earn the market return before costs. Active management in aggregate cannot beat the market, because in aggregate it is the market.

    Now introduce costs. Active management costs more — research, salaries, trading, turnover. Subtract those and the average actively managed dollar must return less than the average passive dollar. Not usually. Not on historical evidence. Necessarily. Some active managers beat the index; they do it by taking the returns from other active managers, and the whole pool still loses the fee drag to the industry that runs it.

    The reason this matters so much more than it sounds is compounding. A fee is not a one-time toll; it is a permanent reduction in the growth rate. Take two portfolios compounding for forty years, one at 7 percent and one at 6 percent — a single percentage point of difference. The first multiplies by roughly fifteen. The second multiplies by roughly ten. The 1 percent did not cost 1 percent. It cost about a third of the final outcome, and it took the entire forty years to do it, silently, in a way no statement ever displays as a line item.

    It's worth being precise about what the argument establishes, because it gets stretched. It concerns the average dollar, and it holds by definition once "the market" means everything in it — definitions that do real work, since a published index is not the whole market. It says nothing about which investor ends up where, and nothing about the horizon, tax position, income stability, or tolerance for a bad decade that make one arrangement sensible for one household and wrong for the next. Those are individual questions, better taken to a qualified advisor than to an essay about a 1970s corporate structure. What the arithmetic does establish is narrower and more durable: costs are subtracted with certainty, while returns are not added with any.

    The Part That Was Harder to Copy¶

    The index fund itself turned out to be trivially copyable. Every large asset manager now sells index products, several at costs lower than Vanguard's, and the idea that a low-cost broad market fund is a reasonable default is now so mainstream it is nearly furniture.

    The ownership structure was not copied. No major competitor has replicated it, and the obstacle is structural rather than a failure of imagination — you cannot retroactively give away your own management company to your customers and expect your existing owners to applaud. And that structure is what made the index fund credible rather than a marketing position. Any firm can launch a cheap fund; sustaining low costs for decades requires that no one inside the building benefits from raising them. Bogle's real insight was not that markets are hard to beat. It was that the fee, not the strategy, was the variable investors could actually control, and that a company's incentives will eventually overwhelm its intentions unless the incentives are wired correctly from the start.

    Which is a strange legacy for a decision that began as a bad merger and a firing. The go-go years taught Bogle what chasing performance costs. Getting removed taught him what happens when ownership and interest diverge. He built a company that solved for both, and the industry has spent fifty years adopting one half of it.
