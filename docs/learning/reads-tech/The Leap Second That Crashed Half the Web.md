# The Leap Second That Crashed Half the Web

<small>6 min read</small>

At midnight UTC on June 30, 2012, the world's clocks did something they only do a couple dozen times a decade: they ticked from 23:59:59 to 23:59:60, an extra second inserted before rolling over to July 1. Within hours, a strange and disconnected-looking set of failures started showing up across the internet. Reddit went down. Mozilla's services degraded. Foursquare, Yelp, LinkedIn, and a number of other high-traffic sites reported outages or severe slowdowns. Qantas, the Australian airline, had its check-in and reservation systems fail badly enough to ground flights and force manual processing at airports. None of these companies shared infrastructure, and none of them had deployed anything unusual that day. What they had in common was Linux, and a kernel bug that a single extra second was enough to trigger.

## Why there's an extra second at all

Leap seconds exist because humanity ended up with two different, entirely reasonable ways of measuring time, and they don't agree with each other. Atomic time — the basis for International Atomic Time, TAI — is defined by counting oscillations of a cesium atom, and it is extremely uniform: every second is exactly the same length, forever, by definition. Solar time, the thing humans actually experience as "a day," is defined by the Earth's rotation, and the Earth is not a precision instrument. Tidal friction from the moon is gradually slowing its spin, and shorter-term effects — the redistribution of mass in the atmosphere and oceans, seismic activity, even the melting of ice sheets — nudge the rotation rate around in less predictable ways. The upshot is that a solar day is not reliably 86,400 atomic seconds long; it drifts.

UTC, the time standard nearly everything runs on, is a compromise: it ticks in atomic seconds but is kept within roughly a second of the Earth's actual rotational position, specifically the astronomical time scale UT1. When the drift approaches that threshold, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service schedules a leap second, almost always inserted at the end of June 30 or December 31 UTC. It is, structurally, the same idea as a leap year — periodically reconciling a clean, uniform counting system with the messy physical reality it's supposed to track — except a leap year adds a whole day on a schedule known centuries in advance, while a leap second adds one second on a schedule that isn't finalized more than about six months out, because Earth's rotation isn't predictable that far ahead. That irregularity is part of why leap seconds have caused so much operational pain: software gets almost no advance notice, and the event is rare enough that most systems never get meaningfully tested against it.

## The bug: a race between two things that should never have met

Inside the Linux kernel, there are two largely separate pieces of machinery relevant here. One is the hrtimer subsystem — high-resolution timers, the mechanism the kernel uses to wake up a process at a precise future moment. The other is futex, the "fast userspace mutex" primitive that a huge amount of concurrent software — including the JVM, which is why so many Java-based services were affected — uses under the hood for locking and thread synchronization, often with a timeout implemented via hrtimer.

When the kernel processes the leap second, it has to update its internal notion of the current time and, critically, re-evaluate any timers whose expiry was computed relative to that clock. A race condition in that update path meant that, for a timer a thread was waiting on via a timed futex call, the kernel could get into a state where it believed the timer had already expired and needed to be rearmed, rearmed it, immediately treated the rearmed timer as expired again, and repeated — a tight loop that never resolved, consuming an entire CPU core indefinitely without the process crashing, hanging in any way a monitoring system would obviously flag, or doing anything except spin. Because it was a race — a matter of precise timing between the clock update and a given timer's internal state — it didn't hit every machine identically. Two servers running the same kernel, the same software, side by side, could come out of the leap second in completely different states, which made the incident look bizarre and inconsistent while it was unfolding, rather than obviously a single root cause.

The fix on-call engineers discovered, before anyone had shipped a proper kernel patch, was almost comically indirect: forcibly touching the system clock — running `date` to reset it, or restarting the NTP daemon so it stepped the clock — kicked affected processes out of the loop, because it forced the kernel to redo the time-update bookkeeping cleanly instead of leaving it stuck in the inconsistent state the leap second had left behind. It wasn't a fix so much as a way to manually re-trigger the update and get lucky. A real kernel patch closing the race followed later. And this wasn't even the first time: a closely related hrtimer issue had already surfaced during the 2008 leap second, which should have been a warning that the code path was fragile — it just hadn't been fully exercised again until 2012 gave it another chance.

## The part that makes this more than trivia

The leap second bug is a clean illustration of a category of failure that has nothing to do with leap seconds specifically: bugs that live exclusively in code paths triggered by rare, monotonic assumptions being violated. Most software that touches time implicitly assumes the clock only moves forward, and moves forward smoothly. A leap second is one of the few sanctioned events where that assumption breaks — the clock briefly reports the same second twice, or from certain vantage points appears to move backward — and because it happens perhaps once or twice a year at most, it is exactly the kind of edge case that never shows up in normal testing, staging environments, or load tests, no matter how rigorous those are in every other respect.

The generalizable engineering lesson is to inventory the discontinuities your systems assume will never happen — clock changes, counter wraparound, daylight saving transitions, a leap year's February 29th, a timestamp rolling past a fixed-width integer's maximum value — and to test those transitions deliberately rather than trusting that "it's worked fine so far" means anything. Fine so far usually just means the discontinuity hasn't occurred yet. It's also worth knowing that the pain leap seconds caused across the industry, repeatedly, is a large part of why the world eventually agreed, in 2022, to stop inserting them altogether by 2035 — and why, in the meantime, companies like Google adopted a "leap smear," spreading the extra second gradually across an entire day instead of inserting it as a single sharp discontinuity, precisely to avoid handing every downstream system that one moment where its assumptions stop holding.
