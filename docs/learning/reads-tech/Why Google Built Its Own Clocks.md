# Why Google Built Its Own Clocks

<small>6 min read</small>

There is a particular kind of problem that shows up in every distributed system eventually, and it is deceptively simple to state: two things happened, on two different machines, and you would like to know which happened first. Not approximately. Not usually. Actually first, in a way you can build correctness guarantees on top of.

The obvious answer is to look at the clocks. Machine A says the write landed at 10:00:00.000, machine B says its write landed at 10:00:00.005, therefore A came first. Except that this is not sound reasoning, and the reason it is not sound is one of the more underappreciated facts about computers. **Clocks on different machines disagree, they disagree by amounts you cannot easily bound, and the disagreement changes over time.** Quartz oscillators drift with temperature and age. NTP corrects them, but NTP measures time over a network whose latency is asymmetric and variable, so its corrections carry their own error. Worse, clocks can jump: an NTP step correction can move a machine's clock backwards, which means a naive system can generate a timestamp, then generate an "earlier" one a moment later. Virtualisation compounds this, because a VM can be descheduled for an unbounded interval and wake up with a stale idea of the present. The wall clock is not a shared truth. It is a per-machine opinion that happens to be roughly correlated with everyone else's.

## The Discipline of Never Trusting a Timestamp

Distributed systems research absorbed this lesson early and drew a firm conclusion: stop depending on physical time. Lamport's logical clocks and their descendants, vector clocks, capture causality without reference to seconds at all — if A caused B, then A's counter is lower, and that is all you need for the orderings that actually matter. Consensus protocols like Paxos and Raft go further, ordering operations by putting them through an agreed-upon log rather than by asking anyone what time it is. Both share the same good instinct: if a physical quantity is unreliable, design so that correctness never touches it.

The trouble is that this instinct has a cost, and the cost shows up precisely when you want global consistency at global scale. Logical clocks tell you about causality between operations that are causally related; they say nothing useful about two transactions on opposite sides of the planet that never communicated. If you want a database where a transaction committed in Europe is guaranteed visible to a subsequent read in Asia — external consistency, the property where the system behaves as if all transactions happened one at a time in real-time order — you need something that relates independent, non-communicating operations to each other. Traditionally you get that by routing everything through a single coordinating point, which is exactly the bottleneck that made globally consistent databases slow enough that a generation of engineers concluded they were impossible.

## Buying the Constraint Down

Spanner, Google's globally distributed database, made a move here that reads as slightly absurd the first time you encounter it, and then obviously correct the second time. Instead of avoiding physical time, Google decided to *invest in it*. The company deployed GPS receivers and atomic clocks across its data centres, ran a set of time master machines against them, and had every server discipline its local clock against multiple masters. GPS gives an independent, satellite-sourced reference; atomic clocks catch the failure mode where GPS is unavailable or wrong, since a good local oscillator drifts slowly and predictably. Two time sources with uncorrelated failure modes, deployed as physical infrastructure, hedging against the possibility that the fleet's sense of time quietly diverges.

But the hardware is not the interesting part. The interesting part is the API. TrueTime, the interface Spanner exposes, does not return a timestamp. It returns an *interval* — an earliest and a latest — with the guarantee that the true time lies somewhere inside it. The uncertainty is not hidden behind a plausible-looking number the way `System.currentTimeMillis()` hides it. It is the return value. A caller cannot accidentally forget that clocks are imprecise, because the type system will not let them pretend otherwise. **The engineering achievement is not that Google made clocks accurate; it is that Google made clock error explicit and bounded.** With the atomic-and-GPS infrastructure in place, that bound is small — single-digit milliseconds under normal conditions — but the smallness matters less than the boundedness.

## Sitting Still to Go Faster

And then comes the trick that makes the whole thing pay off. If a transaction picks a commit timestamp and then simply *waits* until the uncertainty interval around that timestamp has definitely elapsed before it releases its locks and becomes visible, the system can guarantee that any transaction starting afterwards will get a strictly later timestamp. Commit wait. The transaction deliberately sits still, doing nothing, for the duration of its own ignorance, and in exchange the timestamps it produces are trustworthy as a global ordering. Reads can then be served at a timestamp from any sufficiently up-to-date replica without coordination, because the timestamps mean something real.

It is worth sitting with why this is such a striking design. Clock skew, in the traditional framing, is a correctness hazard — an unbounded, unpredictable thing that can silently produce wrong answers, and the only defence is to never rely on it. Spanner converts it into a latency cost. The uncertainty does not go away; it gets *paid for*, in milliseconds, on the commit path, in a quantity you can measure on a dashboard. And a latency cost is a fundamentally different kind of problem than a correctness hazard. You can budget against it. You can reduce it by buying better clocks and tightening the bound, and watch commit latency fall in response. You can reason about it in a capacity plan. Correctness problems do not have dials; latency problems do. Turning one into the other is one of the highest-leverage moves available in systems design, and it recurs far outside databases — it is the same shape as trading throughput for backpressure, or paying a retry cost to eliminate a duplicate-delivery bug.

## Which Walls Are Load-Bearing

The lesson people usually take from Spanner is the technical one about commit wait. The more useful lesson is about the shape of the decision. Everyone else treated imprecise clocks as a law of nature and designed elaborate workarounds. Google looked at the same constraint and asked what it would cost to move it — and the answer, in GPS antennas and rubidium oscillators and the operational burden of running a time infrastructure, turned out to be affordable when amortised over a fleet that size. That is the uncomfortable part. This option was not available to Google because its engineers were cleverer. It was available because the fixed cost of narrowing a physical constraint divided by an enormous denominator. Most of the architectural choices we admire from hyperscalers are like this: not insights the rest of us failed to have, but purchases the rest of us could not have justified. Knowing which of your constraints are genuinely physical and which are merely expensive is worth more than any particular technique, because it tells you which walls are worth pushing on — and, more often, which ones are load-bearing for you no matter how much cleverness you throw at them.
