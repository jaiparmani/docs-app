# Breaking Things on Purpose

Somewhere in the middle of Netflix's migration from its own data centres to AWS, the engineering organisation arrived at an uncomfortable realisation. They had built a system spread across thousands of instances, in which any individual machine could vanish without warning — because in a cloud environment, machines really do vanish without warning, and no amount of complaining to a provider changes that. Their architecture was designed to tolerate this. The design documents said so. And nobody actually knew whether it was true, because the only evidence would come from an event nobody had scheduled, at a time nobody had chosen, in a configuration nobody had inspected recently.

That gap — between a system that is designed to be resilient and a system that is *known* to be resilient — is the entire subject of chaos engineering. The response Netflix built, Chaos Monkey, is famous enough now to be a punchline: a service that wanders through production and terminates instances at random. It sounds like vandalism dressed up as methodology. The detail that turns it from vandalism into engineering is the scheduling. **Chaos Monkey ran during business hours, on weekdays, when engineers were at their desks, caffeinated, and available.**

## Choosing the Hour of Your Own Disaster

Think about what that choice actually does. Instances were going to die regardless; that was never in question. The only variable under Netflix's control was *when* they would find out what happens next. Left alone, that discovery arrives on its own schedule — which, by the grim arithmetic of a system that runs continuously and engineers who do not, means a meaningful share of first encounters happen at three in the morning, to a sleep-deprived on-call engineer holding a pager and no context. By deliberately inducing the failure at eleven on a Tuesday, the same information gets extracted under wildly better conditions: the people who wrote the code are present, the people who understand the dependency graph are reachable, and nobody is making architectural inferences while half-asleep. The failure was never optional. Its timing was, and Netflix took the timing.

The second thing the approach does is change the statistical character of failure. In an untested system, a given failure mode is rare, and because it is rare, each occurrence is a genuine crisis: unfamiliar, high-stakes, poorly understood, resolved by improvisation. Rarity sounds like a good property until you notice it is precisely what makes the events catastrophic. Continuous injection inverts this. Instance death stops being an incident and becomes ambient background — something the system absorbs dozens of times a week without anyone noticing, that engineers have watched often enough to have real intuitions about. **Chaos engineering trades rare, large, mysterious failures for frequent, small, boring ones**, and boring failures are the ones you survive.

## Resilience Has an Expiry Date

There is a third effect that is easy to miss and probably the most important organisationally. Resilience claims in a design document have no expiry date and no enforcement mechanism. A service that genuinely handled node loss in March can quietly stop handling it in September, because someone added a cache that assumes affinity, or a new dependency without a timeout, or a startup path that takes ninety seconds and blows the health check budget during a mass replacement. Nothing in the ordinary development process catches this. Continuous failure injection catches it in the same way tests catch regressions: the assertion is executed, repeatedly, against the system as it currently exists rather than as it was once described. Resilience stops being a property you claim at design review and becomes a property you maintain.

## Slow Is Worse Than Dead

Netflix escalated the idea steadily. Latency injection — slowing a dependency's responses rather than removing it — turns out to expose more bugs than outright termination, because a dead dependency trips circuit breakers and fails fast, while a slow one quietly exhausts thread pools and connection limits and propagates backpressure into callers that never anticipated it. Partial degradation is genuinely harder than clean failure, and only deliberate injection surfaces it. From there the scope grew: injecting failures into specific dependencies to verify that fallback paths were real rather than theoretical, and eventually simulating the loss of an entire AWS region, evacuating traffic to the survivors. Region-level exercises are the most expensive and the most valuable, because regional failover is the classic capability that exists on an architecture diagram, has never been executed end to end, and is discovered to be broken at exactly the moment it is needed. Running it deliberately, repeatedly, on a Tuesday, is the only way to know.

## The Preconditions Nobody Quotes

All of which comes with a large and non-negotiable set of preconditions, and it is worth being blunt about them, because "we do chaos engineering" has become a thing organisations say when what they mean is that their system is unreliable and they have decided to be philosophical about it. You need monitoring good enough to distinguish "the experiment did nothing" from "the experiment broke something we cannot see yet" — without that you are not running an experiment, only adding noise. You need a bounded blast radius: a way to limit exposure to a small fraction of traffic or users, so a bad outcome costs you an inconvenience rather than a headline. You need an abort switch that works instantly and that everyone trusts, which in practice means it must have been tested too. You need a hypothesis stated in advance, because an experiment without a prediction produces anecdotes rather than knowledge. And you need genuine organisational buy-in, from the people who own the customer relationship as much as the people who own the servers, because the first time an experiment causes real user impact — and it will — whether the practice survives is decided by whether leadership agreed beforehand.

Absent those conditions, breaking production on purpose is simply breaking production. There is no methodology in it. And the honest extension of that point is that plenty of systems should never be subjected to this. Chaos engineering assumes a system that is already redundant, already instrumented, already expected to survive component loss, and whose failures are recoverable and non-destructive. If your service has a single database with no tested restore path, injecting failure teaches you nothing you did not already know and costs you data. If your system controls something physical, or handles irreversible financial movements, or has regulatory obligations around availability, the calculus is entirely different. The technique earns its keep in large, redundant, horizontally scaled systems where failure is statistically continuous. It is malpractice in a system where failure is rare because the stakes are enormous.

## Believing Versus Knowing

What lingers, past the tooling and the monkey metaphors, is an epistemological point that applies well beyond infrastructure. There is a real and often invisible difference between believing your system is resilient and knowing it. Belief is assembled from design intent, from code review, from the comforting fact that nothing has gone wrong lately. Knowledge comes only from having watched the thing fail and observed what happened next. Every system carries some set of failure behaviours that are already determined — they are in the code right now, fixed, waiting. Your only real choice is whether you find out what they are on a Tuesday morning of your choosing, or on some future night of theirs.
