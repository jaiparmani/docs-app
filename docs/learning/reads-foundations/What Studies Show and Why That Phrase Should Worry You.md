# What "Studies Show" and Why That Phrase Should Worry You

<small>6 min read</small>

Sometime around the early 2010s, psychology had a bad decade. Researchers began systematically re-running well-known experiments — famous ones, textbook ones, results that had been cited thousands of times and taught to undergraduates as established fact — and discovered that a substantial fraction of them did not come out the same way twice.

This became known as the replication crisis, and it was not confined to psychology. Similar concerns surfaced in medicine, economics, cancer biology, and elsewhere. It is genuinely one of the more important things to understand about how knowledge gets made, and — this matters — it is not an argument that science is broken.

## What a p-value actually means

Almost every empirical claim you encounter rests on a number called a p-value, and almost everyone, including many people who publish papers, holds a wrong belief about what it means.

Here is what it is not: the probability that the hypothesis is false. It is not the chance the result happened by luck. It is not a measure of how important or large the effect is.

Here is what it is: **if there were genuinely no effect at all, how surprising would data this extreme be?** A p-value of 0.05 says that if nothing real is going on, you'd see a result this striking about 1 time in 20 by chance alone.

That's a useful thing to know. But sit with the implication. If nothing real is going on, one in twenty studies will clear that bar anyway. Run twenty analyses on random noise and, on average, one comes back "statistically significant." Nothing has been discovered. The threshold simply did its job at exactly the rate it was designed to.

The convention of treating 0.05 as the line between "finding" and "nothing" is arbitrary. It was a rough rule of thumb that hardened, over decades, into a publication gate — and once a number becomes a gate, people start optimising for it.

## Cheating without cheating

The word "p-hacking" makes it sound like fraud. Occasionally it is. Far more often it's something subtler and much harder to police, because the people doing it are behaving reasonably at every individual step.

Imagine you've collected data on whether a new teaching method improves student performance. The headline result isn't significant. So — quite naturally, as a curious researcher — you look closer. Does it work for the younger students? For the ones who started behind? Does it show up in maths but not reading? What if you exclude the three students who missed half the sessions? What if you measure improvement as a percentage rather than an absolute score?

Each of those is a defensible analytical choice. But you have now run a dozen tests, and one of them comes back at p = 0.04. You publish that one, describing it accurately and honestly. You have not lied about anything. And yet the result means far less than it appears to, because the reader has no idea that eleven other analyses were run and discarded.

This is sometimes called the garden of forking paths, and it's insidious precisely because no dishonesty is required. A researcher who makes every choice after seeing the data, each time in good faith, can generate significant-looking findings from pure noise without ever noticing they've done it.

## The findings nobody publishes

Layered on top of this is **publication bias**, which distorts the literature in a completely different way.

Journals have historically been far more interested in "we found something" than "we looked and found nothing." Researchers know this. So null results — the study that tested a plausible idea and found no effect — disproportionately never get written up, or get rejected if they are.

Now consider what this does to the visible body of research. If twenty labs independently test a drug that doesn't work, and by chance one gets a significant result, the nineteen null findings sit in desk drawers while the one positive result gets published. A reader surveying the literature sees a single study showing the drug works and no studies showing it doesn't. The literature is not lying, exactly. It's just been filtered.

Combine publication bias with the garden of forking paths and you have an environment that reliably produces striking, significant, published findings that are not real — with no villain anywhere in the process.

## Why this is science working, not failing

It would be easy to read all of the above as an indictment. It isn't, and the reason matters.

The replication crisis was discovered *by scientists*, using scientific methods, and published openly. Nobody outside the field caught this and forced a reckoning. Researchers built large collaborations specifically to re-test their own discipline's canon, reported unflattering results, and then went about changing how the work is done.

The reforms that followed are real and are the thing to actually take away. **Pre-registration** — publicly committing to your hypothesis and analysis plan *before* collecting data — closes the forking-paths problem almost completely, because you can no longer choose your analysis after seeing what would look best. Registered reports go further: journals agree to publish based on the study design, before results exist, which removes the incentive to produce exciting outcomes. Journals dedicated to publishing null results have appeared. Data sharing has become far more standard.

A field that finds a systemic flaw in its own methods and rebuilds them is not a field that has failed. It's the only kind of field that can be trusted at all — self-correction is the entire distinguishing feature.

## Reading a study without a statistics degree

You don't need technical training to be substantially better at this than average. A handful of questions do most of the work.

**Has it replicated?** This is by far the most important one and it requires no expertise. A single study is a hypothesis with some evidence attached. A finding reproduced by independent groups is knowledge. Most media coverage collapses this distinction entirely.

**How many people were in it?** Small samples produce wildly variable results. A striking effect in 20 participants should move you very little; the same effect in 20,000 is a different creature.

**How big is the effect, not just how significant?** Statistical significance says "probably not zero." It says nothing about "large enough to matter." With a big enough sample, trivially small effects become significant. A drug that reliably extends life by four hours is significant and useless.

**Was the analysis planned in advance?** Pre-registered studies are meaningfully more trustworthy than exploratory ones. Exploratory work is valuable — it generates hypotheses — but it should be labelled as generating questions rather than answering them.

**Who is making the claim, and does the headline match the paper?** An enormous amount of distortion happens between the paper's careful conclusion and the article's headline, often introduced by a press office rather than by any researcher.

## The habit worth building

The realistic response to all of this isn't to distrust research. It's to hold beliefs with a calibration that matches the evidence.

A single striking study should move you a little. A finding replicated across independent labs, with large samples and pre-registered analysis, should move you a lot. Most of what you encounter is the former, presented with the confidence of the latter.

The phrase "studies show" is doing a great deal of concealed work. It flattens the distance between a preliminary result from forty undergraduates and something the field has hammered on for twenty years. Learning to ask **which kind of study, and has anyone checked?** is a small habit that quietly upgrades every claim you'll ever read.
