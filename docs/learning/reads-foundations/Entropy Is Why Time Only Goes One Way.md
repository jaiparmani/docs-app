# Entropy Is Why Time Only Goes One Way

<small>6 min read</small>

Show someone a video of a glass shattering on a floor and ask whether it's playing forwards or backwards. They'll answer instantly and correctly, every time, without thinking.

Now ask *why* they know. The obvious answer — that shards don't spontaneously assemble into glasses — is true but doesn't explain anything. The genuinely strange fact is this: **nearly every fundamental law of physics works identically forwards and backwards in time.** Run the equations governing every atom in that shattering glass in reverse and nothing is violated. The reassembly is not forbidden by any law of motion. And yet you have never seen it, and never will.

The resolution is entropy, and it's one of the deepest ideas in science.

## Entropy is not disorder

The standard shorthand — entropy is disorder — is misleading enough that it's worth discarding entirely. "Disorder" is aesthetic and subjective. Entropy is a count.

Consider a deck of cards. There is exactly **one** arrangement we'd call "sorted by suit and rank." There are roughly 8 × 10⁶⁷ arrangements total. The number we'd call "shuffled" is essentially all of them.

Now shuffle the deck. Why does it come out disordered? Not because shuffling has a preference for mess. Because when you're picking effectively at random among 10⁶⁷ possibilities and only one of them counts as sorted, you are overwhelmingly likely to land on one of the other 10⁶⁷ minus one.

That's entropy. It measures **how many microscopic arrangements produce the same macroscopic description.** A "sorted deck" corresponds to one arrangement — low entropy. A "shuffled deck" corresponds to almost all arrangements — high entropy. Systems drift toward high-entropy states not because something pushes them there, but because high-entropy states are so vastly more numerous that random wandering lands in them practically always.

Apply this to the glass. "Intact glass" describes an extraordinarily small set of atomic configurations. "Shards scattered across a floor" describes an unimaginably larger set. Both are permitted by the laws of physics. One is overwhelmingly more probable, and "overwhelmingly" here involves numbers that make 10⁶⁷ look small.

## Statistical, not mechanical

This is the part worth sitting with, because it changes what kind of law the second law of thermodynamics actually is.

Most physical laws are prohibitions. Energy cannot be created. Nothing exceeds light speed. The second law of thermodynamics — entropy in a closed system tends to increase — is different in character. It is not a prohibition. It's a statement about overwhelming probability.

Entropy *could* spontaneously decrease. The air molecules in your room could all drift into one corner, leaving you in vacuum. Nothing forbids it. It's just that the number of arrangements where air is evenly distributed exceeds the number where it's all in a corner by a factor so enormous that you could wait many times the age of the universe without seeing it.

So the second law is a probabilistic law masquerading as an absolute one, and it holds absolutely for all practical purposes only because the numbers involved are so extreme. This is a rare and slightly unsettling thing in physics.

## Life does not break the rule

The common objection: living things build order out of chaos constantly. A seed becomes a tree. A cell divides into an organism. Doesn't that reduce entropy?

Locally, yes. And the second law only applies to closed systems.

You are not closed. You are a system running a continuous flow of energy through yourself — consuming food, breathing oxygen, and radiating heat into your surroundings. Maintaining your body's low-entropy organisation requires dumping *more* entropy into the environment than you remove from yourself. The books balance, with a surplus, every time.

A refrigerator is the honest version of this: it makes its interior colder and more ordered, and to do so it pumps heat out the back and consumes electricity, producing more total entropy than it removed. Stand behind a fridge and you can feel the second law being obeyed.

Life, viewed thermodynamically, is a very sophisticated process for degrading energy — taking concentrated energy from the sun or food, using some of the flow to maintain internal structure, and releasing the rest as diffuse heat. The order is real and locally paid for.

## The same mathematics, in a different field

In 1948, working on communication over noisy channels, Claude Shannon needed a measure of how much information a message carries. The formula he arrived at has essentially the same mathematical form as the physicists' entropy.

This is not a coincidence or a cute analogy. Both quantities are measuring the same underlying thing: **how many possibilities are consistent with what you know.**

A message that could equally be any of a million things carries a lot of information when it arrives, because it eliminates 999,999 alternatives. A message you could have predicted with certainty carries none — you learned nothing. High information entropy means many possible messages; high thermodynamic entropy means many possible microscopic states. Same structure, different subject matter.

This link runs deeper than the formula. There's a real, physical cost to erasing information — irreversibly destroying a bit necessarily produces heat, at a minimum quantity set by thermodynamics. Information is not abstract. It is a physical thing with thermodynamic consequences, which is one reason computation generates heat and always will.

## The question nobody has answered

Here's where it gets genuinely strange, and it's the reason this topic sits among the deepest open problems in physics.

Entropy increases because systems move from less probable to more probable states. Fine. But that logic runs both directions in time — apply the same statistical reasoning *backwards* and it predicts entropy was higher in the past too, which is flatly wrong. Yesterday had lower entropy than today. So did last year. So did the year before.

Follow that chain all the way back and you reach an inescapable conclusion: **the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state.** Astonishingly, improbably low. And the entire forward direction of time — everything you experience as past-versus-future, the whole reason effects follow causes and glasses shatter rather than assemble — is downstream of that single initial condition.

Why the universe started that way is not known. It's not explained by any law we have; it's an initial condition those laws take as given. Some of the most serious work in cosmology circles this question.

Which means the answer to why you can tell that video is playing forwards is not really about glass, or floors, or physics as we usually mean it. It's that you are embedded in a universe that started in a fantastically improbable configuration and has been relaxing toward the ordinary ever since — and your sense of time passing is you, riding that slope downhill.
