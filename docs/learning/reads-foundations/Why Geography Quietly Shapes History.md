# Why Geography Quietly Shapes History

<small>5 min read</small>

For most of human history, moving a heavy object overland was brutally expensive. Roads were bad or nonexistent, animals needed feeding, and the amount a cart could carry was limited by what the axle could bear and the animal could pull. Moving the same object by water was, by comparison, almost free — a boat carries an enormous load, the water bears the weight, and the current or wind does much of the work.

That single asymmetry, applied over thousands of years, did more to determine where cities exist, which regions grew rich, and which political units held together than almost any deliberate human decision. It's the clearest example of a broader point: **geography doesn't determine history, but it relentlessly shapes the odds.**

## Water was the highway

Look at where major old cities are and the pattern is almost monotonous. River mouths, natural harbours, confluences, fords, the head of navigation where a river stops being passable to seagoing ships. These are not coincidences of taste. They're the places where the cost of moving goods dropped sharply, and commerce accumulated where transport was cheap.

Regions with navigable rivers running through their interior — connecting inland production to coastal ports — had a durable structural advantage in developing internal markets. Goods could move, which meant regions could specialise, which meant they could trade with each other rather than each producing everything locally. Regions where rivers were unnavigable, seasonal, or ran the wrong direction faced a permanent tax on internal commerce that no amount of local effort could remove.

The modern residue of this is landlocked countries, which as a group face persistent structural disadvantages in trade. Every import and export must cross someone else's territory, subject to their infrastructure, their tariffs, and their politics. This is not destiny — some landlocked countries are wealthy — but it is a real, measurable headwind that has to be overcome rather than something that simply doesn't matter.

## Barriers, and what they do to states

Mountains and deserts do something specific to political organisation: they make conquest expensive and administration difficult.

Regions carved up by serious mountain ranges have historically tended toward political fragmentation, because projecting force across a mountain range is costly and holding territory on the far side is costlier. Large open plains have historically tended toward either large unified states or repeated conquest, because there's nothing to stop an army moving.

Neither pattern is absolute — there are fragmented plains and unified mountains — but the tendency is real, and it compounds. A region that stayed fragmented for centuries developed different institutions, different competitive dynamics between rival states, and different relationships between rulers and the ruled than a region unified early under a single administration. Those institutional differences then persisted long after the military relevance of the mountains faded.

## The axis argument

One of the more provocative geographic claims concerns the orientation of continents, popularised by Jared Diamond.

The argument runs: crops and domesticated animals are adapted to particular climates, day lengths, and disease environments. Move them east or west along a similar latitude and they often transplant successfully. Move them north or south across climate zones and they frequently don't. Eurasia's principal axis runs east-west, spanning a broad band of similar latitude, while the Americas and Africa run predominantly north-south, crossing tropics, deserts, and temperate zones.

The claimed consequence is that agricultural packages — the bundle of crops and animals that let dense settled populations exist — spread far more readily across Eurasia than across the other continents, giving Eurasian societies a head start in accumulating the population density from which specialisation, writing, and technology follow.

It's an elegant argument and it has genuine explanatory force. It also has serious critics, and the criticism is worth taking seriously rather than treating the thesis as settled.

## The case against reading too much into it

The strongest objection to geographic explanation is that it can quietly erase human agency and read suspiciously cleanly backwards from known outcomes.

The most damaging evidence is empirical: pairs of regions with nearly identical geography and radically divergent outcomes. Neighbouring territories sharing climate, terrain, resources, and often ethnicity and history, separated by a political border, ending up with vastly different levels of prosperity. Geography cannot explain a difference that appears on one side of a line drawn by people. **Institutions** — property rights, rule of law, the structure of political power, whether elites can expropriate freely — do explain it, and there is a substantial body of economic history arguing that institutional differences dominate geographic ones in accounting for modern inequality between countries.

There's also a methodological worry. Geographic explanation is very good at generating a plausible story for any outcome after the fact. Once you know a region developed slowly, it's rarely difficult to find a geographic feature that could account for it. That flexibility is a warning sign — a framework that can explain any outcome is doing less work than it appears to.

And there's a moral hazard in the framing. Geographic determinism can slide, sometimes unintentionally, into treating global inequality as natural and inevitable rather than as substantially produced by conquest, extraction, and political choices that specific people made.

## Where this actually lands

The defensible position is neither "geography explains history" nor "geography is irrelevant." It's more specific and more useful than either.

**Geography sets constraints and shifts probabilities. Institutions and choices determine what happens within them.**

A landlocked country faces a real trade disadvantage. It can be overcome — through regional agreements, through specialising in goods where transport cost matters less, through building infrastructure — but overcoming it requires deliberate effort that a coastal neighbour never had to make. Geography made one path harder, not impossible.

The genuinely interesting analytical work is in the interaction. Why did some societies facing hard geographic constraints build institutions that overcame them while others didn't? Why did some regions with enormous natural advantages squander them — the resource-curse pattern, where mineral wealth funds extractive politics and undermines the broader economy, is a case where good geography produces bad outcomes through institutional channels?

Those questions are more interesting than either determinism or its denial, and they're the ones that geographic literacy actually helps you ask. The map isn't the explanation. But you can't read the explanation without it.
