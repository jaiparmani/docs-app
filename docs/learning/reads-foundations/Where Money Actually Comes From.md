# Where Money Actually Comes From

<small>6 min read</small>

Most educated adults carry a mental model of banking that goes roughly like this: savers deposit money at a bank, the bank keeps a fraction in reserve, and lends the rest out to borrowers. Money moves from the people who have it to the people who need it, and the bank takes a cut for arranging the introduction.

This picture is intuitive, widely taught, and substantially wrong about the direction of causation. The Bank of England published a widely-cited explainer in 2014 making this point directly, and it remains one of the more useful things a non-economist can read, precisely because the correct version is so counterintuitive.

## The loan creates the deposit

When a commercial bank makes a loan, it does not go and fetch someone else's savings. It credits the borrower's account with a number.

That's it. That number is new money. Before the loan agreement was signed, it did not exist anywhere in the economy; afterwards, it sits in the borrower's account, spendable like any other money. The bank has simultaneously created an asset for itself (your obligation to repay) and a liability (the deposit in your account). Both came into existence in the same instant, from nothing, as a matter of accounting.

This is not a scandal or a loophole. It is how the system is designed to work, and it's how the overwhelming majority of the money in a modern economy — the money in your current account, the money you get paid in — came to exist. **Most money is created by commercial banks making loans.** Physical cash printed by a central bank is a small fraction of the total.

The intuitive story has the causation backwards. Deposits don't fund loans; loans create deposits. When you take out a mortgage, the bank isn't handing you the savings of a careful pensioner. It's typing a number into your account and writing a corresponding entry on its own books.

## Then why can't they create infinite money?

This is the obvious next question and it has real answers, several of them.

The most binding constraint is simply **demand for loans from creditworthy borrowers**. A bank creating money by lending is also creating an asset that must be repaid. Lend to people who don't pay you back and you don't get rich — you go bankrupt, which is precisely what happened to a lot of banks in 2008. The lending decision is a credit-risk decision, and it caps expansion far more than anything else in ordinary times.

Then there are **capital requirements**. Banks are required by regulation to hold capital — genuine equity, funded by shareholders rather than depositors — in proportion to the risk-weighted assets on their books. Expanding the loan book means needing more capital behind it, and capital is expensive to raise. This is a hard, actively-enforced brake.

There is also the **central bank's policy interest rate**, which sets the price at which banks can obtain reserves and therefore shapes the whole cost structure of lending. Raise rates and fewer loans make commercial sense, so less money gets created. This is the main lever central banks pull when they want to slow things down.

And banks must be able to settle with each other. When your loan gets spent and the money lands at a different bank, your bank must transfer central bank reserves to settle. It needs access to those reserves, at a price.

So banks create money, but inside a cage — built from credit risk, capital rules, interest rates, and settlement obligations.

## Two completely different kinds of money

A great deal of public confusion dissolves once you separate two things that are both called "money" and are not interchangeable.

**Broad money** is what households and businesses hold and spend: deposits in bank accounts, plus cash. This is the money that buys groceries. It's created mainly by commercial bank lending, as above.

**Central bank reserves** are a different thing entirely — deposits that commercial banks hold at the central bank, used to settle payments between banks. Ordinary people and companies cannot hold or spend reserves. They exist in a closed circuit among banks and the central bank.

This distinction is the reason so much commentary about "money printing" misfires. When a central bank conducts quantitative easing, it creates reserves and uses them to buy assets — typically government bonds — from financial institutions. That does create new central bank money. But it primarily swaps one asset (a bond) for another (reserves) in the financial system's hands. It is not the same operation as depositing new spendable money into everyone's current account, and treating the two as identical produces predictions that have repeatedly failed. The years following the 2008 financial crisis saw enormous QE programmes accompanied by persistently *low* inflation in most advanced economies — a fact that a naive "printing money always means inflation" model cannot accommodate.

That is emphatically not the same as saying QE has no effects or no risks. It's saying the mechanism is more specific than the shorthand, and the shorthand generates bad reasoning.

## Where the genuine disagreement lives

It's worth being honest about the boundary between what's settled and what isn't, because they get conflated constantly.

The mechanics above — that commercial bank lending creates deposits, that reserves and broad money are distinct, that QE operates on the asset side of the financial system — are essentially a matter of describing how the plumbing works. There isn't much dispute.

What economists argue about, sometimes fiercely, is what follows from it: how much of inflation is driven by money creation versus supply constraints versus expectations; how much room governments that issue their own currency really have to run deficits, and what the limits actually bind on; whether QE was effective, ineffective, or actively harmful in its distributional effects; how much the mechanics of money creation should change policy at all. These are live, contested questions where reasonable specialists disagree, and anyone presenting them as settled — in either direction — is selling something.

Knowing the mechanics doesn't hand you a policy position. It just stops you from holding one for a reason that turns out to be factually mistaken.

## What changes once you know this

The practical payoff is that a large amount of confident commentary becomes visibly confused.

When someone says a government "has run out of money" in the way a household does, you can notice that a government issuing debt in its own currency faces genuine constraints — inflation, exchange rates, the willingness of buyers to hold its debt at a given price — but that "the pot is empty" is not among them, and that the real constraints are more interesting than the metaphor.

When you read about a central bank raising rates to fight inflation, you can see the actual mechanism rather than a vague gesture: raising the price of credit reduces the volume of new lending, which reduces the rate at which new broad money enters the economy, which cools demand.

And when someone explains a financial crisis as banks having "lent out too much of the deposits," you'll know the story is describing something that doesn't happen — and that the real story, about credit quality and capital adequacy and what happens when a great many loans that created money simultaneously stop being repaid, is both more accurate and considerably more alarming.
