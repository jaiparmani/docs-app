# 07 — The Will to Meaning

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
In the book's second half, Frankl sets out the theoretical break at the heart of logotherapy: where Freud located the primary human drive in a will to pleasure, and Adler located it in a will to power, Frankl proposes a will to meaning as the more fundamental motivating force. On this view, pleasure and power aren't the deepest thing people are after — they become substitute goals when the will to meaning is frustrated, a consolation prize pursued once the search for meaning itself has stalled.

## Why it matters
This reframes both what drives human behavior and what psychological distress often actually is. If Freud's model were the whole picture, people under extreme deprivation should regress fully toward primitive drives for pleasure or basic gratification — and Frankl acknowledges that some prisoners did. But he uses the counter-observation that many others, under identical physical deprivation, remained preoccupied not with pleasure or power but with a different question entirely: whether there was any meaning in what they were enduring. That preoccupation, present even at the edge of survival, is the evidence Frankl leans on for treating meaning as the more basic drive.

## Example from the book
Frankl draws on his post-war clinical practice as further evidence for the theory: patients who came to him complaining of a persistent inner emptiness — a condition he would later name the existential vacuum — were frequently not lacking in pleasure or achievement by any conventional measure. They had comfortable lives, functioning relationships, professional success, and still reported feeling that nothing meant anything. Their difficulty, in his diagnosis, was not a deficit of gratification but a deficit of meaning, which no increase in pleasure or accomplishment resolved — direct clinical support, in his view, for treating the will to meaning as a distinct drive rather than a disguised form of the will to pleasure.

## Practical application
When trying to diagnose your own or someone else's persistent malaise, resist the reflex to reach first for more pleasure or comfort, or more achievement and control, as the fix. Ask instead what would actually satisfy a will to meaning in that specific life — what deed, relationship, or stance is missing — since Frankl's account suggests that pleasure and power applied to a meaning problem tend not to resolve it, however much of either is added.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think about a moment you had everything you thought you wanted — comfort, status, achievement — and still felt empty. What might that emptiness have actually been asking for?
