# 05 — Love as the Ultimate Goal

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
In one of the memoir's most personal passages, Frankl arrives at what he calls the central truth of his experience: that love is the highest goal to which a person can aspire — not derived from anything else, not a means to some other end, but the thing itself. He reaches this not as an abstract conclusion but in the middle of physical suffering, marching in a labor column, when the image of his wife rose in his mind with a vividness that let him carry on a kind of inner conversation with her, without knowing at the time whether she was even still alive.

## Why it matters
What makes this observation more than a sentimental aside is the separation Frankl draws between love and the physical presence or even survival of the person loved. He realized, in that moment, that the meaning and sustenance he was drawing from his wife's presence in his mind did not depend on whether she was, at that moment, actually alive to receive it — "love goes very far beyond the physical person of the beloved," he writes, and finds its meaning in something closer to a spiritual relationship that persists independent of the outcome. This matters because it locates a source of meaning that isn't hostage to circumstances a person cannot control or even know.

## Example from the book
Frankl describes marching before dawn on a work detail, cold and exhausted, and conjuring his wife's face and voice in his mind so vividly that he spoke to her and, in his account, heard her answer. He did not know, at that time, whether she was alive or dead. He would later learn that she had died in the camp at Bergen-Belsen. But he reflects afterward that the meaning and strength he drew from her presence in his mind during that march was not diminished by that fact — the relationship he was sustaining himself with in that moment was real to him regardless of what was, unknown to him, already true.

## Practical application
During periods of separation, loss, or uncertainty about someone you love — physical distance, estrangement, illness, or death itself — treat the inner relationship (memory, imagined conversation, the internalized sense of how they'd respond to you) as a legitimate and sufficient source of strength in its own right, not merely a substitute for the real thing or a fantasy to be embarrassed about. Frankl's account suggests this isn't self-deception; it's a real form of connection that doesn't require the other person's current presence to do its work.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of someone whose remembered presence has gotten you through something hard, even in their physical absence. What did that inner relationship give you that circumstance couldn't take away?
