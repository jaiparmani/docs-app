# 03 — The Last of the Human Freedoms

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
At the philosophical center of Frankl's memoir is a claim about what cannot be taken from a person even when everything else has been: the freedom to choose one's own attitude toward the circumstances one is given. Prisoners in the camps had been stripped of possessions, names, physical dignity, and often any realistic hope of survival. What remained, Frankl insists, was a domain that guards and circumstance could not reach — the inner stance a person took toward what was happening to them. He calls this "the last of the human freedoms," and argues that every day, every hour, still presented prisoners with real decisions about who they would be inside conditions they had no power to change.

## Why it matters
This is the hinge on which the rest of the book's argument turns. If dignity depended entirely on circumstance — on being treated decently, on having enough food, on physical survival being reasonably assured — then the camps would have made dignity impossible for everyone inside them. Frankl's claim is narrower and, because of that, more durable: dignity depends on the stance taken toward circumstance, which remains available even when circumstance is at its worst. This matters well outside the extreme case of the camps, anywhere a person faces conditions they cannot change or escape.

## Example from the book
Frankl describes men who, in the middle of the camp's daily brutality, chose to comfort others despite having nothing to offer — walking through the huts to give a kind word, or giving away their own last piece of bread to someone worse off, at real cost to their own already-starved bodies. He is explicit that these were not many men, and that most did not behave this way — but their existence was proof enough for his argument: that even stripped of everything else, some prisoners still had a decision in front of them about how to act, and made it in a direction that had nothing to do with survival advantage. He writes that this was sufficient to prove "everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances."

## Practical application
In situations of genuine powerlessness — a diagnosis you can't change, a loss already final, treatment you can't undo — separate explicitly what is and isn't within your control. Rather than waiting for circumstances to improve before deciding who you'll be inside them, choose the stance deliberately, as its own act, independent of whether the circumstances ever improve. This isn't a call to manufacture positivity about things that are genuinely bad — it's a narrower claim that the choice of stance is real and remains yours even when nothing else is.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a situation you're currently powerless to change. What is one choice about your attitude toward it that is still entirely yours?
