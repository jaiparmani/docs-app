# 01 — Gender as a Training Program

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Adichie's central move is to reframe gender not as a natural fact but as something closer to a training program — a set of rules drilled into children from the moment they're old enough to be corrected, long before they're old enough to question them. She draws this almost entirely from her own childhood in Nigeria: specific classroom moments, specific remarks from adults, specific small corrections that, taken individually, look trivial, but taken together add up to a curriculum. The word "feminist" itself enters the essay as a memory — a childhood friend, Okoloma, called her one during an argument when she was fourteen, using it as an insult, the way he might have called her something worse. Neither of them fully knew what it meant. That gap — a serious word deployed as a casual slur, absorbed by both kids without examination — is the essay's opening proof that the training starts early and mostly goes unnoticed while it's happening.

## Why it matters
If gender roles were simply natural, they wouldn't need constant reinforcement — nobody has to train a child to grow taller. The fact that so much active correction is required (this is how a girl sits, this is what a boy doesn't cry about) is itself evidence that the rules are made, not given. This matters because it relocates the argument: the question stops being "are men and women different" and becomes "why did we build a training program this elaborate around it, and who decided what it should teach." Once gender is visible as a curriculum rather than a fact of nature, it becomes something that can, in principle, be taught differently.

## Example from the book
Adichie recounts a primary school class where the teacher announced that whoever scored highest on an upcoming test would be made class monitor — a small but real position of authority among the students. She studied hard and got the highest score. The teacher then said only a boy could be class monitor, a rule nobody had mentioned before the test existed to matter, and gave the position to a boy who had scored lower. The lesson she draws from it isn't just that it was unfair — it's how casually the rule appeared exactly when it was needed, as though it had been there all along.

## Practical application
Pick one instruction you remember receiving as a child that was framed as being about your gender specifically — something you were told to do, stop doing, or aspire to, "because you're a boy" or "because you're a girl." Try to trace it: who told you, how old you were, and whether anyone ever explained the reasoning or it just arrived as settled fact. Most people, doing this for the first time, are struck by how many of these rules they can recall precisely and how few of them were ever actually justified.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's a gender rule you were taught as a child that you've since quietly stopped following — and did anyone ever notice, or object, when you dropped it?
