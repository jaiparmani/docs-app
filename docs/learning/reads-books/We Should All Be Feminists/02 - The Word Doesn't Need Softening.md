# 02 — The Word Doesn't Need Softening

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A recurring pressure Adichie describes is the suggestion that she should drop the word "feminist" in favor of something gentler and less specific — "humanist," or simply "someone who believes in equality" — on the theory that the softer term would make the same point without the baggage. She rejects the substitution. Her argument is that the vagueness is exactly the problem: a word broad enough to include everyone stops naming who actually bears the cost of the arrangement being described. Gender inequality is a specific, asymmetric problem, and a term engineered to avoid friction ends up avoiding the substance too. Using the specific word is a refusal to let the problem get quietly generalized into something nobody has to own.

## Why it matters
This is a pattern well beyond this one word: whenever a claim about a specific group's experience gets rephrased into universal, feel-good language, something real is usually being smoothed away along with the discomfort. "Feminist" names a particular imbalance and a particular direction of correction; "humanist" describes a value everyone can already agree to in the abstract without changing anything. Adichie's insistence on precision is itself the argument — if the word makes people uncomfortable, that discomfort is information about how much resistance the underlying idea still meets, not a reason to find a more comfortable word.

## Example from the book
Adichie describes being warned, by a Nigerian critic reviewing her work, not to call herself a feminist — the reasoning offered was that feminists are unhappy women, women who couldn't find husbands. She also names a milder, more common version of the same retreat that she calls "Feminist Lite": people willing to say "I believe in equality, but—," where the "but" quietly walks back whatever the sentence just claimed. Both moments are examples of the same pressure at different levels of hostility — one openly dismissive, one polite — pushing the word toward something softer or conditional.

## Practical application
Notice the next time you or someone else reaches for a softer, vaguer substitute in the middle of naming a specific problem — not just about gender, but generally: "people struggle" instead of naming which people, "mistakes were made" instead of naming who made them. Ask what the vaguer phrasing is doing. Sometimes it's genuine inclusiveness; often it's a way of avoiding the discomfort of being specific about who the claim is actually about.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a word you've personally avoided using about yourself — feminist or otherwise — because of what people might assume about you if you claimed it? What did the softer alternative cost you in precision?
