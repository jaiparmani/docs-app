# 03 — Biology vs. the Rules Built on Top of It

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Adichie doesn't deny that men and women are physically different — she treats that as obvious and uninteresting. Her actual argument is about scale: the biological differences are real but narrow, while the social rulebook built on top of them is vast and mostly unrelated to biology at all. Somewhere back in human history, when physical strength determined who could hunt, fight, and dominate, it made a rough kind of sense for the physically stronger sex to end up running things. That's the origin she points to — not malice, just an old sorting rule from a world where muscle mattered more than it does now. The problem is that the world changed and the hierarchy didn't. Leadership today runs on intelligence, creativity, and judgment, none of which sort by sex, yet the old assignment of who's in charge persisted long after the reason for it disappeared.

## Why it matters
This distinction — a small biological fact versus a large inherited hierarchy — is what lets Adichie argue for change without having to argue against biology itself, which would be a losing and beside-the-point fight. Nobody has to pretend bodies are identical for the argument to work. The claim is narrower and harder to dismiss: whatever advantage physical strength once conferred stopped being the basis for who leads, who earns, or who decides a long time ago, which means the current arrangement is running on inertia, not on the reason it was originally built for.

## Example from the book
She points to how naturally people still default to imagining a leader, an executive, an authority figure as male — not because anyone consciously reasons their way there, but because the image was inherited from an era when physical dominance was the actual qualification, and nobody has gone back to update the picture even though the qualification changed completely.

## Practical application
Next time you catch yourself with a default mental image for a role — doctor, CEO, engineer, nurse, assistant — pause and ask what that default is actually tracking. If the role has nothing to do with physical strength, the default probably isn't tracking a real qualification at all; it's tracking an old picture that never got updated.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your own field or community does authority still seem to track something like "physical dominance once mattered here" rather than the actual skills the work now requires?
