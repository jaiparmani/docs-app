# 05 — Marriage, Anger, and the Pressure to Be Liked

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Adichie traces a specific strand of the training program that targets ambition itself: girls are raised to treat marriage as the highest achievement available to them, in a way boys simply aren't. A boy's success is measured by what he does; a girl's success gets measured, additionally and sometimes primarily, by whether she's married — which means two women can have identical accomplishments and be judged completely differently depending on their marital status. The mechanism that enforces this is social rather than legal: women are quietly taught to see other women as competitors for a limited pool of husbands, and to soften or downplay their own success so it doesn't read as threatening. Underneath both is a subtler rule — that a woman should prioritize being liked over being honest, which in practice means learning to suppress anger and ambition alike, because both are read as unlikeable in a way they aren't when a man displays them.

## Why it matters
This matters because it explains a specific, recognizable social reflex: the impulse to describe a successful woman as "successful, but not married" — as though the second fact quietly cancels or qualifies the first — while no equivalent qualifier exists for successful men. That reflex isn't really about marriage at all; it's a mechanism for keeping female achievement provisional, always pending a different kind of approval. Once you see it as a mechanism rather than a fact of life, the specific, almost automatic way people phrase these judgments becomes visible instead of invisible.

## Example from the book
Adichie points to how routinely a woman's professional success gets narrated alongside her relationship status, as if the second were the real scoreboard — successful women get met with a kind of concerned pity if unmarried, in a way that has no male equivalent, and women themselves often internalize the anxiety and moderate their own visible success to avoid appearing to have "failed" at the other, supposedly more important, goal.

## Practical application
Notice the next time you hear (or think) a sentence that pairs a woman's achievement with her relationship status as if the second needs to be accounted for — "she's brilliant, but she's still single" — and ask whether the same sentence structure would ever be applied to a man's achievement. If not, that asymmetry is the pattern Adichie is describing, caught in the act.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Have you ever downplayed your own success, anger, or ambition specifically to avoid seeming unlikeable — and what did you get in exchange for the smaller version of yourself?
