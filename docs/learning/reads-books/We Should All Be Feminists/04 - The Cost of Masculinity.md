# 04 — The Cost of Masculinity

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Adichie is explicit that the training program she describes doesn't only constrain women — it constrains men too, just in the opposite direction. Where girls are corrected for being too assertive, too loud, too visibly ambitious, boys are corrected for the opposite failures: showing fear, showing hurt, appearing anything less than strong, capable, and in control at all times. The essay's phrase for this is that boys are taught to be afraid of fear itself, afraid of weakness, afraid of vulnerability — not taught to manage those feelings, but taught that having them visibly is itself the failure. The result is a masculinity so rigid it has to be defended constantly, because it was never allowed room to be honest about its own limits in the first place.

## Why it matters
This reframing matters because it breaks the assumption that gender roles are a simple transaction where men benefit and women pay. Men pay too, just in a different currency — the freedom to be afraid, uncertain, or in need of help without it registering as a failure of manhood. A boy who's punished for crying doesn't stop feeling whatever made him cry; he just learns to hide it, which is a worse outcome for everyone, including him. Recognizing this cost is also what makes the essay's argument more than a grievance against men — it's a case that the current system is a bad deal for almost everyone involved, just unevenly bad.

## Example from the book
Adichie describes the pressure on men to always be the one who pays, to always project competence and control, and connects this to something more fragile underneath: the expectation that a woman in a relationship should actively manage a man's ego, be careful not to appear more successful or more capable than he is, so as not to threaten a sense of manhood that was built to be threatened by exactly that. The fragility isn't presented as men's personal failing — it's presented as the predictable result of raising them never to have practiced being anything other than strong.

## Practical application
Notice a moment when you (if you were raised as a boy) suppressed an honest reaction — fear, sadness, uncertainty — specifically because showing it would have registered as unmanly. Or, if you weren't, notice a time you unconsciously managed a man's ego to avoid threatening it. Either exercise makes the cost concrete rather than abstract — it's not a slogan, it's a specific moment where an honest response got edited for the sake of appearing strong.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What would you have said or done differently in a recent moment if showing uncertainty or fear hadn't felt like a risk to how you'd be seen?
