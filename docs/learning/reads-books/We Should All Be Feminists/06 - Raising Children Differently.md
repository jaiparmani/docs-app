# 06 — Raising Children Differently

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The essay's close returns to where it started — childhood — but turns the argument forward instead of backward. If gender roles are a training program rather than a fact of nature, then the program can be rewritten, and the place to rewrite it is at the point where it's currently installed: in how children are raised. Adichie's proposal is not to raise girls to be more like boys, or to erase difference, but to stop teaching boys and girls a different rulebook for the same underlying qualities — strength, capability, responsibility, honesty about one's own feelings. Teach a child to be strong because strength is useful, not because strength is what their sex requires of them. The essay's closing line makes the stakes explicit: culture does not make people, people make culture — meaning the training program persists only because it keeps getting run, generation after generation, and nothing about it is fixed except the fact that so far, nobody has stopped.

## Why it matters
This is the point where the essay's two earlier threads — the cost to women and the cost to men — merge into a single practical proposal instead of staying two separate grievances. If both boys and girls are currently being taught a narrower version of themselves than they're capable of, then a single change (raise them by the same standard) addresses both problems at once, rather than trading one group's constraints for the other's. It also reframes the whole essay from being about blame to being about a design choice that's still being made, actively, in ordinary households, right now — which means it's a choice available to change starting with the next child raised, not a historical wrong that can only be studied.

## Example from the book
Adichie contrasts the specific, elaborate list of instructions given to girls — about modesty, about marriage, about managing others' perceptions — with the comparatively short list given to boys, and proposes closing that gap not by adding rules to boys' lives but by removing the excess from girls'. The standard she offers as a replacement is a shared one: raise a child to be a full person, capable and responsible, and let that standard apply regardless of which set of expectations they were previously going to inherit.

## Practical application
If you're raising, mentoring, or simply around children, pick one instruction you're tempted to give differently depending on the child's sex — about toughness, about emotion, about ambition, about caretaking — and try giving the same instruction to a child of either sex. Notice what feels awkward about it; the awkwardness is usually the old rule resisting being dropped, not a sign the new instruction is wrong.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you could rewrite one instruction from your own childhood so it applied equally regardless of your sex, which one would you change first — and what do you think would have been different if it had?
