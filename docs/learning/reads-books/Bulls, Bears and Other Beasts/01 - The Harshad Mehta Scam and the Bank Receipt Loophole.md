# 01 — The Harshad Mehta Scam and the Bank Receipt Loophole

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book's opening episode is the scandal that made Harshad Mehta a household name in India: the securities scam of 1991-92. Mehta, a stockbroker, exploited the mechanics of "ready forward" deals — short-term lending arrangements between banks that were meant to be backed by government securities — to route bank funds into the stock market instead. Using fake or unbacked bank receipts (BRs) as collateral, he was able to borrow enormous sums that banks believed were secured by securities that didn't actually exist in the amounts claimed. That money went into buying shares, most famously driving up the price of Associated Cement Company (ACC) to extraordinary levels, which in turn drew in more money chasing the rally. The scheme wasn't a violation of a rule written on paper so much as an exploitation of the gap between two systems — banking settlement and stock trading — that weren't being reconciled in real time.

## Why it matters
This scam functions as the book's founding case study because it establishes the pattern the rest of the narrative keeps returning to: the biggest failures in Indian market history happen not where rules are absent, but where two systems meet and nobody is checking that they agree with each other. The banking system trusted the paper; the stock market trusted the money. Neither side was positioned to catch that the underlying securities backing the whole chain were fictional. It's also the episode credited with catalyzing serious regulatory reform — the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), which existed already in a limited form, was given real statutory teeth via the SEBI Act in 1992, largely as a direct response to this scandal being exposed.

## Example from the book
The scam was brought into public view by journalist Sucheta Dalal, whose reporting exposed the irregularities in the banking-to-market money flow that had let Mehta's positions balloon. The book treats this exposure itself as part of the story, not just the fraud — a reminder that in a system with weak formal oversight, financial journalism was doing some of the work that regulators weren't yet equipped to do. The aftermath included a market crash, a wave of investigations, and years of litigation, with Mehta becoming both a folk villain and, to some, a symbol of a market whose infrastructure hadn't caught up with the ambition of the people trading in it.

## Practical application
The transferable lesson isn't about stock-picking; it's about where to look for risk in any system you rely on. Fraud tends to cluster at reconciliation gaps — points where one party's record of a transaction isn't being verified in real time against another party's. When evaluating a financial product, a counterparty, or even an internal company process, it's worth asking specifically: what is the settlement lag here, and who is actually checking that the paper and the money agree with each other before more money moves on the strength of that paper?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your own financial life — a broker, a lender, a shared account — are you trusting a record you've never actually verified against the underlying reality?
