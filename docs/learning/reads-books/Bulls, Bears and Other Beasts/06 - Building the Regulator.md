# 06 — Building the Regulator

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A thread running underneath the book's individual scandal chapters is the slow, scandal-driven maturation of the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) itself. SEBI existed in a limited, largely advisory form from 1988, but it was the 1992 Harshad Mehta scandal that led directly to it being given real statutory power via the SEBI Act later that same year — the authority to investigate, penalize, and set binding rules for market participants, rather than merely to observe. The book treats SEBI's authority not as a fixed backdrop to the scandals it covers but as something that was itself forged, expansion by expansion, in direct response to the specific failures each scandal exposed.

## Why it matters
This framing matters because it inverts the usual assumption that a regulator exists first and scandals happen despite it. In the book's telling, much of SEBI's actual power is a byproduct of the scandals it failed to prevent — each crisis exposing a specific gap in its authority, which then got legislated shut, at least for that specific gap. The Ketan Parekh episode roughly a decade after Mehta prompted further tightening. This isn't a story of steady, planned institution-building; it's a story of an institution being built reactively, one scar at a time, which is a very different — and in the book's telling, more honest — account of how financial oversight actually comes into being than the clean narratives regulators tend to publish about themselves after the fact.

## Example from the book
The clearest marker of this pattern is the timing itself: the SEBI Act's passage in 1992 sits immediately after the Mehta scandal's exposure, not as part of some pre-planned regulatory roadmap. The book's implicit argument is that you can read the history of SEBI's powers as a direct map of the market's historical failures — each new power roughly corresponds to a specific gap a specific scandal had just revealed.

## Practical application
When evaluating how much to trust any regulatory or oversight body — financial or otherwise — it's worth asking not just what powers it currently holds, but what crisis each of those powers was a response to, and how long ago that was. An oversight regime that hasn't been tested by a new kind of failure in a long time isn't necessarily robust; it may simply not have encountered the next gap yet.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a rule or safeguard you rely on in your own work or finances — do you know what specific failure it was created in response to, and whether that context still applies?
