# 02 — Journalism Over Theory

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Nair's central structural choice, more than any single scandal, is to tell the history of the Indian stock market the way a crime-beat reporter tells a city's history: through specific operators, specific loopholes, and specific institutional blind spots, rather than through the abstracted vocabulary of an economics textbook. Where an academic treatment would fold Harshad Mehta's bank-receipt manipulation and a later scandal's circular trading into a single generic lesson about "leverage" or "informational asymmetry," Nair keeps each episode's actual mechanics intact — because that's where the real instruction lives. A book that flattens every scam into the same case study with the names swapped out ends up teaching a lesson that's too general to be useful.

## Why it matters
This choice has a real cost and a real payoff. The cost is that a reader doesn't come away with a portable analytical framework — no clean model to carry into evaluating the next market you look at, the way a systematic treatment of financial crises might offer. The payoff is texture: a granular sense of how Indian market infrastructure specifically has failed, repeatedly, in ways that were each only obvious in hindsight. That specificity matters because financial regulation is reactive by nature — regulators close the loophole that just got exploited, not the one that hasn't been invented yet — so understanding *how* a specific loophole worked is more useful preparation for spotting the next one than memorizing an abstract taxonomy of manipulation types.

## Example from the book
The clearest evidence of this approach is structural: the book doesn't open with a chapter defining "market manipulation" and then slot historical examples underneath it. It opens with people and events — the scam, the operator, the specific instrument being gamed — and lets the reader accumulate the general pattern themselves across chapters, rather than being handed the pattern up front and shown illustrations of it.

## Practical application
When you're trying to learn the failure modes of any domain — a market, an industry, an organization — resist the pull toward the single unifying framework too early. Seek out the specific, granular case histories first: what exactly happened, in what order, exploiting what precise gap. The generalizable lesson, if there is one, tends to hold up better when you've built it yourself from several detailed cases than when you've adopted someone else's abstraction and gone looking for cases to fit it.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a domain where you've been relying on a tidy general theory of "how things go wrong," and would a handful of specific, detailed case histories actually serve you better than the theory does?
