# 03 — The Ketan Parekh Scam and the K-10 Stocks

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Roughly a decade after Harshad Mehta, the book turns to a second major scandal built on a different mechanic entirely: the Ketan Parekh episode of 2000-01. Parekh, a Mumbai-based stockbroker, concentrated his activity in a small cluster of stocks — informally dubbed the "K-10" — many of them technology and media names riding the global dot-com wave. Through concentrated buying and coordinated trading among a small circle of players, he drove those stocks to prices disconnected from any underlying fundamentals, funded in part through borrowing linked to cooperative banks. When the dot-com bubble burst globally and the funding behind the positions came under strain, the K-10 stocks collapsed, taking a slice of the banking sector down with them.

## Why it matters
The significance of this episode, in the book's framing, is less about Parekh individually and more about what it proves institutionally: that the reforms triggered by the Mehta scandal a decade earlier — a strengthened SEBI, tighter rules around the specific bank-receipt mechanism Mehta had exploited — did not prevent a second major scam. They just meant the second scam had to find a different seam. Where Mehta had exploited the banking system's reconciliation gap, Parekh exploited concentrated ownership in illiquid stocks combined with bank lending that wasn't being scrutinized closely enough for its ultimate use. The instrument changed with the era — dot-com-flavored stocks instead of a cement company — but the underlying shape, concentrated leverage meeting weak oversight, repeated.

## Example from the book
The book situates this scam within the broader global dot-com bust, giving it a texture distinct from the 1992 episode: this wasn't purely a domestic regulatory failure, it was a domestic version of a manipulation playing out against the backdrop of an international speculative bubble that was cracking everywhere at once, which made the eventual unwind faster and harder to contain.

## Practical application
The recurring pattern worth internalizing is that closing yesterday's loophole doesn't close tomorrow's. If you're assessing risk in a market or system that has "already been reformed" after a past failure, it's worth asking what shape the *next* failure might take rather than assuming the fixed vulnerability was the only one. In investing terms specifically, a cluster of thinly traded stocks moving together, funded by opaque or hard-to-trace credit, is a pattern worth treating with more suspicion regardless of which decade or which country you're looking at.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> After a past failure gets "fixed," how often do you assume the system is now safe rather than asking what new gap the fix itself might have opened up?
