# Bulls, Bears and Other Beasts

<small>3 min read</small>

*Santosh Nair*

The premise is that the history of the Indian stock market is best told the way a crime beat reporter tells a city's history — through the scams, the operators, and the specific mechanics of how each boom curdled into the next bust — rather than through the abstracted, sanitized version that shows up in economics textbooks or in the sanded-off retrospectives regulators publish after the fact. Nair, a financial journalist rather than an academic or a fund manager, structures the book around the market's major episodes across several decades: the Harshad Mehta-era securities scam of the early '90s, and the string of booms and crashes that followed it, told through the operators, brokers, and regulatory blind spots specific to each one rather than as a single repeating morality tale.

## The case for journalism over analysis

That reportorial framing is the book's real argument, even if it's never stated as one: that market crashes are not abstract failures of "irrational exuberance" in the aggregate, they are concrete failures of specific incentive structures, specific regulatory gaps, and specific people who understood the gaps better than anyone watching them. An academic treatment of the same history would flatten these episodes into a single lesson about leverage or informational asymmetry; Nair's approach keeps each scandal's particular mechanics intact, which is where the actual instruction is. A pump-and-dump scheme run through the settlement-cycle loopholes of one era is not interchangeable with a broker exploiting bank receipts in another, even though a textbook chapter on "market manipulation" would happily treat them as the same case study with different names swapped in.

The tradeoff of that approach is the one you'd expect from journalism versus analysis: less generalizable framework, more texture. Reading it won't leave you with a portable model to apply to the next market you're evaluating the way a systematic treatment of financial crises would; it leaves you instead with a granular sense of how Indian market infrastructure specifically has failed, repeatedly, in ways that were each obvious only after the fact and often eerily specific to loopholes that got closed only once someone had already been ruined by them.

## What earns the five stars

The five-star register this earns is less about grand analytical ambition than about execution within the genre it's actually working in: financial-scandal narrative, done well, is a specific and unforgiving craft — it has to make market mechanics legible to a reader who isn't a finance professional without dumbing the mechanics down into inaccuracy, and it has to make institutional failure feel as consequential and human as the personal greed of the operators exploiting it. A chronicle of scams and cycles is only as good as its ability to make regulatory arbitrage feel like plot rather than homework, and that's the needle a book in this genre lives or dies threading. Judged as that kind of book rather than as a systematic economic history, it's doing exactly what it sets out to do.

## What it isn't

It's worth being precise about what the book doesn't attempt, since the temptation with market-scandal chronicles is to read them as either investment advice or as a unified theory of financial crises, and this is neither. It's closer in spirit to a well-reported true-crime history of an institution than to a Kindleberger-style theory of manias and panics — the value is in the specificity of what actually happened in the Indian market's particular history, not in a portable framework you walk away holding. For a reader who wants that context on Indian financial history specifically, rather than a general theory of why markets crash, that specificity is the whole point rather than a limitation.
