# 10 — Wounds Heal, Scars Last

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A single formative experience — especially a painful or frightening one — can permanently reset how a person perceives risk, long after the specific circumstances that caused it have disappeared. The event itself heals: the recession ends, the crisis passes, life returns to normal. But the scar it leaves — a lasting shift in how much risk feels acceptable, how much trust feels warranted, how safe "safe" needs to be — often stays for the rest of that person's life, shaping decisions in situations that have nothing to do with the original event.

## Why it matters
This is Housel's explanation for why people who look at the same data, the same market, the same set of facts can reach completely different, deeply held conclusions about how risky it is — and why neither side finds the other's confidence persuasive. It's not that one side is better informed. It's that each person is reasoning from a different set of scars, formed by whatever they personally lived through, and those scars function less like a data point and more like a lens that filters every future data point that comes after them. Recognizing this explains a lot of otherwise puzzling disagreement, and it's a caution against assuming your own risk tolerance is the objectively correct one rather than a personal history wearing the costume of logic.

## Example from the book
Housel points to people who lived through the Great Depression as a defining case: many carried a lasting wariness of debt and a compulsion to save for the rest of their lives, even decades later, even once their financial circumstances had become comfortable and the Depression itself was long over. He draws the same line to people who came of age during the dot-com crash of the late 1990s and early 2000s, whose relationship to risk in markets was permanently shaped by watching that bubble collapse — a caution that persisted regardless of how different later market conditions actually were. In both cases, the economic wound healed; the psychological scar governing behavior didn't.

## Practical application
When you notice someone's risk tolerance seems wildly out of step with current conditions — too cautious, or too reckless — look for the scar behind it rather than arguing with the position directly. And apply the same lens to yourself: identify the formative experience that's quietly setting your own baseline for risk, and ask whether it's still the right baseline for the situation you're actually in now, rather than the situation that created the scar.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's a scar from your own past — financial, personal, or otherwise — that's still setting your risk tolerance today, even in situations that have nothing to do with what originally caused it?
