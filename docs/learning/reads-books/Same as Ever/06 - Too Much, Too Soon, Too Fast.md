# 06 — Too Much, Too Soon, Too Fast

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Growth that happens too quickly tends to be structurally weaker than growth that happens slowly, even when the end result looks the same from the outside. Slow growth gives a system — a tree, a company, a skill, a fortune — time to develop the density and resilience that only comes from accumulating under some resistance. Fast growth skips that process, producing something that looks equivalent on the surface but lacks the internal strength to survive stress.

## Why it matters
This reframes "fast" as a hidden liability rather than a pure advantage. The instinct is to treat rapid growth as unambiguously good — more, sooner, is better than more, later. Housel's point is that the speed of growth and the durability of what's grown are often in tension: the very conditions that allow something to grow fast (abundant resources, no resistance, no forced patience) are the conditions that prevent it from developing the toughness it needs to last. That matters for evaluating anything that's growing impressively fast — a company, a portfolio, a habit — because impressive short-term growth is weak evidence of long-term durability, and can even be a warning sign of the opposite.

## Example from the book
Housel uses the biology of trees: a sapling that grows in the shade of a forest canopy, with limited sunlight, grows slowly and produces dense, hard wood. A tree planted alone in an open field, with abundant sunlight and no competition, grows fast — but the wood it produces is soft and airy, because it never had to develop density under constraint. Forester Peter Wohlleben's observation, which Housel cites, is blunt: a tree that grows quickly rots quickly, and never gets the chance to grow old. Housel pairs this with Warren Buffett's line that you can't produce a baby in one month by getting nine women pregnant — some processes have a minimum time they require, and no amount of resources thrown at them shortens that minimum.

## Practical application
When you're tempted to accelerate something that's currently growing steadily — pour in more capital, more hours, more leverage, to hit a result sooner — ask whether the speed itself is what's supposed to build the strength you'll eventually need, and whether skipping ahead means skipping the part that makes the result durable rather than fragile.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there something in your life right now — a business, a skill, a relationship — that's growing unusually fast, and if so, what would "dense wood" look like for it versus "soft, fast wood"?
