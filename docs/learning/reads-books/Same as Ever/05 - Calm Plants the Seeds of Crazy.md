# 05 — Calm Plants the Seeds of Crazy

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Stability is not the opposite of instability — it's the cause of it. Housel builds this chapter around economist Hyman Minsky's financial instability hypothesis: a long stretch without a recession doesn't mean recessions have been solved, it means people have had time to forget why they were ever cautious. Calm breeds confidence, confidence breeds more borrowing and more risk-taking, and that accumulating risk is exactly what turns into the next downturn. The absence of a crisis is what quietly manufactures the next one.

## Why it matters
This is the mechanism behind "this time is different" thinking, and it explains why the belief keeps recurring even though it's wrong almost every time. During a genuinely calm stretch, the case that things have changed for the better feels completely reasonable — the data really does look stable, the recent past really does support optimism. Nobody living through the calm period can point to evidence that it's a problem, because the evidence a calm period produces is exactly the evidence that convinces people to relax. That's what makes the pattern so hard to interrupt: it isn't fueled by obviously bad reasoning, it's fueled by reasonable-looking extrapolation from a stretch of good luck.

## Example from the book
Housel traces this back to Minsky's own context: in the 1960s, a wave of scientific and economic optimism led some economists to argue that with enough smart people and enough data, recessions themselves could be engineered out of existence. Minsky's hypothesis was a direct rebuttal — he argued the opposite was true, that a prolonged absence of recessions is precisely what sets the next one up, because stability itself is what convinces people to take on the leverage and risk that eventually breaks. The mechanism he described in the stock market works the same way: when volatility disappears, investors read the absence of volatility as an absence of risk, and pile into decisions they'd never make if they were being reminded of risk more often.

## Practical application
Treat an unusually calm, good stretch — in markets, in your industry, in your own finances — as a signal to tighten your risk management, not relax it. The instinct runs backward: calm feels like permission to take on more debt or more exposure, when historically calm is closer to a warning that complacency is quietly accumulating. Ask what you'd be doing differently right now if the current stretch of stability had already ended.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your own life has a long stretch of things going well made you quietly more careless — with money, with a relationship, with your health — than you'd have been if things had been shakier?
