# 08 — Keep Running

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
No competitive advantage lasts forever, and the more dominant an advantage becomes, the more it tends to plant the seeds of its own decline. Success breeds imitation, complacency, and a sense that the current approach is permanently correct — right at the moment when the world is moving on and a competitor without that baggage is free to adapt. The companies and strategies that stay ahead for a long time are usually the ones that keep behaving as if their edge is temporary, even after it's proven durable for years.

## Why it matters
This cuts against the natural instinct to treat past dominance as evidence of future dominance. A long track record of winning feels like it should be reassuring, but Housel's argument is that it's often the opposite — a long stretch of winning is exactly what erodes the paranoia and adaptability that produced the winning in the first place. The organizations and people who keep their edge longest tend to be the ones who never fully believe they've earned a permanent one, and who keep running scared long after the scoreboard says they don't have to.

## Example from the book
Housel points to Sears, General Motors, and Kodak — each a company that spent decades as the dominant, seemingly unassailable leader in its category, and each one that lost that position not because a superior competitor appeared overnight, but because years of dominance had dulled its willingness to keep adapting. The very success that made each company a household name was also what made it slow: entrenched ways of operating, confidence that the current model would keep working, and a loss of the urgency that had built the advantage in the first place. By the time each company recognized the threat, competitors without that inertia had already closed the gap.

## Practical application
Whatever edge you currently have — professionally, competitively, financially — treat it explicitly as temporary rather than earned permanently, and look for the place where you've started operating on autopilot because "this has always worked." The discomfort of staying paranoid after you've already won is exactly the discipline that keeps the win from expiring.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your own life or work have you started coasting on an advantage you built years ago, assuming it would keep working without you having to keep earning it?
