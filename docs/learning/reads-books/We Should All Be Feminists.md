# We Should All Be Feminists

<small>4 min read</small>

*Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie*

The argument is that gender, as practiced in most societies, functions less like a natural fact and more like a training program run on children from the moment they're old enough to be corrected — boys taught that vulnerability, gentleness, and dependence are failures of manhood to be drilled out of them, girls taught that ambition, appetite, and directness are failures of womanhood to be apologized for — and that the word "feminist" describes nothing more radical than the position that this training program is a problem worth naming and undoing. Adapted from Adichie's TEDx talk of the same name, the essay's second move is a defense of the word itself: against the pressure to soften it into "humanist" or qualify it into harmlessness, on the grounds that the specificity of naming which group bears the cost of the training program is exactly the part people want removed, and removing it is how the problem gets talked around instead of talked about.

## The argument works through particulars, not abstraction

What keeps the essay from reading as a manifesto of assertions is that it's built almost entirely out of small, concrete, recognizable scenes rather than statistics or theory — the kind of everyday moment where the training is visible in the act of happening, rather than argued for after the fact. That choice is the essay's real technique: it doesn't try to win the argument that gender roles are a cultural construction through data, it wins it by pointing at moments ordinary enough that most readers, regardless of where they started, will recognize the pattern from their own life rather than needing to be persuaded it exists. This is a much harder rhetorical trick to pull off in a short form than in a book-length treatment, since there's no room to build the case slowly — the essay has to make each example carry real weight, and Adichie's background as a novelist shows in how efficiently the scenes are chosen and how little explanation each one needs.

The redefinition of "feminist" is the more contested part of the argument, and Adichie handles the standard objections directly rather than avoiding them — that the word alienates men, that it implies hatred of men, that a more neutral term would achieve the same goal with less friction. Her response is essentially that the friction is the point: a word softened enough to be comfortable for everyone stops naming the specific and asymmetric thing it needs to name, and the discomfort some people feel at the word is itself diagnostic of how threatening the underlying idea is to a system that currently benefits from not being named. That's a strong rhetorical position but it's also somewhat unfalsifiable as stated — any objection to the word can be read as confirming the thesis rather than engaging it, which is a rhetorical convenience worth noticing even where the underlying point about linguistic softening has real merit.

## What's underdeveloped, given the format

The essay's brevity is mostly a strength but does leave real gaps. The training-program framing is most vivid and most persuasive applied to the specific cultural context Adichie is drawing from — Nigerian, and more broadly African, gender norms she grew up inside — and the essay doesn't spend much time on how the same mechanism varies across the very different gender regimes elsewhere in the world; the reader is left to do that generalizing work themselves, and it holds up reasonably well but isn't demonstrated. It's also worth noting explicitly that the essay isn't trying to be a comprehensive theory of gender or a policy argument — it's a persuasive case for a definition and a disposition, and judging it against the standard of a longer feminist text would be holding a fifteen-minute talk to the wrong bar.

## What carries over

The core reframe — that the roles assigned to both men and women are a system that costs both of them something, not a zero-sum arrangement where men simply benefit and women simply lose — is the essay's most useful and most durable idea, and it's argued through recognizable specifics rather than asserted. The defense of the word "feminist" against softening is the more debatable piece, worth taking seriously as a rhetorical stance even where it resists being argued with directly.
