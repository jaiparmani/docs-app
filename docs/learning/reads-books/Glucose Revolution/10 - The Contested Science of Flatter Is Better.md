# 10 — The Contested Science of "Flatter Is Better"

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
It's worth naming directly that CGM-driven advice for non-diabetic people is a genuinely contested area in nutrition science, not a settled one. Continuous glucose monitors were developed and validated for managing diagnosed diabetes, where sustained high glucose and large swings have clear, well-documented harm. Extending that same "flatter curve is always better" framework to metabolically healthy people is a newer and more debated claim. Some researchers argue this generalization oversimplifies real individual metabolic variability — a spike that's meaningful in a diabetic's physiology may carry different, much smaller implications in someone with normal insulin sensitivity — and that glucose spikes in healthy people haven't been shown to be harmful with anywhere near the same clarity as they have in diabetics.

## Why it matters
This matters for reading the book itself with the right calibration. Inchauspé writes with the confident, prescriptive tone of settled science throughout, but the leap from "CGM data shows spikes look different across meals" to "spikes are quietly causing widespread harm in the general population" is a bigger inferential jump than the tone suggests. That doesn't mean the hacks are useless — food-order and pairing effects on the glucose curve are real, replicated findings — but the broader claim that everyone should be actively managing their curve the way a diabetic manages theirs is where the evidence is thinner and the scientific community more divided.

## Example from the book
The book doesn't itself present this debate — it's written in a confident, advocacy register throughout, citing studies to support each hack without much space given to researchers who'd push back on the broader framework. What the book doesn't foreground is that population-level research on continuous glucose monitoring in non-diabetics is still relatively young, and that findings about optimal glucose patterns established in diabetic populations don't automatically transfer to people whose baseline insulin response is already functioning normally — a point some metabolic researchers have raised in response to the recent wave of consumer CGM and glucose-focused wellness content this book is part of.

## Practical application
Hold the specific hacks (food order, pairing carbs with fiber or protein, a short walk after eating) more firmly than the broader causal story connecting everyday glucose spikes to a long list of unrelated symptoms in healthy people. The hacks are cheap, low-risk, and individually testable regardless of which side of the broader scientific debate turns out to be right; the sweeping claim that flatter is unconditionally better for everyone is the part worth treating as one hypothesis among competing ones rather than settled fact.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If a claim in a health book is presented with total confidence but the underlying science is still actively debated, how do you decide which specific pieces of advice to adopt anyway versus which to treat with more skepticism?
