# 05 — Hack 3: Stop the Glucose Gremlins

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Inchauspé's term "glucose gremlins" refers to sugary foods and drinks eaten on their own, isolated from a meal — the mid-morning pastry with nothing else, the can of soda, the handful of candy eaten between meals rather than after one. Her argument is not that these foods are forbidden, but that eating them in isolation is what makes them spike glucose hardest: there's no fiber, protein, or fat present to slow absorption, so the sugar hits the bloodstream essentially undefended. The hack is less "never eat sugar" and more "don't eat sugar alone" — the same cookie eaten as dessert immediately after a meal containing protein and vegetables produces a meaningfully different curve than the same cookie eaten by itself at 4pm on an empty stomach.

## Why it matters
This is one of the more psychologically useful reframes in the book because it doesn't ask for abstinence, which is usually where restrictive food rules fail. Most people don't stop eating sugar because a book told them to; they eat less of it, feel deprived, and eventually rebound. Telling someone their sweet snack just needs *company* rather than elimination is a much smaller ask, and it directly explains a pattern many people already sense intuitively — that dessert after dinner feels different, physically, than the same dessert eaten alone mid-afternoon, even though the food itself hasn't changed.

## Example from the book
Inchauspé describes the common habit of an isolated sweet snack between meals — she uses examples like a cookie or candy eaten alone in the afternoon — as one of the sharpest, least-buffered spikes a person can produce in a normal day, precisely because there's nothing else in the stomach at that moment to slow absorption. Her recommended fix is not to cut the snack but to reposition it: eat it as dessert directly after a meal, when protein, fat, and fiber from the meal are still being digested and can blunt the resulting spike, rather than as a stand-alone snack hours away from other food.

## Practical application
Audit your typical day for "glucose gremlins" — sweet things eaten in isolation, away from a meal — and without eliminating them, try relocating them to immediately after a meal instead. A piece of chocolate after lunch rather than at 4pm on an empty stomach, or dessert directly following dinner rather than as a separate later snack, keeps the food but changes the physiological context it's eaten in.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of the sweet snacks you eat in a typical week. How many are eaten alone, away from a meal, versus attached to one? Would simply moving them — not removing them — be a change you could actually sustain?
