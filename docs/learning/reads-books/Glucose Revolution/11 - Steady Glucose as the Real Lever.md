# 11 — Steady Glucose as the Real Lever

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book's closing argument is a reframe of what "eating well" is even trying to optimize for. Calorie counting and macro tracking treat food as a ledger of totals to hit or stay under; Inchauspé's proposal is that the shape of the glucose curve across a day is a better lever for energy, mood, and appetite than the totals are, and — importantly — it's a lever you can pull without eliminating any food group or reducing how much you eat. All six hacks (chunks 3–8) are variations on the same underlying move: change the order, pairing, or timing of food and movement so the same calories produce a flatter curve, rather than changing what or how much you eat.

## Why it matters
This is what distinguishes the book from most other popular nutrition advice, and it's a genuine point in its favor regardless of how the broader spike-harm debate (chunk 10) eventually settles: it's compatible with, not opposed to, whatever eating pattern someone already follows. Someone eating for weight loss, muscle gain, or simple maintenance can layer these hacks on top of their existing approach — the hacks aren't a diet in the sense of a set of foods to avoid, they're a set of orderings and pairings to apply to food already being eaten. That's a meaningfully lower bar than most nutrition books ask of a reader, and it's likely part of why the hacks are sustainable enough that a reader might actually keep doing them.

## Example from the book
Inchauspé closes by returning to her own experience wearing a continuous glucose monitor: what changed her own eating wasn't a new diet in the conventional sense, but noticing in real time which small changes to the same meals flattened her curve, and building the ten hacks as the accumulated, generalizable version of that personal experimentation. She frames the aim not as achieving a flat line — some rise after eating is normal and expected — but as avoiding the sharp spike-and-crash pattern that drives the cravings and energy dips the book spends its first chapters describing.

## Practical application
Rather than adopting every hack simultaneously, pick the one or two with both the strongest evidence and the lowest friction for your own life — food order and carb-pairing are the best-supported and easiest to start with — and apply them consistently for a couple of weeks before layering in the others. Judge the results by how you actually feel (energy, cravings, afternoon crashes) rather than by a number on a scale, since that felt experience is the outcome the whole framework is built around.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you had to pick just one of these hacks to actually keep doing a month from now, which one would it be — and is it the one with the strongest evidence, or just the one that's easiest to fit into your life?
