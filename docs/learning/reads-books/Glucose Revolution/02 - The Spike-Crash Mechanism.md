# 02 — The Spike-Crash Mechanism

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The physiological chain Inchauspé leans on is well established, even if some of what she builds on top of it is more speculative: a fast, high glucose spike triggers a correspondingly fast, large insulin response, because insulin's job is to clear glucose out of the bloodstream and into cells. When the spike is steep, the insulin response frequently overshoots — it clears not just the excess glucose but pulls blood sugar down below its starting baseline. That trough, arriving roughly ninety minutes after the meal, is what she argues registers subjectively as an energy crash, brain fog, and — critically — hunger and sugar cravings, even though you may have eaten a substantial meal not long before.

## Why it matters
This mechanism reframes a very common experience — the 3pm slump, the mid-afternoon craving for something sweet despite having eaten lunch — as a predictable downstream consequence of how a meal was structured, not a willpower failure or a sign you didn't eat enough. If the crash is what's driving the craving, then the craving isn't really "I need more energy," it's "my blood sugar just fell below where it started and my body is asking for a fast fix" — which is usually more sugar, which produces another spike, and can start a cycle where each crash sets up the next craving. Understanding the mechanism is what makes the later hacks (chunks 3–8) make sense as more than arbitrary rules — each one is specifically aimed at blunting the spike so the compensatory crash never gets triggered in the first place.

## Example from the book
Inchauspé describes the common scenario of a sweet, low-fiber breakfast — pastry, juice, sweetened cereal — producing a sharp glucose spike within the first thirty to sixty minutes, followed by an insulin overshoot that drops blood sugar below baseline around mid-morning. The person experiencing this typically doesn't connect the ten-a.m. urge for a second coffee and something sweet to the breakfast itself; it feels like an independent event, a separate wave of hunger or tiredness. Her argument is that it's the same wave, just delayed — the crash from breakfast is what's manifesting as the mid-morning craving, and reaching for something sweet at that point is treating a symptom by reproducing its cause.

## Practical application
The next time an unexplained craving or energy dip hits roughly ninety minutes to two hours after a meal, treat it as data rather than a random event: what was in that meal, and was it eaten in a way likely to spike quickly (sweet, low-fiber, eaten on its own)? Rather than reaching immediately for more sugar to fix the crash — which restarts the cycle — the more useful move is to notice the pattern over a few days and adjust the meal that precedes it, using the ordering and pairing principles in the chunks that follow.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of your most reliable mid-afternoon or mid-morning craving. If you traced it back roughly ninety minutes, what did you actually eat right before it started — and does it fit the spike-then-crash pattern?
