# 01 — The Glucose Curve, Not the Average

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Inchauspé's central move is to shift attention away from the number most people already track — average blood sugar, or whether it falls inside a "normal" range on an annual panel — and toward the *shape* of the curve after any single meal. A meal that sends glucose up fast and steep, then crashes it back down fast, is a different physiological event from the same total amount of glucose entering the bloodstream gradually, even if the two meals contain identical calories and even identical sugar. The spike-and-crash pattern, not the total glucose load, is what she argues actually drives how you feel a few hours later. This reframing matters because it applies to people who are not diabetic and would never see anything abnormal on a standard blood test — the curve's shape is invisible to the tests most people get.

## Why it matters
Most nutrition advice people already know — count calories, watch carbs, avoid sugar — treats food as a ledger of totals. The curve framing says two meals that are identical on that ledger can produce very different physical experiences, because the body's response depends on speed of delivery, not just amount. This explains a common experience that calorie-counting can't: eating "enough" and still crashing an hour later, or eating something technically "healthy" (a bowl of cereal with skim milk, a smoothie) and feeling hungrier afterward than before. If the curve, not the total, is doing the work, then the fix isn't necessarily eating less or eating differently in terms of ingredients — it's changing the order and pairing of the same food so the curve flattens.

## Example from the book
Inchauspé's own account of this book's origin is that she started wearing a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) — a small sensor that tracks blood sugar in real time rather than at a single blood-draw moment — and was surprised to see large, sharp spikes after meals she had always considered fine, like a bowl of granola or a piece of fruit eaten on its own. Watching her own curve in real time, rather than trusting a single fasting-glucose number from a yearly checkup, is what pushed her toward the idea that the *pattern* across a day, not a single snapshot, is the more informative signal — and that small changes to the same food (not different food) could visibly flatten the curve on the monitor.

## Practical application
Before assuming a food is "good" or "bad," ask what shape of curve it's likely to produce on its own versus in combination with something else. A piece of fruit eaten alone on an empty stomach will spike faster than the same fruit eaten after a handful of nuts or at the end of a meal — the food hasn't changed, only its position and company have. Start noticing, without needing a CGM yourself, which foods reliably leave you crashing roughly ninety minutes later (sweet coffee-shop breakfasts, juice, cereal) versus which leave you steady, and treat that pattern as more diagnostic than the ingredient list alone.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a food you've always assumed was either "healthy" or "unhealthy" based on its ingredients. Would your assessment change if you thought about it in terms of the curve it produces rather than what's in it?
