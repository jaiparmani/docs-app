# 02 — The Rich Don't Work for Money

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Lesson One. Rich dad's teaching method is deliberately uncomfortable: he pays nine-year-old Kiyosaki and Mike ten cents an hour to work in his store, then, a few weeks in, cuts their pay to nothing. The point isn't cruelty — it's forcing the boys to feel, directly, the two emotions Kiyosaki argues run most people's financial lives without ever being examined: fear (of not having enough money) and desire (for more of it). Most adults, he claims, respond to a job or paycheck they resent the same way a child does to an unfair wage — with anger — and then either quit in protest or grudgingly accept it and repeat the cycle for decades without ever stepping back to question the arrangement itself.

## Why it matters
The claim here isn't that money is unimportant — it's that fear and desire, left unexamined, keep people locked into working harder for money without ever asking whether the job itself is the right lever to pull. A raise doesn't resolve the fear of not having money; it just resets the fear at a higher number, because the underlying belief about money never got questioned. Financial fear operates mostly beneath conscious awareness, which is exactly why it's so durable — you can't argue yourself out of a reflex you've never noticed you're running.

## Example from the book
When rich dad cuts the boys' pay to zero, Kiyosaki gets furious and confronts him directly. Rich dad uses the confrontation as the actual lesson: he points out that this exact reaction — anger at being underpaid, followed by either quitting or silent resentment — is what most working adults do for their entire careers, without ever using the anger as a signal to ask a bigger question. Rather than quitting or demanding a raise, the boys are pushed toward a third option: they stop asking to be paid more for their labor and instead start a business of their own — a comic-book lending library — the first time in the book that frustration gets redirected into ownership instead of into either compliance or a formal walkout.

## Practical application
Next time you feel the specific irritation of being underpaid, overworked, or stuck in a job that isn't going anywhere, treat the irritation itself as data rather than just something to vent or suppress. Before deciding to quit or to grit your teeth and stay, ask the question rich dad was pushing the boys toward: is there a version of this situation where you stop trading hours for money on someone else's terms and start building something that's yours instead — even a small one?

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a time you felt genuinely angry about how you were being paid or treated at work. Did that anger get directed at quitting, at quiet resentment, or at building something of your own — and looking back, which of those would rich dad have said was the most useful response?
