# 01 — The Two Dads

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book is built around a single structural device: two real father figures, held up against each other on every question about money. Kiyosaki's own father — "poor dad" — was highly credentialed, held a doctorate, and rose to head Hawaii's state department of education, yet lived paycheck to paycheck and habitually said "I can't afford it" about anything beyond routine expenses. His best friend Mike's father — "rich dad" — never finished the eighth grade, but built a string of businesses and real estate holdings, and habitually asked "How can I afford it?" instead. Every subsequent lesson in the book is delivered as a contrast between what these two men believed, not as abstract financial theory.

## Why it matters
This is the book's whole method, not just its opening hook. Rather than arguing for a set of financial principles on their own terms, it makes the case narratively — by pairing two men who looked similarly positioned to succeed (intelligence, ambition, work ethic) and showing how differently their lives turned out based on beliefs about money rather than credentials or income. That's what makes the book readable and persuasive as a story, and it's also exactly why its evidentiary weight is worth questioning later (see chunk 08) — the whole argument leans on these two lives having actually played out this way.

## Example from the book
At around age nine, frustrated that his family couldn't afford things other kids had, Kiyosaki and Mike go to Mike's father and ask him to teach them how to get rich. Rich dad doesn't hand them money or a lecture. He offers Kiyosaki a job in his convenience store for ten cents an hour — a deliberately unsatisfying wage that sets up the entire first lesson of the book (chunk 02): that the way most people respond to a bad deal for their labor reveals more about their financial thinking than any amount of theory could.

## Practical application
For one week, notice which of the two phrases is your reflexive response to something you want but can't currently afford: "I can't afford it" (a full stop) or "How can I afford it?" (a question that keeps your brain working on the problem). You don't have to act on the second phrase immediately — the point is just to catch which one fires automatically, because that reflex is doing more work in your financial life than you'd expect.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a specific adult who shaped your early beliefs about money — not necessarily a parent. What was their reflexive phrase about things they couldn't afford, and how much of your own reflex did you inherit from them without ever examining it?
