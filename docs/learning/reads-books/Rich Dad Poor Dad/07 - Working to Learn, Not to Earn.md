# 07 — Working to Learn, Not to Earn

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Lesson Six. Kiyosaki argues that the standard advice to specialize — pick a lane, climb it, become the expert — optimizes for the wrong thing early in a career. Rich dad instead pushed him toward jobs and assignments chosen for the specific competencies they'd build: sales and marketing (because the inability to sell and communicate, rich dad claimed, holds back more otherwise-capable people than any other single skill), plus systems thinking and cash-flow management, over jobs that simply paid the most or matched a chosen profession most tightly. The recommendation isn't to be a permanent generalist — it's to sequence early career moves around skill acquisition, and only optimize for salary and specialization later, once the broader financial and communication skill set is already in hand.

## Why it matters
This reframes a common career trade-off — take the higher-paying job versus take the job that teaches you more — as usually a false choice in favor of the paycheck. Kiyosaki's claim is that skills like selling, negotiating, reading a balance sheet, and managing cash flow compound across every future venture a person attempts, whether that's climbing further in employment, starting a business, or investing, while a narrow technical specialization mostly only pays off within that specialization. Someone who can sell, manage money, and understand how a business's numbers fit together carries that toolkit into every future opportunity; someone who optimized purely for the highest salary at each step may have a strong paycheck but a much narrower set of transferable capabilities to fall back on if that particular ladder disappears.

## Example from the book
Kiyosaki recounts turning down a well-paying, prestigious job offer after college — a decision his highly educated "poor dad" found difficult to understand — in favor of a sales job with Xerox that paid comparatively little, specifically because rich dad had convinced him that Kiyosaki was terrified of selling and rejection, and that this fear was a bigger long-term liability than a modest starting salary. He describes forcing himself through the discomfort of cold calls and rejection for years, treating the job as an education in a skill he'd need for the rest of his business life, rather than judging the job primarily by its paycheck. He credits that decision, not any single subsequent deal, as one of the more consequential choices in his early career.

## Practical application
When evaluating a job offer, a lateral move, or even a volunteer role, add a second column next to the salary: what specific, transferable skill would this role force you to build that you don't currently have — sales, negotiation, reading financial statements, managing people, structuring deals? Weigh that column deliberately against the paycheck, especially early in a career, rather than defaulting to whichever option pays more, and be honest about whether you're avoiding a lower-paying option because of the pay or because it would push you into something uncomfortable, like rich dad pushed Kiyosaki into sales.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a skill you avoid — sales, public speaking, reading a balance sheet, negotiating — precisely because it makes you uncomfortable. What would it cost you, over the next ten years, to keep avoiding it versus spending a year or two deliberately building it now?
