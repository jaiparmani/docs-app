# 06 — Pay Yourself First

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Lesson Four. Rich dad's rule is simple to state and deliberately uncomfortable to follow: every time income arrives, direct money into your asset column before you pay anyone else — before rent, before the electric bill, before creditors. Most financial advice says to pay your bills first and save or invest whatever's left over; rich dad inverts the order on purpose, because he's convinced that for most people "whatever's left over" is reliably zero. Paying yourself first means the asset-column contribution isn't a leftover, it's the first and least negotiable line item, even when the month's other obligations are staring you down.

## Why it matters
The mechanism only works because of what it does under pressure. If you pay your creditors first and yourself with the leftovers, you never build the discipline of finding a way to cover shortfalls — you just quietly let the asset-building slide whenever things get tight, which, for most people, is most of the time. Paying yourself first and letting the pressure from unpaid bills build instead forces a different response: you're pushed to find additional income, negotiate, hustle, or otherwise solve the problem creatively, rather than solving it the easy way by skipping the one payment (to yourself) that has no one chasing you for it. Rich dad's view is that this discomfort is the entire point — it's what keeps the habit of self-payment from quietly evaporating the moment life gets financially inconvenient, which is also, not coincidentally, the moment it matters most.

## Example from the book
Kiyosaki describes rich dad's own practice of paying himself first no matter how tight cash got in his businesses, and the pressure that created — bills stacking up, creditors calling — as a feature rather than a bug. Rather than caving and diverting the money he'd already committed to his asset column, rich dad used the pressure as fuel: he'd go work harder, find another deal, or otherwise generate the extra income needed to satisfy his creditors without ever touching the money already earmarked for his assets. Kiyosaki argues that most people do the reverse instinctively — pay every outside obligation in full, then treat their own savings or investing as the flexible, postponable line — and that this single ordering choice, repeated month after month for a career, is a major reason many people who pay their bills responsibly still end up with no assets to show for decades of income.

## Practical application
Before your next paycheck lands, set an automatic transfer — even a small, fixed amount — that moves money into an investment or savings account the moment income arrives, ahead of any bill payment. Resist the temptation to treat that transfer as flexible or postponable if a tight month hits; instead, treat the resulting pressure as rich dad did — a prompt to find a way to cover the gap elsewhere, rather than a signal to skip paying yourself. Start with an amount small enough that the "find a way" muscle actually gets exercised without derailing your ability to cover essentials.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> When money is genuinely tight in a given month, which payment do you instinctively treat as the flexible one — a bill, or the money you'd planned to put toward your own assets? What would change if you reversed that instinct even once?
