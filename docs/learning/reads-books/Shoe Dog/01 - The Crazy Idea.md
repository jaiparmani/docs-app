# 01 — The Crazy Idea

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
In 1962, fresh out of Stanford's MBA program, Phil Knight took a solo trip around the world built around a single idea from a business-school paper he'd written: Japanese-made running shoes could do to the German-dominated athletic shoe market — Adidas and Puma controlled almost all of it — what Japanese cameras had already done to German cameras, undercutting them on price without sacrificing quality. Knight wasn't a manufacturer or a designer. He was a middle-distance runner from the University of Oregon who understood shoes as a customer, and who had just enough business-school framing to turn a hunch into something that sounded like a plan. He routed part of the trip through Kobe, Japan, specifically to walk into the offices of Onitsuka Co., maker of Tiger shoes, and pitch himself as the West Coast representative of an American distribution company — Blue Ribbon Sports — that did not yet exist anywhere except in his head.

## Why it matters
What makes this the founding moment of the book, rather than just a colorful anecdote, is how little separated the idea from a fantasy. Knight had no capital, no company, no product, and no relationship with Onitsuka — only a theory and the willingness to show up and say it out loud to strangers as if it were already real. The entire rest of *Shoe Dog* is, in a sense, the slow, grinding process of making that overstatement true after the fact. It's a useful corrective to the version of entrepreneurship where the idea is the hard part and the execution is a formality; here the idea took an afternoon of nerve, and the execution took the next eighteen years.

## Example from the book
Knight describes walking into the Onitsuka offices in a suit that didn't quite fit the moment, improvising a pitch about the size and appetite of the American market, and coming away with samples and an informal understanding that Blue Ribbon Sports would be able to import and sell Tiger shoes on the West Coast. He hadn't incorporated anything yet. He went home to Oregon, took a day job as an accountant, and started selling the imported Tiger shoes out of the trunk of his Plymouth Valiant at track meets — a company that existed, in its first years, mostly at the tailgate.

## Practical application
Notice how much of Knight's early advantage came from being willing to represent something slightly ahead of its actual existence — a company that was one conversation and one shipment away from being real, presented with the confidence of something already established. The lesson isn't "lie about your company"; it's that the gap between "I have an idea" and "I am the person who does this" can be closed by simply acting as though you already are that person, then spending the following years making the claim true. Where in your own work are you waiting for permission or proof before you'll say the thing out loud — and what would happen if you said it first and built the proof after?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Knight's pitch to Onitsuka worked before Blue Ribbon Sports had a single dollar or employee behind it. What is the version of that pitch you haven't made yet because you're waiting to feel more ready — and what's actually stopping you from making it now?
