# 09 — Betting the Company Every Season

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The cash crisis described in Chapter 5 wasn't a one-time event to be solved and moved past — it was structural, built into the mechanics of how the shoe business actually worked, and it recurred on a predictable seasonal cycle for years. Manufacturing shoes in Japan and shipping them to the United States took months; retailers ordered on their own seasonal calendar; and BRS had to commit to and pay for a production run well before it knew for certain it could sell through the previous one. Every selling season effectively required the company to place a bet — sized larger than the last one, if the business was growing — on demand that hadn't fully materialized yet, using money it largely didn't have on hand.

## Why it matters
This is what made the crisis feel less like a series of unlucky events and more like a recurring feature of the business Knight had chosen to run. Each season's order had to be placed before the previous season's sales had fully proven out, which meant the company's growth rate was effectively capped not by demand — demand was rarely the constraint — but by how large a bet its financing could support. Understanding the crisis as structural rather than incidental reframes the whole first half of the book: the bank meetings, the Nissho Iwai financing, the constant renegotiation weren't a string of separate emergencies so much as the recurring cost of operating a growing business inside a supply chain with long, unforgiving lead times.

## Example from the book
Knight describes the rhythm of the business almost like a recurring gamble: place the order, wait months for production and shipping, sell through the previous batch to generate the cash to pay for the new one arriving, and hope the timing worked out closely enough that the company didn't run out of cash in the gap. A single season where sales came in slower than expected, or a shipment was delayed, could compress that timing enough to trigger exactly the kind of banking crisis described in earlier chapters — meaning the "crisis" wasn't really a discrete event so much as the visible symptom of a structural mismatch that was always present underneath.

## Practical application
If your own business has a similarly long gap between committing resources and collecting revenue — a production lead time, a sales cycle, a development timeline — it's worth explicitly modeling that gap as a recurring structural risk rather than treating each instance of cash strain as a surprise. The fix usually isn't working harder in the moment of crisis; it's redesigning the financing, the order sizes, or the lead times themselves so the structural mismatch shrinks. What's the recurring timing gap in your own work that keeps generating the same kind of crisis, season after season, that you haven't yet addressed at the structural level?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Blue Ribbon Sports's crisis wasn't a single bad quarter — it was a structural mismatch between when the company had to pay and when it got paid, repeating every season. Is there a recurring version of that same gap somewhere in your own work that you've been treating as a series of unrelated emergencies rather than one structural problem to redesign?
