# 10 — Going Public to Buy Peace

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
By the time Nike went public in 1980, the company was no longer the fragile importer of the early chapters — it had its own brand, its own manufacturing relationships, and a real share of the American athletic shoe market. But the underlying financing problem described throughout the book — chronic thinness of working capital relative to the size of the orders growth demanded — had never actually gone away, it had only gotten larger in absolute terms as the company grew. The IPO, in Knight's telling, wasn't primarily a triumphant cash-out or a validation lap; it was, at its core, a way to finally and permanently solve the capital problem that had nearly ended the company multiple times over the preceding decade.

## Why it matters
This framing matters because it connects the book's ending back to its actual throughline. A less honest memoir might present the IPO as the payoff for years of vision and hard work, full stop. Knight's account is more specific than that: going public was the mechanism that finally let the company stop depending on the goodwill of individual bankers and the patience of trading-company financing, by replacing debt-driven, season-to-season survival with a permanent equity cushion. The crisis that structured most of the book — the recurring gap between what the company owed and what it had on hand — is the same problem the IPO was designed to solve, just at a much larger scale than the earlier bank negotiations.

## Example from the book
Knight describes the lead-up to the IPO with a mixture the book has used throughout for its big moments: some genuine relief that the company might finally be free of its old vulnerability, alongside an odd wistfulness about giving up the kind of total, if precarious, control he'd held onto through a decade of near-collapses. The Michael Jordan endorsement deal and the "Just Do It" advertising era that made Nike a cultural institution came after this narrative arc closes — the book is much more interested in the company's fight to survive than in the fame that followed.

## Practical application
It's worth asking, of any major structural decision in your own work — raising outside capital, taking on a partner, changing your business model — what specific recurring problem it's actually meant to solve, rather than treating it as a generic milestone of success. Knight didn't take the company public because IPOs are what successful companies do; he did it because it was the specific tool that matched the specific, recurring problem that had almost ended the company more than once. What's the actual structural problem underneath your own next big decision, and does the decision you're considering actually address it?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Knight frames the IPO less as a victory lap and more as the final fix for a problem that had nearly killed the company repeatedly. Is there a milestone you're chasing in your own work mostly because it signals success — where you haven't actually checked whether it solves the specific problem that's been recurring underneath everything else?
