# 05 — Outrunning the Balance Sheet

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The single structural fact that shapes most of *Shoe Dog* is this: Blue Ribbon Sports was, for its entire first decade and beyond, profitable and growing and almost perpetually out of cash at the same time. Every dollar the company earned selling one season's shipment of shoes was already spoken for — committed to ordering the next, larger shipment demanded by growing demand — before it ever sat in an account as free cash. Growth, which should have been the company's greatest asset, was instead its most dangerous liability, because faster growth meant a bigger next order, which meant a bigger financing gap, which meant more exposure to the bank's patience running out.

## Why it matters
This is the detail that separates *Shoe Dog* from the tidier startup narratives where the crisis is a single dramatic low point before the triumphant turn. Here, undercapitalization isn't a crisis the company survives once — it's the persistent condition of the business for most of a decade, recurring every single season as new orders came due. Knight describes it almost as a chronic illness rather than an acute one: manageable most of the time, but never actually cured, and always one bad quarter away from becoming fatal. Understanding this is the key to understanding almost everything else in the book — the frantic bank negotiations, the reliance on Nissho Iwai, even the eventual decision to go public are all downstream of this one structural mismatch between how fast the company could sell shoes and how fast it could actually get paid for them.

## Example from the book
Knight repeatedly describes the mechanics: BRS had to pay its manufacturers well before it received payment from the retailers who'd ordered the shoes, and the gap between those two dates only widened as order sizes grew. A stronger sales quarter, paradoxically, could put more strain on the company's finances than a weak one, because it meant an even larger production order — and an even larger sum the company didn't yet have — was now due.

## Practical application
The instinct in a growing business is to treat rising sales as unambiguously good news. Knight's account is a useful corrective: growth that outpaces your financing arrangements isn't success, it's a countdown. If your own revenue or demand is growing faster than your cash conversion cycle can support, the actual constraint on your business isn't sales — it's working capital, and it deserves the same urgent attention founders usually reserve for landing the next customer.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Blue Ribbon Sports's best sales quarters were often its most financially dangerous ones, because each one demanded a bigger bet on the next order. Is there a part of your own work where more success would actually strain you rather than relieve you — and have you built the capacity to handle that version of winning?
