# 07 — The Lifeline from Nissho Iwai

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
When BRS's American banking relationships were stretched to their limit, an unexpected source of stability came from Nissho Iwai, a large Japanese trading company that had its own reasons to want Blue Ribbon Sports to keep growing — it earned fees and built its own trading relationships by financing the flow of goods between Japanese manufacturers and American buyers like BRS. Nissho Iwai effectively became a second, less visible lender to the company, providing financing arrangements that helped bridge the gap between paying Japanese manufacturers and collecting from American retailers, at moments when Knight's domestic bank was unwilling or unable to extend further.

## Why it matters
This relationship is one of the clearest examples in the book of the company's survival depending on something Knight didn't design or fully control: a large foreign trading company's own strategic interest in keeping the shoe supply chain moving. It wasn't a relationship BRS could have manufactured on demand — it existed because Nissho Iwai had its own reasons, tied to its business in Japan, to want Knight's company to succeed. That's part of the book's broader honesty about luck: Nissho Iwai's willingness to help wasn't primarily a reward for BRS's merits, it was a byproduct of a larger company's incentives lining up, for its own reasons, with Knight's survival.

## Example from the book
At more than one point when the American bank relationship was at its most fragile, Nissho Iwai's financing gave BRS room to keep placing the orders its growth required, without which the company would have had to either turn down demand it couldn't afford to fill or risk defaulting on its existing bank obligations. Knight is candid that this outside lifeline, arriving from a source he hadn't originally built the company around, mattered as much to the company's survival through this period as any decision he made himself.

## Practical application
It's worth actively looking for the version of Nissho Iwai in your own situation: a party whose interests happen to overlap with your survival, even though they aren't investing in you out of belief in your mission. Suppliers, adjacent companies, even competitors sometimes have their own reasons to want you to keep operating — and those relationships can matter as much as the ones you deliberately cultivated. Have you mapped who benefits from your continued existence for reasons that have nothing to do with rooting for you specifically?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Nissho Iwai helped keep Blue Ribbon Sports alive not out of belief in Knight's vision, but because the arrangement served its own business. Who in your current situation might have that same kind of self-interested reason to want you to succeed — and have you actually cultivated that relationship, or only noticed it in hindsight?
