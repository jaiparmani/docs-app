# 02 — A Handshake Instead of a Contract

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The original arrangement between Blue Ribbon Sports and Onitsuka was never pinned down in the way a distribution agreement of this importance should have been. Knight came away from Kobe with samples, warm words, and an understanding that BRS would be the West Coast (and later national) distributor for Tiger shoes in the United States — but not with the kind of specific, binding, long-term contract that would have protected either side from the other's second thoughts once the relationship became valuable. For years the whole partnership ran on goodwill, informal correspondence, and the assumption that both sides intended to keep behaving the way they had been behaving.

## Why it matters
This matters because it planted the seed of the book's central legal crisis years before that crisis actually arrived. A relationship built on an unwritten understanding works exactly as long as both parties' interests stay aligned — and stops working the moment one side sees a better opportunity elsewhere. Knight built an entire company on top of a supplier relationship he did not have durable legal control over, which meant that Blue Ribbon Sports's fate was never fully in Blue Ribbon Sports's hands. The ambiguity that felt like flexibility in the early years became existential risk once Onitsuka started wondering whether it needed Knight at all.

## Example from the book
As Blue Ribbon Sports grew and proved that there was real American demand for Tiger shoes, the terms of the relationship kept needing to be renegotiated, and each renegotiation happened from a position of some uncertainty about what Knight was actually entitled to versus what he was being allowed to keep doing. That vagueness is what later let Onitsuka executives explore other US distribution options without technically violating any explicit agreement — because there wasn't one specific enough to violate. Knight spent real energy over the years just trying to get the relationship formalized into something he could rely on, with only partial success.

## Practical application
The instinct to avoid a hard, explicit negotiation with a partner who's currently being generous is understandable — it feels like you'd be jeopardizing a good thing by asking for terms. But an informal understanding only protects you as long as it's convenient for the other side to honor it. If a relationship — a supplier, a co-founder, a key client — is becoming load-bearing for your business, that's exactly the moment to convert the handshake into something specific and durable, even if the conversation is uncomfortable. Where in your own working life are you relying on an understanding that was never actually written down?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Blue Ribbon Sports let its most important relationship stay informal for years because formalizing it felt like it might disrupt something that was working. Is there a relationship in your own work you're avoiding pinning down for the same reason — and what's the actual cost of that ambiguity if the other side's interests ever shift?
