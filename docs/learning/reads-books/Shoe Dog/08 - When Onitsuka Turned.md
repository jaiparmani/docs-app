# 08 — When Onitsuka Turned

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The informal, never-quite-formalized relationship with Onitsuka, in place since the 1962 Kobe meeting, eventually broke — and it broke in a way that confirmed Knight's worst fear about never having pinned the terms down. As Blue Ribbon Sports proved out the American market for Tiger shoes, evidence emerged that Onitsuka was exploring other US distribution arrangements, potentially cutting BRS out of the relationship it had spent a decade building. What had felt for years like a productive if under-specified partnership turned out to have always been a one-sided dependency: BRS needed Onitsuka far more than Onitsuka needed BRS, and once that imbalance became inconvenient for Onitsuka, the relationship stopped serving Knight's interests.

## Why it matters
The Onitsuka rupture is the moment the book's slow-burn tension over the missing contract from Chapter 2 pays off, and it's also the moment that made the Nike brand launch (Chapter 4) existentially necessary rather than merely prudent. Knight and his team had already begun quietly building an alternative — their own brand, under their own name, with their own manufacturing relationships in Japan — before the Onitsuka relationship formally collapsed into litigation. That parallel-track preparation is what kept the company alive through the rupture: by the time the legal fight over the Onitsuka relationship became public and adversarial, Blue Ribbon Sports already had a fallback identity it could operate under.

## Example from the book
The falling-out led to a lawsuit between Blue Ribbon Sports and Onitsuka, each side alleging the other had violated the terms of their relationship — terms that, per the earlier chapter, were never fully specified in the first place, which made the legal fight genuinely uncertain rather than a formality. Knight describes the period with real anxiety: a loss in court could have stripped the company of its remaining Tiger inventory rights and left it without a functioning shoe supply at all, at a moment when the Nike brand was not yet established enough to fully replace it. The company ultimately came through the litigation intact, but Knight's own account makes clear this outcome was not a foregone conclusion at the time it was happening.

## Practical application
The most useful move Knight's team made here wasn't in the courtroom — it was building the exit before they needed it. When a critical relationship shows any sign of strain, the moment to start building an independent alternative is before the relationship fully breaks, not after. If BRS had waited for the Onitsuka relationship to formally end before starting on its own brand, the company likely wouldn't have survived the gap. Where in your own work is a critical dependency showing early signs of strain that you're not yet treating as urgent?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> BRS survived its rupture with Onitsuka mainly because it had quietly started building an alternative before the relationship fully broke. Is there a dependency in your own work you can see cracking at the edges — and are you already building your fallback, or waiting until you're forced to?
