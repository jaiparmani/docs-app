# 04 — Naming the Company Nike

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
For its first decade, the company's fortunes were tied to a brand it didn't own: Tiger, Onitsuka's shoe line. As the relationship with Onitsuka grew strained, Knight and his small team realized they needed their own brand name and their own shoe line that didn't depend on a supplier who might not be a partner much longer. The name that stuck — Nike, after the Greek goddess of victory — didn't come from Knight, and didn't come from a branding process in any formal sense. It came from Jeff Johnson, BRS's first full-time employee, an obsessive, slightly eccentric former sales rep who ran the company's earliest customer correspondence and mail-order operation out of his own house.

## Why it matters
The naming episode is a small, concrete illustration of how much of BRS/Nike's identity was assembled under deadline pressure by whoever happened to be in the room, rather than planned out in advance. Knight himself was reportedly unenthusiastic about the name when it was proposed, and the company needed a name and a logo fast because shoes with the new branding needed to ship to a trade show. The swoosh logo, designed by a graphic design student, Carolyn Davidson, for a modest one-time fee, has a similarly improvised, unglamorous origin relative to how iconic it later became. Both moments matter because they undercut the tidy retrospective narrative that a great brand is the product of careful, foresighted brand strategy — this one was closer to an urgent, good-enough decision made under time pressure by people doing their best with what they had.

## Example from the book
Johnson later said the name came to him in a half-dream, and he passed it along to Knight along with several other candidates as the team scrambled to settle on something before a production deadline. Knight's own account is that he didn't love it at the time and only agreed because there wasn't a better option and time had run out — a far cry from the confident, deliberate brand launch the name's later ubiquity might suggest.

## Practical application
Not every important decision in a growing venture gets the deliberation it seems to deserve in hindsight — sometimes the name, the logo, the tagline, or the hire that ends up mattering most for decades is the one made in a rush, by whoever was closest to the problem, because a deadline forced a decision. The lesson isn't to be careless; it's to notice that waiting for the perfect, fully-deliberated version of a naming or branding decision can cost you more than making a good-enough choice under a real deadline and letting the market decide whether it was right.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> The name Nike survived not because everyone loved it, but because a deadline forced a choice and nobody had a better idea in time. Where in your own work are you stalling on a "good enough for now" decision, waiting for a certainty that a real deadline would force you past?
