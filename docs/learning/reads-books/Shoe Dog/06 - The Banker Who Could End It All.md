# 06 — The Banker Who Could End It All

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
For years, Blue Ribbon Sports's survival depended less on its customers than on the patience of a single lending relationship — first with Bank of California, later with First National Bank — and specifically on the judgment of individual loan officers who had the power to call the company's loans and effectively end it on any given visit. Knight describes returning again and again to the same tense meetings: presenting the company's growth as evidence it deserved more credit, while the bank saw the same growth as evidence of exactly the kind of overextension that precedes a default. The two sides were looking at identical numbers and reaching opposite conclusions about what they meant.

## Why it matters
This dynamic captures something true about growing a business on borrowed money that goes beyond BRS's specific situation: a lender's incentives are not the same as a founder's. Knight wanted the bank to see the company's trajectory and extend more credit to match it; the bank wanted assurance the company wouldn't fail before the existing loans were repaid, and rapid, undercapitalized growth looked to them like risk, not promise. Knight spent enormous energy — arguably more than he spent on product or marketing in some years — managing this one relationship, because losing it meant the company couldn't pay for its next shipment of shoes, which meant the company effectively stopped existing.

## Example from the book
Knight recounts specific, high-tension meetings where the bank threatened to cut off the company's credit line entirely, forcing him into the position of talking his own lender out of ending the business — not through a stronger balance sheet, which he didn't have, but through persuasion, relationship, and the sheer momentum of having survived the last several crises. The bank's skepticism wasn't irrational; by any conventional lending standard, BRS was a bad risk for most of this period. It survived several of these confrontations by margins that, in Knight's own retelling, feel closer to luck than to a strategy anyone could have designed on purpose.

## Practical application
If a single lender, investor, or client relationship has effectively become a veto power over your business's continued existence, that's worth naming clearly rather than treating as background risk. Diversifying that dependency — even slowly, even imperfectly — reduces the number of individual people whose mood or risk tolerance on a given afternoon can end something you've spent years building. Where in your own work does one relationship currently hold more power over your survival than you'd be comfortable admitting out loud?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Knight's company survived several meetings where a single banker could have ended it by simply deciding not to extend more credit. If you trace the relationships your own work depends on, is there one person or institution whose single decision could do comparable damage — and what would it take to loosen that dependency?
