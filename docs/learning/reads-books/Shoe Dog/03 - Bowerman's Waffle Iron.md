# 03 — Bowerman's Waffle Iron

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Bill Bowerman was Knight's track coach at the University of Oregon, a man with a decades-long, half-obsessive habit of taking apart his runners' shoes and rebuilding them to shave off fractions of an ounce or improve traction, convinced that shoe design could change race outcomes as much as training could. When Knight started importing Tiger shoes, he sent a pair to Bowerman not expecting much more than an old coach's opinion — and got back not just feedback but a business proposal: Bowerman wanted in, offering to keep experimenting with the shoes' design in exchange for a share of the company. He became a co-founder and, for years, BRS's unofficial and unpaid R&D department, working out of his own garage and kitchen in Oregon.

## Why it matters
Bowerman gave Blue Ribbon Sports something Knight alone could never have supplied: product credibility. Knight was a salesman and a numbers person; Bowerman was a genuine obsessive who tested prototypes on his own team's runners and treated every design as provisional. That combination — a founder who could sell and raise money, and a co-founder who could not stop tinkering with the actual product — is a big part of why BRS's shoes weren't just cheaper Japanese imports but a running line that elite American runners began to trust. It's a reminder that a company built by a single kind of founder is missing something.

## Example from the book
The best-known example is the waffle sole. Bowerman, looking for a better outsole tread pattern, poured rubber compound into his wife's waffle iron to see what the grid pattern would produce, ruining the appliance in the process. The resulting tread — the Waffle Trainer — became one of the defining products of Nike's early identity, directly traceable to a coach experimenting in his own kitchen rather than to any formal product-development process the company had built.

## Practical application
Bowerman's role suggests a specific kind of partnership worth seeking out deliberately: someone whose obsession with the underlying craft is genuinely independent of the business's growth targets, who will keep improving the actual thing regardless of what the sales numbers say that quarter. If your own venture has plenty of people thinking about growth, customers, and capital, but nobody who is simply obsessed with making the product itself better for its own sake, that's a gap worth naming and filling — even informally, even before you can pay for it properly.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Bowerman joined not for salary but for the chance to keep experimenting on a problem he already couldn't stop thinking about. Is there someone in your orbit with that kind of obsession over a problem adjacent to your work — and have you actually asked them to get involved, or just assumed they wouldn't be interested?
