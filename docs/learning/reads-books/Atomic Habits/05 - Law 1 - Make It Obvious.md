# 05 — Law 1: Make It Obvious

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The first law targets the cue stage of the loop. Two main tools: the **Habit Scorecard** (going through your day and simply labeling each existing habit as positive, negative, or neutral, without judgment, purely to build awareness of cues you currently act on unconsciously) and **implementation intentions** — a specific sentence structure from behavioral psychology, "I will [BEHAVIOR] at [TIME] in [LOCATION]," which converts a vague intention into a concrete, cued plan. Related to this is **habit stacking**: "After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]" — attaching a new habit to an existing one that already has a reliable cue, rather than trying to invent a brand-new trigger from scratch.

## Why it matters
Most failed habits aren't failures of desire, they're failures of specificity — "I'll exercise more" has no obvious cue attached to it, so it competes for attention with everything else happening in a given moment and usually loses. Clear cites research showing people who form implementation intentions are substantially more likely to follow through than people with the same goal stated vaguely. Habit stacking solves the deeper problem of where to even find a reliable cue: your existing habits already have well-worn cues (waking up, finishing coffee, sitting down at your desk) — you don't need a new trigger, you need to borrow one that already works.

## Example from the book
Clear tells the story of a study in a Dutch hospital where doctors dramatically increased hand sanitizer use not by lecturing staff on hygiene, but by moving the dispensers to more visible, obvious locations. He also describes his own habit stack for building a reading habit: "After I pour my cup of coffee each morning, I will read one page." The coffee-making ritual, already automatic, becomes the cue for the new behavior — no willpower required to remember, because the trigger is unmissable.

## Practical application
Write your own habit stack for one habit you want to build, using the exact formula: "After [a habit you already do every day, without fail], I will [new habit, kept small]." Then physically place any object the new habit needs (the book, the yoga mat, the water bottle) right next to the location where the existing habit happens, so the cue is visible, not just written down.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Run through your morning routine and name three habits you already do automatically, every single day, without exception. Which one has the strongest, most reliable cue — and what could you stack onto it?
