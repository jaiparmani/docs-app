# 09 — The Goldilocks Rule

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Once a habit is established, a new problem replaces the original one: boredom, not difficulty, becomes the main threat. Clear names this the **Goldilocks Rule**: humans experience peak motivation when working on a task that is neither too easy nor too hard, but right on the edge of their current ability — challenging enough to require real focus, achievable enough to stay believable. He pairs this with a related idea: the danger zone for a habit isn't when it's new and hard, it's later, once it's become "good enough" — the moment mastery starts to feel routine is precisely when people plateau, because staying at a comfortable, mastered level no longer requires or produces growth.

## Why it matters
This addresses a gap the other laws don't cover: the Four Laws are mostly about getting a habit started and making it stick in its first weeks, but they don't explain why people who've successfully built a habit sometimes lose it months later anyway. The Goldilocks Rule explains sustained engagement, not just initial adoption — a habit that stays at a fixed, unchanging difficulty eventually stops delivering the "flow"-like engagement that made it satisfying, and satisfaction (Law 4) quietly drains out even though nothing about the habit's structure has changed.

## Example from the book
Clear cites tennis players and video game designers as parallel cases of the same principle: professional tennis players report their most engaging matches are against opponents rated just slightly above their own skill level, not far above or far below; well-designed video games continuously recalibrate difficulty as a player improves, specifically to keep the challenge at that same edge. He contrasts this with what he calls the trap of "the danger of a plateau" — Olympic athletes and elite performers who keep improving specifically because they resist settling into "good enough," while most amateurs stop improving the moment a skill becomes merely competent, because competence itself removes the productive discomfort that was driving growth.

## Practical application
For a habit you've had for a while that's started to feel stale or is quietly slipping, don't ask "how do I force myself back to it" — ask whether it's actually gotten too easy relative to your current skill, and deliberately raise the difficulty by a small, specific notch (a slightly longer run, a harder book, a stricter version of the same rule) rather than either abandoning it or gutting through it unchanged.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a habit you used to enjoy but now find yourself avoiding even though it's easy for you now. Is it possible the habit didn't get harder to sustain — it got too easy to stay interesting?
