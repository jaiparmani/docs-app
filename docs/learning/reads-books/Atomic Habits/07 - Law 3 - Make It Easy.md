# 07 — Law 3: Make It Easy

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The third law targets the response stage, and Clear treats it as the most practically important of the four: what he calls the "Law of Least Effort" — humans are drawn to the option requiring the least work, so the real lever isn't motivation, it's friction. Reduce friction for habits you want (environment design: putting the guitar on a stand in the living room instead of in its case in a closet) and increase friction for habits you don't (removing the TV batteries, deleting an app). The signature tactic here is the **Two-Minute Rule**: scale any new habit down until it can be started in two minutes or less — "read before bed" becomes "read one page," "do yoga" becomes "take out my yoga mat."

## Why it matters
This directly attacks the most common reason ambitious habits fail: they're designed at the scale of the outcome you eventually want, not the scale of what you can reliably start on a bad day. A habit that requires real willpower to begin will get skipped on exactly the days motivation is lowest — which, over a year, is most days. A habit scaled down to two minutes has almost no activation barrier, and Clear's claim is that showing up is the actual habit being built; the two-minute version isn't a lesser version of the real habit, it's a "gateway habit" that, once started, frequently continues past two minutes anyway — but even when it doesn't, the identity vote (chunk 02) still counts.

## Example from the book
Clear describes an experiment by Dr. Anne Thorndike at Massachusetts General Hospital, where simply changing the layout of a hospital cafeteria — adding more water options at eye level and to easily accessible spots, without banning or restricting anything — measurably shifted what staff and visitors chose to drink, purely by reducing the friction to the desired choice below the friction to the old one. On the personal-habit side, he describes reducing his own habit of buying junk food by simply not keeping it in the house — not through willpower at the moment of temptation, but by engineering a decision made once (the grocery list) to eliminate hundreds of future decisions.

## Practical application
Take a habit you keep failing to start and rewrite it using the Two-Minute Rule until it sounds almost embarrassingly small — not "meditate for 20 minutes" but "sit down and take one breath." Separately, identify one piece of friction in your physical environment currently working against a habit you want (an item that's out of sight, an extra step required) and remove it today rather than relying on remembering to do the habit despite it.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick your most-abandoned habit. What is its two-minute version — and does the fact that it currently requires more than two minutes to even start explain more of the failure than "lack of motivation" ever did?
