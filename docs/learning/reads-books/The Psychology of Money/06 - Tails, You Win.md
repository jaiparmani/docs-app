# 06 — Tails, You Win

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
In investing, business, and much of life, a small number of events account for almost all of the results — everything else is closer to noise. Housel calls this "tail events," borrowing the term from the far ends, or tails, of a statistical distribution. The unsettling implication is that you can be wrong or mediocre most of the time and still come out far ahead overall, as long as you stay exposed to the rare outsized wins and don't let ordinary losses knock you out of the game before they arrive. Most of what determines long-run success isn't a high hit rate — it's still being in the room when the tail event happens.

## Why it matters
This reframes how you should judge both your own decisions and other people's track records. A venture capital fund, an art collector, or an individual stock picker can be "wrong" — meaning most individual picks lose money or go nowhere — and still be an enormous overall success, because the payoff from the few picks that go right dwarfs the losses from all the ones that didn't. Judging any individual decision by whether it personally worked out, rather than judging the whole strategy by whether it kept you exposed to enough attempts for a tail event to eventually land, leads to the wrong conclusions about what "good" decision-making even looks like in a world governed by long-tailed outcomes.

## Example from the book
Housel tells the story of Heinz Berggruen, a German-born art dealer who fled Nazi Germany and, over his life, amassed one of the most significant private art collections in the world, including major works by Picasso, Klee, Matisse, and Braque. Berggruen bought and sold thousands of pieces over his career, and by his own account most of them were mediocre investments or worse. But a small handful — the Picassos above all — became so extraordinarily valuable that they alone accounted for the majority of his collection's worth, more than compensating for everything else. Housel also points to the Walt Disney Company, which came close to bankruptcy more than once in its early decades, before the singular success of *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs* in 1937 provided the cash that let the entire studio survive and grow into what it became. In both cases, a small number of outcomes carried the entire enterprise.

## Practical application
Expect most of your individual bets — specific stocks, specific projects, specific investments of time or money — to be mediocre or outright wrong, and don't treat that as evidence your overall strategy is failing. What matters is staying diversified and staying in the game long enough that you remain exposed when a tail event does occur, rather than concentrating so heavily, or bailing out so early, that you're no longer in position to catch it. Judge your strategy by its long-run design, not by how any single recent decision turned out.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your life have you judged a decision as a failure just because that one instance didn't pay off, when the real question should have been whether the overall strategy kept you positioned for a rare big win?
