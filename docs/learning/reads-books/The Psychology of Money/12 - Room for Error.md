# 12 — Room for Error

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Housel's closing practical argument is that you should plan your finances around a range of possible outcomes, not the single outcome you expect to happen, because history is dominated by events nobody saw coming. He borrows the investing concept of "margin of safety" — building in a buffer between what you can afford to lose and what you're actually risking — and extends it to financial planning generally. The specific insight is that you can do everything right, be skilled and well-informed, and still get blindsided by something outside your model, so the room for error itself has to be treated as a required input, not an optional extra layered on afterward.

## Why it matters
This matters because most financial plans, and most individual predictions embedded in them, are quietly overconfident — they assume a specific range of future returns, a specific timeline, a specific set of circumstances, and leave little slack for being wrong about any of it. Housel's point is that being wrong, or unlucky, isn't a remote possibility to plan around only after it happens; it's close to guaranteed to happen to everyone eventually, in some form, over a long enough timeline. A plan without room for error isn't an aggressive plan, in his framing — it's a fragile one, because it depends on a level of accuracy about the future that nobody actually has, no matter how much expertise or data went into building it.

## Example from the book
Housel describes gamblers who count cards at blackjack — people who have a genuine, provable mathematical edge over the casino — and notes that the skilled ones still cap the size of any individual bet, refusing to wager an amount that could wipe them out even though the odds favor them. They understand that a real statistical edge is not the same thing as a guarantee on any specific hand, and a large enough losing streak, however statistically unlikely, could still end their game before the long-run edge has time to play out. Housel generalizes this into one of the book's more quotable lines: planning is important, but the most important part of any plan is planning for the plan not going as expected. He treats this as true even for investors and businesses with a real, demonstrable advantage, precisely because an advantage that isn't paired with room for error can still be destroyed by a single bad break.

## Practical application
When you build a financial plan — how much to invest, how much debt to take on, how large an emergency fund to hold — deliberately build in a buffer beyond what your best-guess forecast says you need, sized specifically for the scenario where your assumptions turn out to be wrong. This isn't pessimism; it's an acknowledgment that even a well-reasoned plan is a bet on an unknowable future, and the margin of safety is what lets you survive being wrong about it long enough to keep compounding working in your favor.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Look at your current financial plan and find the single assumption it depends on most heavily — what happens to the whole plan if that one assumption turns out to be wrong, and how much room for error have you actually built around it?
