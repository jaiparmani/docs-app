# 03 — Never Enough

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Housel opens this chapter with a story about Kurt Vonnegut telling fellow writer Joseph Heller, at a party thrown by a billionaire hedge fund manager, that their host had made more money in a single day than Heller's novel *Catch-22* had earned in its entire history. Heller's reply was that he had something the billionaire would never have: enough. That word — enough — is the chapter's whole argument. The specific danger Housel is pointing to isn't wanting more money; it's the tendency for "more" to have no natural stopping point once you're already comfortable, so the goalposts keep sliding outward faster than your gains do.

## Why it matters
Most financial ruin doesn't come from people who have too little. It comes from people who have a lot and risk it chasing more than they need, because they never defined what "enough" actually meant for them. Without a fixed target, every gain just resets the baseline — the lifestyle, the peer group, and the ambition all creep upward together, so the feeling of not-quite-there never goes away no matter how much accumulates. That's a dangerous setup, because it pushes people to take on risks that make no sense for someone who is already secure, purely to close a gap that was never going to close on its own.

## Example from the book
Housel tells the story of Rajat Gupta, who rose from an orphaned childhood in India to become managing director of McKinsey and sit on the boards of Goldman Sachs and Procter & Gamble — by any reasonable measure, an extraordinarily wealthy and accomplished man. Yet Gupta was convicted of passing insider information to hedge fund manager Raj Rajaratnam, seemingly in pursuit of turning his existing fortune into a billion-dollar one. He risked a reputation and a freedom that had taken a lifetime to build for a category of wealth he did not need and, once caught, permanently lost everything he already had. Housel's point isn't that Gupta was uniquely greedy — it's that "enough" is a genuinely hard thing to define, and without defining it, more always looks worth reaching for, even at absurd risk.

## Practical application
Try to name, concretely, what "enough" looks like for you — not as a permanent number frozen for life, but as a real answer you could give right now if asked. The exercise matters less for the specific figure than for the discipline of having one, because it gives you something to check new risks against. When a decision would put your current security at risk to chase a gain past that number, that's a signal to slow down, not speed up. The specific danger isn't ambition — it's forgetting that the fastest way to lose what you have is risking it for something you don't need.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you had to state, right now, a concrete definition of "enough" for your own life, what would it be — and does anything you're currently chasing risk what you already have to get past it?
