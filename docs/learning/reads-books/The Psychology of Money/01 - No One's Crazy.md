# 01 — No One's Crazy

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
People make financial decisions based on their own unique experience of the world — the years they were born into, the economy they came of age in, what their parents did or didn't have, what worked and what blew up in their own lives. Housel's opening move is to insist that this makes even wildly different money behaviors *reasonable* from the inside, even when they look crazy from the outside. Someone who lived through the Great Depression and someone who came of age during a decade-long bull market are not equally informed people who reached different conclusions — they are two people who've seen genuinely different worlds, and their financial instincts are calibrated to the one they actually lived through, not to some objective universal reality.

## Why it matters
It's tempting to look at someone else's money decisions — refusing to invest, buying lottery tickets, over-insuring, keeping cash under a mattress — and conclude they're financially illiterate. Housel's point is that this is usually the wrong diagnosis. The decision is very often a rational response to a lived history you don't share. This matters practically because it changes how you evaluate your own decisions too: your instincts about risk, debt, and saving were shaped by a specific slice of experience, not derived from first principles. Recognizing that lets you ask whether your own financial reflexes still fit your current situation, rather than treating them as self-evidently correct just because they're yours.

## Example from the book
Housel points out that people who grew up in poverty are statistically more likely to play the lottery, a behavior that looks financially irrational to a comfortable outside observer. But he argues it reads differently once you account for what a small, affordable chance at a dramatically better life is worth to someone for whom slow, steady accumulation seems structurally out of reach. He also notes that whether someone believes stocks are a good investment often correlates less with their intelligence or financial education than with whether the market was rising or crashing during the specific years they first started paying attention to it — the 1970s stagflation investor and the 1990s bull-market investor absorbed two entirely different "truths" about what the stock market is.

## Practical application
Before judging a money decision — someone else's or your own — as objectively "wrong," ask what specific experience could make it feel reasonable. Then check whether the belief driving your own habits (a fear of debt, an aversion to the stock market, a compulsion to keep a large cash cushion) was formed by a specific event in your history rather than by a general principle. If it was, it's worth deliberately re-examining whether that belief still applies to the world and the goals you have now.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's one financial habit of yours that would look irrational to someone with a completely different money history — and can you trace it back to the specific experience that made it feel reasonable to you?
