# 11 — Reasonable Over Rational

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Housel argues you should optimize your financial decisions for what you can actually stick with for decades, not for what a spreadsheet says is mathematically optimal. A strategy that is theoretically perfect but that you'll abandon in a panic during the first bad year is worse, in practice, than a strategy that's merely reasonable but that you can hold onto through every market cycle you'll live through. Since consistency over time is what makes compounding work, the plan that keeps you calm and committed will usually beat the plan that's technically superior on paper but psychologically unsustainable.

## Why it matters
Most financial advice is built around what a purely rational, emotionless actor would do — maximize leverage when debt is cheap, hold zero excess cash because it drags on returns, sell whichever asset class the math currently favors. Housel's point is that no real person is a purely rational actor, and pretending otherwise when building a financial plan sets people up to abandon that plan exactly when it matters most, during periods of fear or uncertainty. A plan needs to account for the fact that its user has emotions, limited attention, and a breaking point — designing around that reality isn't a compromise on rigor, it's what makes the plan durable enough to actually work over the timeframes where financial outcomes are decided.

## Example from the book
Housel describes his own family's financial choices as deliberately "unreasonable" by strict optimization standards: he and his wife own their home outright with no mortgage, despite historically low borrowing costs making leverage mathematically attractive, and they hold a larger cash position than a purely rational optimizer investing every spare dollar in the market would recommend. He's explicit that this isn't the mathematically optimal choice under most models of expected returns. But it lets them sleep at night, avoid forced decisions during downturns, and stay the course through market cycles without the specific kind of financial stress that causes people to abandon otherwise sound long-term plans. The "cost" of the suboptimal choice, in his telling, is more than repaid by the behavioral consistency it buys.

## Practical application
When comparing two financial strategies, don't stop at which one wins on a spreadsheet — ask honestly which one you can actually maintain through a serious downturn, a job loss, or a decade of disappointing returns, without abandoning it. A slightly-less-optimal plan you'll actually follow for thirty years will outperform a theoretically superior plan you follow for three years and then panic out of. Building in features that cost you a little expected return — extra cash, a paid-off home, a simpler portfolio you understand and trust — can be a rational trade once you account for your own psychology as part of the equation.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a part of your financial plan that's technically optimal but that you secretly don't fully trust yourself to stick with under stress — and what would a "reasonable" version of it look like instead?
