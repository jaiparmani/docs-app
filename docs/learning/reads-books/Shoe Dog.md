# Shoe Dog

<small>4 min read</small>

*Phil Knight*

Nike's founding story, as Knight tells it, is not a strategy — it's a series of near-fatal cash crises survived largely through improvisation, personal relationships, and timing that could easily have broken the other way. The book opens with Knight, fresh out of Stanford business school, deciding on a whim during a round-the-world trip that Japanese running shoes could undercut the German brands (Adidas and Puma) that dominated the US market, and it tracks the company he built on that hunch — first as Blue Ribbon Sports, an importer with no capital and a business plan Knight himself later describes as barely a plan — through its transformation into Nike and its 1980 IPO. What makes the book worth reading as more than a corporate origin myth is how much of the middle two hundred pages are not about growth at all, but about the company almost dying, repeatedly, of the same problem: it grew faster than its banking relationships could fund.

## The banking crisis is the actual plot

Most founder memoirs treat the near-death moments as a single dramatic low point before the triumphant turn. *Shoe Dog* instead treats undercapitalization as the persistent condition of the business for most of a decade. Blue Ribbon Sports kept outrunning its line of credit because every dollar of profit was immediately plowed into the next shipment of shoes, and Knight spends long stretches of the book essentially begging, renegotiating, and bluffing his way past bankers who periodically threaten to call the loans and end the company outright. A Japanese trading company, Nissho Iwai, ends up functioning as an unlikely lifeline more than once. This is the part of the book that distinguishes it from generic startup hagiography: it's candid that the company's survival through its first decade was not a matter of a founder executing a vision under control, but of a founder running perpetually short of cash and getting bailed out by relationships and luck often enough that the story didn't end early.

That candor is the book's real value. Most retrospective founder accounts smooth the near-misses into foreshadowing — the crisis becomes a "test" the founder "passes" on the way to an outcome the reader already knows happened. Knight doesn't fully resist that gravitational pull (he's writing forty years later, knowing exactly how it ends, and the prose occasionally has the shape of a man building a legend), but he's unusually willing to let the story sit in moments where the company's failure looks more likely than its success — the falling-out with the original Japanese shoe supplier Onitsuka Tiger, the lawsuit that followed, the repeated scrapes with the bank that a slightly worse quarter would have ended badly.

## Luck versus strategy

The book is also more honest than most about how much of Nike's early trajectory was contingent rather than strategized. The relationship with Bill Bowerman — Knight's former track coach, who became a co-founder and started experimenting with shoe soles in his own garage, eventually producing the waffle sole — reads less like a deliberate hiring decision and more like a fortunate accident of Knight's own biography as a college runner. The decision to import Japanese shoes in the first place came out of a business school paper and a trip, not market research. Employees who became central to the company's identity were hired in ways Knight describes as essentially ad hoc. None of this is presented as false modesty; it's presented straightforwardly as what happened, and it complicates any reading of the book as a transferable playbook. If there's a strategic throughline, it's closer to "keep moving and stay solvent long enough for the timing to work" than any specific insight about shoes, marketing, or Japan.

## What doesn't quite land

The book thins noticeably in its final stretch, where the company has scaled past the point where Knight was in every room — the Michael Jordan deal and the IPO get comparatively brisk treatment relative to the exhaustive account of the early cash crunches, which suggests the material Knight found most worth dwelling on, decades later, wasn't the triumph but the struggle. That's a defensible authorial choice but it does mean the book is better as an account of how a company almost doesn't survive than as an account of how it wins. Read as the former — a specific, well-told story about undercapitalization, improvisation, and the outsized role of luck and timing in outcomes usually narrated as inevitable — it holds up considerably better than the genre it nominally belongs to.
