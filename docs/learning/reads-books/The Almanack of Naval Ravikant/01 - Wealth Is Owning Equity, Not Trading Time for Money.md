# 01 — Wealth Is Owning Equity, Not Trading Time for Money

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Naval's starting distinction is between two fundamentally different games: renting out your time for a wage, and owning a piece of something that keeps producing value whether or not you personally show up. A salary or an hourly rate has a hard ceiling — there are only so many hours in a day, and someone else is usually the one setting the price of your hour. Equity has no such ceiling, because it isn't paying you for your hours, it's paying you for a share of everything the underlying thing produces, forever, whether you're asleep, on vacation, or dead. Getting wealthy, in this framing, isn't a matter of working harder at a job. It's a matter of ending up on the ownership side of that divide instead of the labor side.

## Why it matters
This collapses a lot of conventional career advice. "Get promoted," "ask for a raise," "find a higher-paying job" are all still moves within the wage game — they raise the ceiling slightly without removing it. A much higher-leverage move is to trade some amount of guaranteed salary for a small equity stake in something that might compound, because compounding equity has no theoretical limit and a maxed-out salary does. It reframes the central career question from "what's my hourly-equivalent pay" to "what, if anything, do I own here, and does it grow without me."

## Example from the book
Naval draws heavily on his own trajectory through Silicon Valley to make this concrete: years spent as a salaried employee didn't move the needle nearly as much as the handful of small equity positions he held — in companies he co-founded, like Epinions, and later in the dozens of startups he angel-invested in through AngelList. He's candid that most of those individual bets didn't pay off; the wealth came from the structure of owning many small equity stakes rather than from any single stroke of genius, and none of it would have compounded if the underlying arrangement had been an hourly rate instead of ownership.

## Practical application
List your current sources of income and, for each one, ask a blunt question: does this keep paying or appreciating if I stop showing up tomorrow? Most salaried work will fail that test completely. The exercise isn't meant to make you quit your job — it's meant to locate where, if anywhere, you currently have equity exposure, and to make you notice when an opportunity to trade a little cash compensation for a little ownership crosses your path (in a job offer's equity component, a friend's early-stage company, a side project you could structure as a business rather than freelance hours). Small, real equity stakes taken early and often are the mechanism, not one big lucky bet.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If every one of your current income streams stopped the moment you stopped actively working, what is the smallest, most realistic step you could take this year toward owning something instead of renting out your hours?
