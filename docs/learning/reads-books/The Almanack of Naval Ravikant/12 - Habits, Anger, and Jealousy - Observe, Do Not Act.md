# 12 — Habits, Anger, and Jealousy — Observe, Do Not Act

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book's closing move on the happiness side applies the meditation-trained gap from the previous chunk to three specific things Naval singles out as worth deliberately not acting on: habits, anger, and jealousy. His claim isn't that these states are wrong to feel — he treats them as more or less automatic, arising whether you want them to or not — but that there's a real choice available in the moment between them, which is whether you act on the state or simply observe it and let it pass. A habit noticed but not indulged loses a little of its grip each time; anger noticed but not acted on burns out on its own within a fairly short window; jealousy noticed and named for what it is (a signal about your own unexamined desires, more than a fact about the other person) stops driving behavior it would otherwise drive.

## Why it matters
This closes the loop on the entire happiness section of the book: desire creates unhappiness, wanting what you have counters it, meditation creates the gap needed to notice states as they arise rather than being swept along by them — and this final piece is where that gap gets applied to the specific states that do the most damage when acted on impulsively. Most conflict, most self-sabotage through bad habits, and much of interpersonal damage from jealousy comes not from having the feeling but from immediately converting the feeling into action or speech, without the pause that would have let the feeling burn through on its own.

## Example from the book
Naval describes treating his own anger, in the moment it arises, as something to simply let run its course internally without translating it into a word or an action — noting that anger which isn't fed by continued rumination or expression tends to dissipate within minutes, while anger that gets spoken or acted on tends to escalate and compound. He applies the same lens to jealousy, describing it as one of the more useful negative emotions precisely because it points, if you're willing to look, at a desire you're not being honest with yourself about — the discomfort is information, not just noise to suppress or a verdict on the other person.

## Practical application
The next time anger, jealousy, or a bad habitual urge arises, practice a short, deliberate pause before any action or speech — long enough to notice the state as a state rather than as an instruction. For anger specifically, notice whether it's still present a few minutes later without you having said or done anything; for jealousy, ask what desire of your own it's actually pointing at rather than what it seems to be saying about the other person; for habits, notice the urge and let it pass once without acting on it, which weakens its automatic pull the next time it arises.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of the last time anger or jealousy drove you to say or do something. If you'd paused for five minutes first and just observed the feeling, what, if anything, would you have actually done differently?
