# 07 — The Wealth Formula — Specific Knowledge, Accountability, and Leverage

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Naval closes the wealth track of the book by combining the previous ideas into a single working formula rather than leaving them as separate observations: find specific knowledge (the expertise only you could have assembled, because it grew out of genuine curiosity rather than credentialing), pair it with accountability (putting your own name and reputation directly on the outcome, rather than hiding inside a committee or a diffuse team where no one owns the result), and apply leverage (code, media, capital, or labor) to multiply what that accountable, specific expertise can produce. Any one of the three alone is limited — specific knowledge without leverage stays small, leverage without specific knowledge multiplies mediocrity, and neither without accountability rarely attracts the trust needed to access real leverage in the first place.

## Why it matters
This formula is meant as a diagnostic, not just a description. If wealth isn't compounding for you, the formula gives you three places to check rather than one vague verdict of "try harder": is what you're offering actually specific to you, or something anyone with similar credentials could offer instead? Are you personally accountable for the outcome, in a way that's visible and attributable to you, or are you diffusing responsibility across a team or employer? And are you applying any leverage at all, or is your output capped at exactly what your own two hands can produce in a day? Naval's claim is that most stalled careers are missing one specific leg of this stool, not all three.

## Example from the book
Naval points to the entrepreneurs and public figures he admires as instances of the formula stacked together deliberately: a founder with genuine domain expertise (specific knowledge) who puts their name and public credibility behind the product (accountability), then builds it as software that scales without additional labor (leverage). He contrasts this with the more common but weaker combination — accountable, hardworking people producing entirely generic output with no leverage attached, which explains effort without explaining outsized returns. He's equally skeptical of the opposite failure mode: leverage applied with no accountability behind it, which he associates with the kind of anonymous, reputation-free operators who can move fast precisely because they have nothing at stake if the outcome is bad.

## Practical application
Take whatever you're currently working on and run it through the three legs explicitly. Name the specific knowledge involved — if you can't name anything that isn't broadly available to competitors, that's the gap to close first. Check whether your name and reputation are actually attached to the outcome, or whether you could quietly disappear from the project without anyone's assessment of you changing. Then check whether the work is capped by your personal hours or multiplied by some form of leverage. Wherever the formula is thinnest is usually the highest-leverage place to invest your next block of effort.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Of the three legs — specific knowledge, accountability, leverage — which one is weakest in your current work, and what's stopping you from strengthening it?
