# 06 — Play Long-Term Games With Long-Term People

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Naval's advice for compounding trust, reputation, and opportunity over a career is structural rather than moral: choose to play repeated games with people who are also playing repeated games, and avoid one-shot transactional relationships wherever you can. In a single-shot interaction, the rational move is often to extract maximum value and walk away, because there's no future relationship to protect. In a long-term relationship, cooperation becomes the rational move on both sides, because reputation and trust compound the same way capital does — every honest interaction is a small deposit that makes the next one easier and higher-stakes.

## Why it matters
This reframes who's actually worth doing business or building relationships with. It's tempting to chase whoever offers the best terms on any single deal, but Naval's point is that the compounding value of a relationship where both sides expect to keep dealing with each other for years or decades usually outweighs the marginal advantage of a better one-time term. It also explains why he's skeptical of environments that reward short-term extraction — high-churn sales roles, adversarial negotiations, anything structured so both sides know the relationship ends after this transaction — because those environments select for exactly the behavior that destroys compounding trust.

## Example from the book
Naval frequently returns to Warren Buffett as the clearest public example of this principle in practice: Buffett's reputation for fair dealing over decades is itself an asset, because it means counterparties come to him first, on better terms, precisely because they trust the relationship won't be exploited for a one-time gain. Naval extends the same logic to his own approach to angel investing through AngelList, arguing that a founder's early trust in an investor who behaves well in a small deal is what earns access to that founder's much bigger opportunities years later. He's also candid that this only works if both sides genuinely expect the relationship to continue — playing nice once with someone you never intend to deal with again isn't the long game, it's just a slower version of the short one.

## Practical application
When evaluating a new relationship — a business partner, a client, an employer — ask explicitly whether this is structured as a repeated game or a one-shot transaction, and let that assessment shape how much you optimize for the immediate terms versus the long-run relationship. Where you have the choice, actively favor situations with long time horizons and exit slowly, if at all, from relationships where both sides are compounding trust; the marginal short-term gain from squeezing a one-time advantage rarely outweighs what the same effort would build in a longer relationship.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Look at your current key relationships — business or personal. Which ones are you unconsciously treating as one-shot transactions, and what would change about how you show up in them if you treated them as decades-long repeated games instead?
