# 05 — Judgment Is the Scarce Skill That Compounds

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Once leverage is cheap and abundant — which Naval argues is now true, since code and media let almost anyone multiply their output for free — the bottleneck stops being access to leverage and becomes the quality of the decisions you apply it to. Bad judgment amplified by leverage doesn't just fail quietly, it fails at scale, publicly and quickly. Good judgment amplified by leverage compounds in the other direction. So the scarce, valuable skill in a world of cheap leverage isn't the ability to work hard or the ability to acquire more leverage — it's the accumulated pattern-recognition and decision-making ability that tells you which bets, products, or partnerships are actually worth applying that leverage to.

## Why it matters
This shifts where effort should go as leverage becomes more accessible to everyone. If code and media make it trivially easy for anyone to build and distribute something, then the differentiator between two people with equal access to that leverage is entirely in the choices they make about what to build and who to work with. It also explains why judgment isn't something you can shortcut by working more hours — it's built the slow way, through accumulated exposure to decisions and their consequences, which is why Naval treats reading widely and thinking in first principles as direct inputs into judgment rather than as separate hobbies from the business of getting wealthy.

## Example from the book
Naval describes judgment as something closer to applied philosophy than to business acumen — he credits long, broad reading (not narrowly in business or technology, but in philosophy, science, and history) as the actual training ground for the pattern recognition he applies to investment decisions. His argument is that most investors and founders overweight domain-specific knowledge and underweight the more general capacity to reason clearly about incentives, probabilities, and human behavior, which is the capacity that actually transfers across situations he's never seen before.

## Practical application
Rather than optimizing purely for acquiring more leverage (more capital, more followers, more code shipped), build a habit of deliberately reviewing your own past decisions and their outcomes — where your judgment was right, where it was wrong, and what pattern you can extract from each. Pair this with reading broadly outside your immediate field of expertise; Naval's claim is that judgment is trained less by depth in one domain than by the ability to import mental models from many domains into whatever decision is currently in front of you. Treat each decision review as a small deposit into the same account, the same way a specific-knowledge skill accumulates — no single review teaches much, but the compounding pattern recognition across dozens of them is where the actual edge comes from.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a recent decision that turned out badly. Was the failure in the judgment behind the decision, or in the execution of a decision that was actually sound — and how would you tell the difference next time?
