# 01 — The Risk Portfolio

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Grant opens the book by dismantling the image of the original as a fearless, all-in risk-taker. In his telling, the people who actually launch successful novel ventures tend to be risk-averse in most areas of their lives — they just take a calculated risk in one domain while deliberately keeping everything else stable. He calls this a **balanced risk portfolio**: hedge where you can, so you can afford to take the one risk that matters.

## Why it matters
The startup-hero myth — quit your job, burn the boats, bet everything on the idea — makes hedging look like a lack of conviction. Grant's point is closer to the opposite: hedging is often what buys an original the time and financial runway to actually get an idea right, rather than being forced by desperation into whatever version of the idea can be built and shipped fastest. Removing unnecessary risk from the rest of your life doesn't dilute your original bet; it protects your ability to keep making it.

## Example from the book
Grant opens with the founders of Warby Parker — Neil Blumenthal, Dave Gilboa, Andrew Hunt, and Jeff Raider — who built the company as a side project while still in business school. Rather than dropping everything to chase it full time, they kept pursuing internships and job offers as a fallback, only letting go of that safety net once the business had already shown it could work. Grant pairs this story with research on so-called "hybrid entrepreneurs" — people who start a venture while keeping their day job rather than quitting immediately — finding that this group was substantially less likely to fail than founders who went all in from day one. The day job wasn't a distraction from the original idea; it was what let the founders wait for good evidence before making the leap.

## Practical application
Before treating an all-in bet as the only "serious" way to pursue an idea, look for what you can keep stable while you test it — a day job, a savings cushion, an existing client base, a part-time schedule. The goal isn't to avoid risk altogether; it's to concentrate your risk-taking in the one place it needs to be (the idea itself) while hedging everywhere else, so a slow start doesn't force you into a worse version of the idea just to survive.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there an idea you've been treating as an all-or-nothing bet — one where "half measures" feel like they don't count? What would it look like to hedge everything around that idea so you could take the actual risk on better terms?
