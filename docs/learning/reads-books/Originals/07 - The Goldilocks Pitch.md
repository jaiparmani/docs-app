# 07 — The Goldilocks Pitch

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
When pitching a novel idea to someone who has the power to say no — a boss, an investor, a client — presenting a single option and asking them to approve it invites resistance, because saying yes means being sold to. Grant argues that offering a small set of options instead, including at least one weaker alternative, changes the dynamic: the audience gets to compare and choose, which makes the choice feel like theirs rather than something pushed on them.

## Why it matters
People are more resistant to being persuaded than to persuading themselves. A single pitch forces a binary yes-or-no decision on someone else's idea; a set of options turns the same decision into an act of judgment the evaluator performs themselves. The idea doesn't have to change to be received differently — the frame it's delivered in does.

## Example from the book
Grant describes how originals who are effective at getting buy-in from skeptical decision-makers rarely walk in with only their favorite idea. Instead, they present several options at once — including versions they know are weaker — so the person being pitched has a genuine comparison to make rather than a single proposal to accept or reject outright. The strongest idea, evaluated alongside visibly weaker alternatives, tends to look better by contrast than it would standing alone, and the decision-maker leaves feeling like they picked the winner rather than being talked into it.

## Practical application
The next time you need buy-in on an idea from someone above you, resist presenting only your best option. Bring two or three, framed honestly, and let the comparison do the persuading rather than your advocacy for a single choice.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a time you pitched a single idea and got resistance. Would offering a real alternative alongside it — even a weaker one — have changed how the decision felt to the person you were pitching?
