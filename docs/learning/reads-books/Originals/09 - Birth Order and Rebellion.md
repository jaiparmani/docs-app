# 09 — Birth Order and Rebellion

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Grant brings in research suggesting that where you fall in your family's birth order shapes how comfortable you are challenging the status quo later in life. Firstborns tend to identify with existing authority and rules, since they were the ones who had that authority modeled directly onto them first; later-born children, competing for a different niche within the family, are statistically more likely to become the ones who question or break from convention.

## Why it matters
This reframes rebelliousness as something shaped by structural position, not just personality. If the willingness to challenge convention is partly a byproduct of where someone had to find their footing relative to siblings or an existing hierarchy, then "who's naturally original" is less fixed than it seems — role and position, not just temperament, predict who ends up pushing back.

## Example from the book
Grant draws on psychologist Frank Sulloway's research into birth order and risk-taking, including a striking finding from professional baseball: later-born major league players attempted the high-risk act of stealing a base considerably more often than firstborn players did, holding other factors constant. Sulloway's broader research extends this into political history, finding that later-born historical figures were disproportionately represented among supporters of revolutionary movements, compared to firstborns who more often defended the existing order. The explanation Sulloway offers is that firstborns have an early, uncontested claim to their parents' expectations and authority, while later-borns have to find a different way to stand out — and that different way often means being willing to go against the grain.

## Practical application
When you're trying to build a team or a family culture that welcomes original thinking, notice how a person's position in an existing hierarchy — not just their personality — might be shaping their appetite for challenging it, and make deliberate room for the people whose structural position gives them the least natural incentive to speak up.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your life has your position in a hierarchy — birth order, seniority, tenure — made it easier or harder for you to challenge the status quo, independent of how original your ideas actually were?
