# 04 — Strategic Procrastination

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Grant makes a case that procrastination — usually treated as a pure productivity failure — can be a deliberate tool for originality. Finishing something early forecloses the divergent thinking that happens while a problem sits unresolved in the back of your mind. Delay, used on purpose rather than out of avoidance, leaves room for better ideas to surface before you lock in a first draft.

## Why it matters
This complicates the standard advice to start early and finish ahead of schedule. If early completion means committing to your first workable idea before better ones have had time to occur to you, then "efficient" execution can quietly cap how original the final result is allowed to be. The book's point isn't that procrastination is good in general — it's that some amount of unresolved incubation time is a precondition for genuinely new ideas, not just a personal failing to be optimized away.

## Example from the book
Grant's central illustration is Martin Luther King Jr.'s preparation for the "I Have a Dream" speech. King was still reworking the speech late into the night before delivering it, and the line that became its most famous — "I have a dream" — wasn't in his prepared text at all. It surfaced only when Mahalia Jackson, who had heard him use similar language before, called out from the crowd to "tell them about the dream," and King departed from his script in the moment. Because he hadn't locked the speech down early, he had the latitude to incorporate something better than what he'd planned to say. Grant pairs this with his own research: students instructed to procrastinate on a task before starting it generated ideas that were rated as more original than those generated by students who began immediately.

## Practical application
For work that depends on originality rather than just execution, deliberately build in a gap between when you could start and when you actually do — sketch the outline, then let it sit before writing the final version, rather than moving straight from first draft to final draft.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a piece of work you finished "early" recently that might have benefited from more time sitting unresolved — and what did locking it in early cost you the chance to discover?
