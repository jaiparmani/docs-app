# 08 — Rethinking Groupthink

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Organizations often try to guard against groupthink by formally assigning someone to play devil's advocate. Grant argues this is a weaker safeguard than it looks — an assigned dissenter, arguing a position they don't actually hold, doesn't produce the same benefit as authentic disagreement from someone who genuinely sees it differently. The value of dissent comes from its being real, not from its being present.

## Why it matters
This distinction matters because it's easy for an organization to feel protected against groupthink simply by having a devil's-advocate ritual, while actually remaining just as vulnerable to it. If assigned dissent doesn't do the real work, then building a culture where people are willing to voice genuine disagreement — even when it's uncomfortable — is doing something a rotating "official skeptic" role can't substitute for.

## Example from the book
Grant draws on research by psychologist Charlan Nemeth comparing groups exposed to authentic dissent against groups given an assigned devil's advocate arguing a position for the sake of argument. The groups exposed to real disagreement made better decisions and generated more original thinking than the groups with an assigned dissenter, even when the content of what was argued was similar — the difference was that the other group members could tell the dissent wasn't sincere, and discounted it accordingly. Grant connects this to the danger of unusually strong, cohesive organizational cultures, where the pressure toward consensus can be so effective that people stop voicing real disagreement at all, leaving no one to catch a bad decision before it ships.

## Practical application
Instead of formalizing a devil's-advocate role that everyone knows isn't sincere, look for the person on your team who actually disagrees with the current plan, and make it clear their disagreement is welcome. A single genuine skeptic is worth more than a scheduled ritual of pretend dissent.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Does your team have a real mechanism for someone to voice authentic disagreement — or does it have a version that looks like one, like an assigned devil's advocate, without doing the actual work?
