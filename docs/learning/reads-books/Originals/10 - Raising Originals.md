# 10 — Raising Originals

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Grant turns to research on parenting and finds a consistent pattern: parents of unusually original or creative children tend to enforce far fewer explicit rules than average, relying instead on explaining the values and reasoning behind expectations rather than issuing fixed commands for every situation. Fewer rules, more explained values — not stricter discipline — correlates with children who grow up willing to think and act originally.

## Why it matters
This runs against the instinct that discipline and structure are what produce well-adjusted, capable kids. Grant's point isn't that rules are bad, but that a household — or by extension a team or organization — built primarily around rule enforcement teaches compliance, while one built around explained values teaches people to reason for themselves about what's right in situations no rule anticipated. Original thinking requires practice reasoning from principles, not just practice following instructions.

## Example from the book
Grant cites research comparing the home environments of unusually creative or original children and adults against less creative peers, finding that the households of the more original group had markedly fewer explicit household rules. Where rules were far more common in less creative households, the families of original children instead spent more time discussing the reasoning behind values — why an action mattered, who it affected, what it said about the kind of person you wanted to be — rather than simply stating what was or wasn't allowed. The children weren't raised with less structure exactly; they were raised with structure built around explained principles instead of enumerated rules.

## Practical application
When setting expectations for kids, or for that matter for a team, notice how much of what you communicate is a rule ("don't do X") versus a value with its reasoning attached ("here's why X matters and who it affects"). Where you can, replace a rule with the value underneath it, and let the person reason their own way to the right call in situations you didn't anticipate.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a rule you enforce, at home or at work, mostly out of habit. What's the value underneath it — and would explaining that value instead of the rule change how the people you're guiding actually think?
