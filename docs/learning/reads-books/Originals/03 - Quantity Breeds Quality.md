# 03 — Quantity Breeds Quality

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Grant's argument here is a direct challenge to the idea that great creators have some internal filter that lets them go straight to their best work. Instead, the evidence points the other way: the surest route to producing a few great ideas is producing a large number of ideas, most of which will be mediocre or outright bad. Quality is a numbers game, not a talent that bypasses the numbers.

## Why it matters
This reframes "having good instincts" as mostly a byproduct of volume rather than a prerequisite for it. If quantity and quality rise together, then holding back ideas until you're confident they're good is exactly backwards — the confidence, if it ever comes, comes from having produced enough material to know what your good ideas even look like relative to your average ones.

## Example from the book
Grant points to some of history's most celebrated creators to make the case. Beethoven composed hundreds of works over his career, the overwhelming majority now forgotten, alongside the small number that became canonical. Picasso's total output — paintings, sculptures, prints, ceramics — runs into the thousands, with only a handful, like *Guernica*, reaching masterpiece status. Edison held over a thousand patents, nearly all of them commercially irrelevant, and is remembered chiefly for a few that changed the world. In each case, the ratio of hits to total output stayed roughly constant — these weren't creators who somehow avoided producing bad work, they simply produced so much work that the bad ideas were outnumbered by the good ones in absolute terms.

## Practical application
When you're in an idea-generation phase, treat volume as the metric that matters, not polish. Resist the urge to perfect your third idea before generating a tenth; the tenth, or the fortieth, is statistically more likely to be the one worth keeping than any single idea you obsess over in isolation.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your work are you currently trying to identify your "best" idea before you've generated enough ideas to actually know what good looks like for you?
