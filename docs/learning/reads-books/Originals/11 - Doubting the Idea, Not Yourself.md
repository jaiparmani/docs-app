# 11 — Doubting the Idea, Not Yourself

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Grant closes on a distinction that runs through the whole book: doubt isn't a single thing, and the two kinds pull in opposite directions. Doubting your idea — wondering if this specific version of it is right — drives useful revision and testing. Doubting yourself — wondering if you're the kind of person capable of pulling it off at all — tends to produce paralysis instead. The originals who keep going aren't the ones who feel no doubt; they're the ones who've located their doubt correctly.

## Why it matters
Most advice about originality treats doubt and fear as obstacles to be eliminated. Grant's reframe is more useful precisely because it doesn't ask you to feel less doubtful — it asks you to notice which kind of doubt you're feeling. Doubt about the idea is data you can act on: test it, revise it, get outside opinions on it. Doubt about yourself doesn't point anywhere productive; it just erodes the will to keep testing at all. Conflating the two means self-doubt can masquerade as prudent skepticism about the idea, stalling you under the cover of looking careful.

## Example from the book
Grant offers a self-deprecating example from his own experience: he was given an early opportunity to invest in Warby Parker and passed on it, unconvinced that an online eyewear company could work. Looking back, he frames his mistake not as a lack of self-belief but as a straightforward misjudgment of the idea itself — he was wrong about the market, not wrong about his own capability to evaluate it. He uses this to illustrate that even a researcher who studies originality for a living isn't immune to misjudging a specific idea, and that the useful response to being wrong about an idea is revising your model of the idea, not concluding you're bad at having ideas.

## Practical application
The next time you feel resistance to moving forward on something, ask explicitly which kind of doubt it is: "I'm not sure this specific idea is right" versus "I'm not sure I'm capable of doing this." The first is worth investigating with data, feedback, and testing. The second is worth noticing and setting aside rather than treating as evidence about the idea at all.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> The last time you talked yourself out of pursuing an idea, was the doubt really about the idea — or was it about whether you personally were allowed to try?
