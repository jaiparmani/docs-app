# 06 — Vuja De

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Déjà vu is seeing something new and feeling like you've encountered it before. Grant names the reverse phenomenon **vuja de**: looking at something completely familiar — a process, a system, a rule everyone follows without question — and seeing it as if for the first time, fresh enough to ask why it's built the way it is at all. Originality often starts there, in refusing to let familiarity stand in for justification.

## Why it matters
Most inefficiencies and bad defaults survive not because anyone actively defends them, but because everyone inside the system has stopped noticing them. Vuja de matters because it's specifically the skill of noticing what insiders have gone blind to — which means the people best positioned to spot the opportunity are often the ones who've been closest to the problem longest, provided they can recover the outsider's eye.

## Example from the book
Grant tells the story of Carmen Medina, an intelligence analyst at the CIA who spent years pushing the agency to move away from static, paper-based intelligence reports toward digital, collaborative information sharing — a proposal that ran into repeated institutional resistance because "that's how it's always been done" carried enormous weight inside the agency. Medina kept pressing the case despite setbacks to her career for doing so, and her persistence eventually contributed to the creation of Intellipedia, a wiki-style platform for sharing intelligence across agencies. What made her effective wasn't outsider status — she was deeply embedded in the system — it was her refusal to treat the existing process as self-justifying just because it was familiar.

## Practical application
Pick a process you follow purely out of habit — at work or otherwise — and ask, deliberately, "if this didn't already exist, would I design it this way today?" The point isn't to distrust every existing system, but to stop letting familiarity substitute for an actual answer to that question.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's a process or rule in your work that you follow without questioning purely because it's always been done that way — and what would it look like to actually ask why?
