# The Almanack of Naval Ravikant

<small>3 min read</small>

*Eric Jorgenson*

Naval Ravikant's argument, distilled across a decade of tweets, podcast transcripts, and essays that Eric Jorgenson has assembled here, runs on two mostly independent tracks that the book insists are related. The wealth track: you don't get rich by trading time for money, you get rich by owning equity in something that scales without you — code and media are the two legible modern forms of leverage because they replicate at zero marginal cost, and specific knowledge (the kind that can't be taught in a credentialed way, only built through genuine curiosity) is what lets you capture a share of the value that leverage creates. The happiness track: happiness is not an achievement sitting at the far end of the wealth pursuit, it's a skill — a default state you return to by dropping desire and comparison, closer to a Stoic or Buddhist framing than a productivity one. The book's implicit claim is that these two tracks resolve into one coherent philosophy of life. They don't, entirely, and that seam is the most interesting thing about reading it closely.

## What a compilation is actually good and bad at

This matters more here than in most books, because it isn't one. Jorgenson has curated Naval's public output — tweetstorms, a widely circulated podcast appearance with Tim Ferriss, essays from Naval's own blog — into thematic chapters, with connective narration bridging fragments that were never written to sit next to each other. The upside of this format is real: you get the highest-density version of the ideas, stripped of the padding a single-author book would need to reach standard length, and you get them in the compressed, quotable form Naval writes in natively, which is arguably his actual medium. Aphorism is the native unit of his thinking, not incidental to it.

The downside is that curation flattens tension that the original context would have preserved. Naval says different things in different moods across a decade of tweets — sometimes wealth-building sounds like a genuinely amoral game of leverage and asymmetric bets, sometimes it sounds like it's supposed to be subordinate to a Stoic detachment from outcomes at all. Stitched into one linear "philosophy," those tensions read as a coherent worldview rather than what they probably are: a smart person's evolving, sometimes contradictory positions across years, now presented with the seams sanded off. The book can't be fact-checked as argument the way an originally authored book can, because there's no single moment of composition to hold accountable — only an editor's choice of what to include and how to sequence it.

## Where the ideas hold up and where they're survivorship

The specific-knowledge argument is the strongest material here, and it's genuinely useful: the observation that the most defensible economic position is doing work that can't be outsourced or trained-for because it's inseparable from your particular curiosity and skills, is a sharper version of "follow your passion" than that phrase usually gets credit for. The leverage taxonomy — labor, capital, code, media — is a clean, teachable framework, one of the few in this genre that actually clarifies rather than just re-labels the obvious.

Where the book is weakest is in not reckoning with its own survivorship bias. Naval's specific path — early Silicon Valley angel investing, AngelList — is a case study of one, generalized into universal principles about wealth-building, in an environment (zero interest rates, a specific technology cycle) that was doing a great deal of the work. The "happiness is a choice" material is philosophically respectable and old — this is largely repackaged Stoicism and Buddhism, credited as such — but presented with the same confident, tweet-native brevity as the wealth claims, which can make contingent personal experience read as universal law. Taken as a collection of sharp individual insights to test against your own situation rather than a systematic philosophy, it earns its popularity; taken as the latter, it overclaims.
