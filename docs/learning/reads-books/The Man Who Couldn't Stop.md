# The Man Who Couldn't Stop

<small>3 min read</small>

*David Adam*

The claim underneath this book is that obsessive-compulsive disorder is not a discrete category most people are safely outside of, but a spectrum everyone stands somewhere on — intrusive, violent, or transgressive thoughts are close to universal, arriving unbidden in nearly every mind, and what separates a clinical disorder from ordinary mental noise isn't the presence of the thought but the meaning the mind attaches to it and the ritual it builds to neutralize that meaning. Adam, a science journalist who has lived with OCD centered on contamination fears, uses his own case as the entry point into a broader account of the disorder's neuroscience and history, and the structural move of the book is to keep switching registers between the two: personal account of a specific compulsion, then zoom out to what's known clinically about why the loop that sustains it is so hard to break.

## The loop as the actual subject

The most useful material in the book is the account of the compulsion-relief cycle as a self-reinforcing neurological pattern rather than a character flaw or simple bad habit. An intrusive thought triggers anxiety; a ritual — checking, washing, counting — temporarily relieves that anxiety; the relief reinforces the ritual as the thing that works, which makes the next intrusive thought more likely to trigger the same response rather than being dismissed as noise. The book's account of this loop draws on findings implicating the orbitofrontal cortex and basal ganglia in circuits that ordinarily flag genuine threats and then, in OCD, fail to switch off the flag once the threat has been addressed — the felt sense sufferers describe of never quite being sure whether a door is locked, no matter how many times they've checked it, tracks a specific breakdown in the neural signal that's supposed to say "this is resolved."

Because the material is filtered through a working science journalist rather than a clinician, it stays appropriately structural where the underlying science is itself unsettled — the neuroscience of OCD, like most psychiatric neuroscience, is correlational and still actively debated, and the book generally doesn't overclaim certainty it doesn't have. The historical sections, tracing how the disorder has been classified and mistreated across different eras of psychiatry, add useful context for how recently "checking obsessively" went from being read as a moral failing to being understood as a specific, treatable circuit problem.

## Where the spectrum framing does useful work and where it risks blurring

The spectrum argument is the book's most quotable idea and also its most easily misused one. Stated carefully, it's defensible and important: intrusive thoughts of harm, contamination, or transgression are documented as nearly universal in non-clinical populations, and the clinical disorder is distinguished not by having such thoughts but by the compulsive apparatus built around them and the functional impairment that follows. Stated loosely — "everyone's a little OCD" — it's the exact flattening that makes the clinical condition harder to take seriously, since the disorder's severity lives entirely in the loop, not the thought, and a book whose thesis rests on that distinction has to keep restating it carefully to avoid being read as license for the looser version. Adam is generally careful about this, but the framing is inherently vulnerable to being extracted from context and used to trivialize a disorder that, in its clinical form, can be severely disabling.

## What to take from it

The loop mechanism — obsession, anxiety, ritual, temporary relief, reinforcement — is the part worth carrying forward, both as a piece of neuroscience and as a more general model for how any anxiety-driven avoidance behavior gets self-reinforcing rather than self-correcting, a pattern that shows up well outside clinical OCD. The memoir sections work as grounding for the science rather than as the main event; readers looking for a rigorous clinical text should treat this as a well-reported bridge into the literature rather than a substitute for it.
