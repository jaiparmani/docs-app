# 10 — Project Checklists and the Habits of Digital Organization

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A second brain isn't a one-time setup — it degrades without a small set of recurring habits, and Forte names three as the essential ones. **Project checklists** ensure you start and finish projects the same deliberate way every time: at the start, reviewing your existing notes and past related work before diving in from scratch; at the end, capturing what you learned and archiving the project's material so future-you can find it. **Weekly and monthly reviews** are periodic check-ins where you look over what's active, move things between PARA categories as your priorities shift, and clear out what's no longer relevant. **Noticing habits** are the smallest of the three — small, in-the-moment opportunities to bold a passage, move a note, or tag something correctly, done in passing rather than as a scheduled task.

## Why it matters
CODE and PARA describe what a working system looks like; these three habits are what keeps it working over time instead of decaying into the same unmaintained pile the system was built to replace. Without a consistent start-and-end routine, projects finish and their useful material simply evaporates instead of becoming an intermediate packet for the next project. Without periodic review, PARA categories stay stuck at whatever state they were in when you first filed something, even after the project underlying it has changed or ended. Without noticing habits, small maintenance — the kind that takes seconds in the moment but hours to redo in batch — never happens, because it's never anyone's dedicated task.

## Example from the book
Forte describes a project checklist as something closer to a pilot's pre-flight checklist than an inspirational productivity ritual — a short, unglamorous, repeatable list you run through at the start of a project (what similar work have I already done? what's relevant in my notes right now?) and at the end (what's worth saving from this before I move on? what should be archived?). The point isn't the specific items on the checklist so much as the consistency of running it every time, so that valuable material generated mid-project doesn't depend on remembering to save it in the moment.

## Practical application
Write a short checklist — five items or fewer — for how you start a new project and how you close one out, and use it on your next project regardless of how small it is. At minimum, the start should include "check existing notes for related material," and the end should include "save anything reusable, archive the rest." Separately, pick a recurring weekly slot, even just fifteen minutes, to skim what's currently in your Projects and Areas and move or archive anything that's changed status.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think about the last project you finished. What useful material — a draft, a piece of research, a lesson learned — likely got lost simply because there was no habit in place to capture it on the way out?
