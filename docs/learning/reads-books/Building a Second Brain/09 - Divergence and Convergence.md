# 09 — Divergence and Convergence

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
In the chapter on creative execution, Forte describes the creative process as a rhythm between two opposing modes: **divergence**, where you generate options, wander, and expose yourself to as many inputs and possibilities as you can, and **convergence**, where you cut those options down, make trade-offs, and commit to what's actually essential so the work can move forward. He maps this rhythm directly onto CODE — Capture and Organize are divergent work, casting a wide net and gathering material without yet forcing a decision; Distill and Express are convergent work, narrowing everything gathered down into a single, finished output.

## Why it matters
Most creative struggles, in Forte's account, aren't a shortage of ideas — they're getting stuck in one mode when the work actually calls for the other. Someone who never diverges runs dry, producing thin work because they never gathered enough raw material or considered enough alternatives. Someone who never converges drowns in open options, endlessly researching and collecting without ever cutting anything down to a finished piece — a familiar failure mode for anyone who has built an elaborate note system and still struggles to finish things with it. Naming the two modes explicitly makes it possible to diagnose which one you're stuck in, rather than experiencing the stall as generic writer's block.

## Example from the book
Forte describes the discomfort of the convergence phase directly — it requires killing options you spent real effort gathering, and that always feels like a loss, even when it's the only way a genuinely finished piece of work gets produced. He argues that people with well-stocked second brains are, paradoxically, sometimes worse at converging than people with sparse notes, because the volume of good, saved material makes it feel like every option deserves inclusion, and cutting feels wasteful precisely because the system worked so well at the divergent stage.

## Practical application
Next time you're stuck on a piece of work, ask directly which mode you're actually in: do you have too little raw material (a divergence problem — go capture and research more) or do you have plenty of material but can't commit to a shape (a convergence problem — the fix is cutting, not gathering). Treating these as two different problems with two different fixes, instead of one undifferentiated "stuck" feeling, usually clarifies the next move immediately.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think about how you tend to get stuck on projects. Is your default failure mode running dry from too little raw material, or drowning in options you can't bring yourself to cut?
