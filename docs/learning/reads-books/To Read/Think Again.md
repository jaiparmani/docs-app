# Think Again: The Power of Knowing What You Don't Know

<small>2 min read</small>

*Adam Grant*

Grant's central claim is that the ability to rethink — to treat your own opinions as hypotheses worth stress-testing rather than positions worth defending — is an underdeveloped skill, and one that gets worse, not better, with expertise. The more competent people become in a domain, the more confident they tend to get, and the less inclined they are to notice when the evidence has moved out from under a belief they've held for years. The book's proposed fix is largely about mental posture: adopting something closer to a scientist's mindset (form a hypothesis, run the test, update on the result) instead of a preacher's (defend the position), a prosecutor's (attack the other side), or a politician's (win the audience), and building habits — actively seeking out disconfirming views, rewarding people who change their minds — that make rethinking a practiced skill rather than an occasional accident.

## The concrete mechanism

The book's most specific and testable claim is the "scientist mindset" reframe: that most people default to treating a strongly held belief as an identity to protect, and that simply relabeling it — this is a hypothesis I currently hold, not a position I've committed to — measurably changes how people respond to counter-evidence. Grant draws on his own organizational psychology research here, and the chapters on "confident humility" (being sure of your ability to figure things out while staying unsure of any particular current answer) are the book's most original contribution, distinct from generic advice to "keep an open mind."

## Why it's plausibly worth the read, and where it sits next to Grant's other work

Coming right after *Originals*, this reads as a natural companion rather than a repeat: *Originals* was about generating and championing novel ideas, and the underlying throughline — psychologically flexible thinking beats rigid conviction — is similar, but *Think Again* narrows the focus specifically to belief-revision rather than creativity or idea generation. It's a plausible fit for anyone who found the earlier book's mechanism-over-mantra approach useful and wants the same treatment applied to a different, arguably more personally uncomfortable skill: admitting you were wrong.

## The honest heads-up

Grant's structure across his books is consistent, and so are its limits — findings from organizational psychology paired with vivid, sometimes cherry-picked case studies (a coach, a CEO, an intelligence analyst) that illustrate rather than prove the underlying claim, with the narrative doing more persuasive work than the isolated studies alone would support. There's also a harder problem the book gestures at without fully resolving: rethinking is genuinely costly in real environments where changing your mind in public carries reputational or professional risk, and the book is stronger on individual cognitive habits than on the social and institutional incentives that often make sticking to a wrong position the locally rational choice. Worth reading as a set of specific, practiced techniques for personal belief-revision — less strong as an account of why organizations and public discourse reward certainty over accuracy in the first place.
