# 05 — Deliberate Practice and the Spelling Bee

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Grit doesn't just mean putting in more hours — it means putting in a specific, uncomfortable *kind* of hour: deliberate practice. Drawing on psychologist Anders Ericsson's research, Duckworth describes deliberate practice as a tight loop: set a stretch goal that targets a specific weakness, concentrate fully while attempting it, get immediate feedback on how it went, and reflect and repeat with adjustment. It's effortful and often not enjoyable in the moment — closer to the feeling of finding your limits than the feeling of "flow" — which is exactly why most people, even ones who genuinely want to improve, default to more comfortable forms of practice instead.

## Why it matters
This distinction matters because "practice more" is useless advice on its own — plenty of people practice for years without improving much, because their practice consists of comfortable repetition rather than the specific, feedback-driven work of deliberate practice. Grit, in Duckworth's data, doesn't just predict who logs more total hours of practice; it predicts who logs more hours of the *deliberate* kind specifically — the kind aimed at a named weakness rather than a pleasant rehearsal of what's already mastered.

## Example from the book
Duckworth studied competitors in the Scripps National Spelling Bee, tracking both their Grit Scale scores and how they described their study methods. She found two distinct types of practice reported: quizzing themselves or being quizzed on new, harder words (deliberate practice, aimed squarely at their current weak point) versus more passive activities like reading for pleasure or being quizzed on words they already knew. Grittier competitors logged more hours specifically of the first kind, and it was that deliberate-practice time — not overall study time — that predicted how far they advanced in competition.

## Practical application
Before your next practice session on anything you're trying to improve, name the specific weak point you're targeting and how you'll get immediate feedback on it — if you can't answer both, you're probably about to do comfortable repetition instead of deliberate practice, and the hours won't convert into improvement at the rate you'd expect.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think about the last time you "practiced" something you want to get better at. Were you working on a named weak point with real feedback, or repeating what you already do comfortably?
