# 03 — The West Point Study

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book's central piece of evidence comes from the United States Military Academy at West Point. Every incoming class goes through "Beast Barracks," a notoriously brutal seven-week summer initiation before freshman year even begins, and every year a meaningful fraction of cadets — many of whom were admitted through an extremely selective process — drop out before it's over. West Point already had a sophisticated tool for predicting success: the Whole Candidate Score, a composite of SAT scores, class rank, leadership record, and physical fitness testing, refined over decades. Duckworth gave incoming cadets her Grit Scale questionnaire before Beast Barracks started, then tracked who made it through. Grit scores predicted who quit and who stayed better than the Whole Candidate Score did.

## Why it matters
This is the book's strongest single piece of evidence because it isn't a lab study or a self-reported life-satisfaction survey — it's a hard, binary, real-world outcome (did you drop out of Beast Barracks or not) measured against an admissions system that was already highly selective and already trying to predict exactly this. If a short self-report questionnaire about passion and perseverance can outperform an institution's own carefully engineered selection formula — one built from objective measures like test scores and fitness metrics — that's a genuinely surprising result, and it's the finding that made grit worth a book rather than a magazine article.

## Example from the book
Duckworth describes standing at West Point during Beast Barracks, watching an environment deliberately engineered to be as physically and psychologically punishing as the Academy can make it, precisely because that's the whole point of the exercise: find out who quits under real pressure before it matters more. What her data showed was that neither the cadets' SAT scores nor their high school class rank predicted who'd still be standing at the end of the summer — but their Grit Scale scores, filled out weeks earlier in a very ordinary-feeling questionnaire, did.

## Practical application
When you're picking a team, a hire, or even a project for yourself and you're tempted to weight it heavily on credentials and past achievement, ask what it would look like to also weight it on a track record of sustained interest and effort — did this person (or did you) stick with hard things for years, not just perform well on a one-time test of ability.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think about a selection process you've been part of — hiring, admissions, a team tryout. Did it measure anything like sustained effort over years, or was it entirely a snapshot of current ability?
