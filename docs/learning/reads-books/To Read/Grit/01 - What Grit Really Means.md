# 01 — What Grit Really Means

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Duckworth defines grit precisely, and the precision is the point: it's **passion combined with perseverance for long-term goals**. The word "passion" is doing something unusual here — she doesn't mean intensity of feeling in a given moment, the way the word gets used in pop culture ("follow your passion," "she's so passionate about this"). She means *consistency of interest over years*. Someone can be intensely excited about ten different projects a year and have zero grit in her sense, because none of them survive contact with boredom, plateau, or a better-looking opportunity. Grit is what's left after the initial excitement fades and you keep going anyway, pointed at the same higher-level goal, for a long time.

## Why it matters
This reframing matters because it separates grit from two things people often confuse it with: raw effort in a single hard moment (a sprint), and talent (a fixed capacity). A person can work brutally hard for a weekend and have no grit; a person can be extraordinarily talented and quit at the first plateau. Grit is specifically about the *multi-year* shape of a person's goals — whether their efforts this year are still in service of the same top-level thing they cared about three years ago. Duckworth argues this is measurable, trainable, and a better predictor of who finishes hard things than IQ, physical talent, or even initial enthusiasm.

## Example from the book
Duckworth opens with her own origin story: growing up, her father would periodically remark, unprompted, that she was "no genius" — a comment she describes as motivating in an uncomfortable way, pushing her away from a career premised on raw brilliance and toward a research question she could spend a career on: if not talent, then what actually explains who succeeds? That personal irritant is what eventually sent her, as a psychologist, into classrooms, boardrooms, and West Point to study grit directly rather than assume talent was the answer.

## Practical application
Take a goal you currently describe as a "passion" and check it against the multi-year test rather than the how-excited-am-I-right-now test: has your interest in it actually held for years, surviving boredom and setbacks, or has the label "passion" been reattached to a new thing every few months? If it's the latter, the problem probably isn't your inherent staying power — it's that you haven't yet let any one interest run long enough to become gritty about.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of something you've called a "passion" in the last year. Has your interest in it actually survived a genuinely boring or difficult stretch — or has it quietly been replaced by the next exciting thing before it had the chance to be tested?
