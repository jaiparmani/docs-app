# 07 — Interest Is Cultivated, Not Discovered

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Popular advice tells people to "follow your passion," as if a fully-formed calling is sitting out there waiting to be found in a single flash of recognition. Duckworth's research and interviews say the opposite: interest is triggered, not discovered, and it takes years of active development before it looks anything like passion. The typical pattern she finds in the people she studies is a first spark of curiosity — often almost accidental — followed by a long, unglamorous stretch of trying the thing, getting a little better, getting encouraged by someone else who noticed, and trying it again. The "aha" moment people report in hindsight is usually a story they've smoothed over afterward; at the time, it rarely felt like destiny.

## Why it matters
Waiting to be struck by passion is a bad strategy because it puts the burden on discovery instead of development, and most people who wait never get struck. It also makes people abandon promising early interests too quickly — if the first weeks of a new hobby or job don't feel like electric, defining passion, the "follow your passion" framing tells you you picked wrong, when in fact that flat feeling at the start is completely normal and expected. Duckworth's point isn't that interest doesn't matter — it's one of her four psychological assets — but that the sequence people imagine (find your passion, then commit to it) is backwards from how it actually develops (try things, notice what pulls your attention back, then deliberately deepen it).

## Example from the book
Duckworth interviewed high achievers across very different fields about their origin stories and found almost none of them described a single defining moment of falling in love with their work. Instead, interest typically appeared in stages: an initial exposure that was pleasant but not obviously special, a period of getting slightly better and finding that satisfying, encouragement or recognition from a parent, coach, or teacher that made the activity feel worth continuing, and only after all of that, something that finally resembled real passion. She also points to her own path — from management consulting, which she left because the work didn't feel meaningful, to teaching math, to psychology research — as a gradual, backtracking process rather than a moment of clarity, which is closer to what she found in her interview subjects than the tidy origin stories people tell after the fact.

## Practical application
If you're waiting to feel a defining spark before committing to something, stop waiting — try it, give it enough repeated exposure to move past the flat first-weeks feeling, and pay attention to what pulls your focus back on its own rather than asking whether it feels like passion yet. Interest gets built through action and feedback, not found through introspection. If you already have something that mildly interests you, the more useful question isn't "is this my true passion" but "am I doing anything to develop it further."

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there something you tried once, found mildly interesting, and then dropped because it didn't immediately feel like a calling? What might it look like now if you'd kept developing it instead?
