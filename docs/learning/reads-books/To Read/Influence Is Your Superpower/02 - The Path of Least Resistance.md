# 02 — The Path of Least Resistance

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Chance names this the bedrock principle of the entire book: people take the path of least resistance. Ease — how effortful something feels to do — predicts behavior better than motivation, better than price, and better than quality, and it does so by a wide margin. This cuts against the instinct most people have when they want to change someone's mind or behavior, which is to build a more compelling case: more reasons, more urgency, more proof of value. Chance's argument is that a stronger case usually isn't the bottleneck. Friction is. People who are fully convinced still don't act if the action itself feels like work, and people who aren't especially convinced will act anyway if the action is nearly frictionless.

## Why it matters
This reorders where effort should go when you're trying to move someone. The default move — sharpening the pitch, adding another compelling fact, raising the stakes — targets motivation, which turns out to be a weaker lever than most people assume. The stronger lever is almost always invisible to the person making the ask, because it isn't about the content of the request at all — it's about how many steps, how much thought, and how much friction stand between "I'd like to" and "I did." Chance's claim is blunt: awareness campaigns and inspiring arguments routinely fail not because people disagree, but because agreeing and acting are two different thresholds, and only the second one is under your control as the person asking.

## Example from the book
Chance points to public health campaigns like "5 A Day" as a case study in this gap: awareness of the message rose sharply, but actual fruit-and-vegetable consumption barely moved, because knowing you should eat more produce doesn't remove the friction of buying it, washing it, prepping it, and remembering to reach for it instead of something already open in the pantry. She contrasts this with a simple, underused business metric called the Customer Effort Score, built around one question — "how easy was this?" — which on its own explains roughly a third of whether someone buys again, expands their business with a company, or recommends it to others. Ease alone, stripped of any appeal to motivation, is doing that much of the work.

## Practical application
Before adding another reason to your pitch, audit the path itself: how many steps, decisions, or moments of friction sit between the person's "yes" and the thing actually happening? Each one is a place the path can lose them, regardless of how motivated they are in the moment they agreed. Concretely: pre-fill what you can, default to the outcome you want rather than requiring an opt-in, hand someone a single next step instead of a list, and remove any point where they have to remember to come back and finish something later. If you're asking a friend to start exercising, don't strengthen the case for exercise — put the workout clothes by the bed and load the class into their calendar for them.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick something you've been trying to convince someone to do — including yourself. Is the problem really that they're unconvinced, or is there a step in the path that's quietly adding friction you haven't noticed because you're not the one who has to take it?
