# 08 — Playfulness as an Influence Tool

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Most advice on asking for things treats the moment of the ask as high-stakes and serious — get it right, or suffer the consequences. Chance argues the opposite posture is usually more effective: bring playfulness to the ask itself. A request made lightly, with visible willingness to be told no and to laugh about it either way, lowers the emotional temperature for both people. It signals that you're not fragile about the outcome, which paradoxically makes people more comfortable saying yes, because a playful ask doesn't put the other person in the position of having to manage your feelings if they decline.

## Why it matters
Heavy, serious asks put pressure on both sides — the asker feels like everything is riding on the answer, and the person being asked can feel the weight of that pressure and sometimes says no just to avoid the discomfort of being on the hook for someone else's high-stakes moment. Playfulness breaks that dynamic. It doesn't lower the sincerity of the request, but it removes the implicit threat that a "no" will be a big, awkward event. People are more willing to engage with a request — to actually consider it, rather than reflexively declining to avoid the discomfort — when the person asking has visibly made room for either answer to be fine.

## Example from the book
Chance draws on the same rejection-seeking project referenced elsewhere in the book — deliberately odd, low-stakes requests made to strangers and institutions purely to practice tolerating "no" — but highlights it here for a different reason: the requests themselves were absurd on purpose, and treated as absurd by the person making them. Asking to make an announcement over a store's intercom, or asking to borrow a stranger's dog for a walk, only works as a request at all because it's framed with visible lightness — nobody making a request like that is pretending the stakes are high. What comes through in how people respond isn't irritation at the oddness of the ask, it's frequent delight at being included in something a little silly, and a willingness to say yes that a deadly serious version of the same interaction would never have gotten.

## Practical application
The next time you have a request that feels loaded — asking for a favor, raising something awkward, proposing an idea you're not sure will land — try delivering it with more lightness than feels natural, rather than more gravity. Say the quiet part out loud in a way that defuses it: "this is a bit of a long shot, but—" or make a small joke about the awkwardness of asking rather than pretending the awkwardness isn't there. You're not lowering the sincerity of the request, you're removing the implicit demand that the other person handle your feelings about their answer.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of the last request you made that felt heavy or high-stakes to deliver. What would have changed — for you, and for the person you asked — if you'd brought playfulness to it instead of gravity?
