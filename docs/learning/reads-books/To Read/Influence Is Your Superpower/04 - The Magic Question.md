# 04 — The Magic Question

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
When a conversation hits resistance — a stalled negotiation, a colleague who keeps missing deadlines, a request someone keeps deflecting — the instinctive move is to push harder or to critique the other person's position. Chance offers a single reframing question instead, some version of "what would it take for us to resolve this?" or "what would it take for you to say yes?" The question does something structural: it stops positioning the conversation as you against them and repositions it as the two of you against a shared problem. It also treats the other person as a capable collaborator with useful information about their own constraints, rather than as an obstacle to be argued past.

## Why it matters
Most resistance in a negotiation or a difficult ask isn't stubbornness, it's unstated constraints — a budget, a fear, a prior commitment, a piece of context you don't have. Arguing harder for your side does nothing to surface that information; it just pressures the other person to defend a position instead of explaining it. "What would it take" hands the floor back to them and asks them to solve the problem alongside you, which both surfaces the real obstacle and, because they proposed the solution rather than had it imposed on them, makes them far more invested in actually following through on it.

## Example from the book
Chance applies the question across very different registers. With a chronically late employee, instead of a reprimand ("you need to be on time"), a manager can ask "what would it take for you to get here on time every day this month?" — which turns a confrontation into the employee designing their own commitment. She applies the identical structure to self-advocacy: rather than simply requesting a raise or a promotion and waiting for a yes or no, she recommends asking your manager directly "what would it take for me to get to the next level?" or "to be at the top of the salary band?" — a question that can't be answered with a flat no, because it demands specifics, and those specifics become a concrete path rather than a closed door.

## Practical application
The next time you hit resistance — from a colleague, a partner, a vendor, or yourself — resist the urge to restate your case more forcefully. Instead, ask some version of "what would it take?" and then stop talking. Let the other person fill the silence with the actual constraint, because that constraint, once named, is usually far more solvable than the vague "no" that was standing in for it. Use the same question on yourself when you're stuck: "what would it take for me to finish this by Friday?" turns a stalled intention into a list of concrete conditions.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a standing "no" in your life right now — from a person, or from yourself. What happens if you replace the argument you'd normally make with the single question, "what would it take?"
