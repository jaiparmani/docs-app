# 06 — Framing: Monumental, Manageable, Mysterious

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The same underlying idea can land completely differently depending on how it's framed, and Chance organizes the useful frames into three broad types. A monumental frame makes something feel urgent and important — this matters, this is bigger than the day-to-day. A manageable frame makes something feel doable — this is a small, concrete step, not an overwhelming commitment. A mysterious frame makes something feel intriguing — it withholds just enough to make the other person want to lean in and find out more. None of these change the substance of what you're proposing; they change which door the other person walks through to get to it, and different people, or the same person in different moods, will respond to different doors.

## Why it matters
People default to pitching an idea the way it feels natural to them — usually monumental, because the person pitching is the one who already finds it important — without checking whether that's the frame that will actually land for the specific person or moment in front of them. A monumental frame can overwhelm someone who's already stretched thin; a manageable frame can undersell something to someone who wants to feel part of something bigger; a mysterious frame can frustrate someone who wants the full picture up front and just intrigue someone who's tired of being pitched to directly. Having three frames on hand, instead of one default, means you can match the door to the person instead of hoping they climb through the one you happened to build.

## Example from the book
Chance points to Marie Kondo's *The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up* as a title that manages to hit all three frames at once — "life-changing" is monumental, "magic" is mysterious, and the underlying promise of a tidier home is manageable and concrete. For a case where the frame itself changed the outcome, she cites Steve Jobs's pitch to recruit John Sculley, then running PepsiCo, to become Apple's CEO. A frame built around stability, salary, and a clear career step would have been a manageable pitch — and an unpersuasive one, given what Sculley was leaving behind. Jobs instead used a monumental frame, asking him some version of whether he wanted to keep selling sugar water for the rest of his life or come do something that could change the world. Same job, same company — a completely different door.

## Practical application
Before you pitch an idea, draft it three ways instead of one: state it as urgent and important (monumental), state it as one small, concrete next step (manageable), and state it as a partial reveal that invites a question (mysterious). Then think about who you're actually talking to — someone overloaded probably needs manageable, someone who wants to feel part of something meaningful probably needs monumental, someone jaded by direct pitches might respond best to mysterious. You don't need to guess right every time; you need to have more than one frame built before you walk in.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of an idea you've pitched that fell flat. Which frame did you use — and if you'd tried one of the other two instead, would it have landed differently with that particular person?
