# 05 — Anticipating Resistance

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Chance borrows a martial-arts frame — aikido — for handling objections: a skilled aikido practitioner doesn't meet an incoming force head-on, they redirect it, using the opponent's own momentum rather than opposing it directly. Applied to influence, this means naming the resistance you expect before the other person raises it, and folding it into your pitch rather than waiting for it to surface as a rebuttal you then have to fight off. Acknowledging the objection out loud — "you might be thinking X" — does two things at once: it removes the charge of confrontation from the moment the other person actually thinks it (you got there first, on their side), and it signals you've genuinely considered their perspective rather than just building a case for your own.

## Why it matters
Unaddressed resistance doesn't go away when you talk over it — it goes underground, and the other person spends the rest of the pitch quietly building their counter-argument instead of listening to you. By the time they voice it, both sides are dug in: they're defending a position, you're defending yours, and the exchange becomes adversarial exactly where it didn't need to be. Naming the objection yourself, early and without defensiveness, keeps the conversation collaborative because there's nothing left for the other person to "catch" you on — you already put it on the table.

## Example from the book
Chance points to how Beyond Meat's Ethan Brown approached an entrenched, predictable resistance: meat-eaters' reflexive skepticism of plant-based substitutes as a lesser, compromise product. Rather than waiting for that resistance to surface as an objection at the point of sale, the positioning got ahead of it directly — framing the product not as a replacement people were settling for, but as something built to be better on its own terms, addressing the "this is a downgrade" objection before a skeptical customer ever had the chance to voice it. The resistance didn't disappear because it was ignored; it was defused because it was named and answered before it could calcify into a stated objection someone then had to publicly back down from.

## Practical application
Before your next high-stakes ask or pitch, write down the two or three objections you'd raise if you were on the other side of the table — the real ones, not the easy ones to answer. Then build the acknowledgment of the strongest one directly into your opening, in your own words, before the other person has to raise it themselves: "you might reasonably think this is going to cost more than it's worth — here's why I don't think that's actually the case." You lose the appearance of a flawless pitch, but you gain something more useful: the other person no longer has to be your opponent to voice their doubt.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's the objection you're most afraid someone will raise the next time you make an important ask? What happens if you say it out loud yourself, first, instead of hoping it doesn't come up?
