# 09 — Saying No Is Also Influence

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A book about influence might be expected to focus entirely on how to get other people to say yes to you. Chance devotes real attention to the reverse skill: your own ability to say no, cleanly and without over-explaining, to the requests other people make of you. She treats this as part of the same skill set as asking well, not a separate topic — someone who can't say no is operating from a position of chronic overcommitment and resentment, which erodes the same trust and goodwill that skillful asking is trying to build. Influence, in her framing, is a two-way relationship with the word "no": how well you tolerate hearing it, and how well you're able to say it yourself.

## Why it matters
People who struggle to say no tend to over-explain, over-apologize, or agree to things they'll quietly resent, all of which damage relationships more than a clean, respectful no ever would. A wordy, guilty no signals that the request put you in a bind; a calm, brief no signals that you know your own capacity and aren't going to pretend otherwise, which is a more trustworthy signal to the person asking than a reluctant, hedge-everywhere yes. Chance's point is that boundary-setting isn't the opposite of being influential and well-liked — over time, it's part of what makes someone worth asking things of in the first place, because their yeses actually mean something.

## Example from the book
Chance runs an exercise with her Yale MBA students built directly around this gap: a 24-hour period in which they have to practice saying no to requests they'd normally accommodate out of habit — extra work, social obligations, small asks that add up — without the usual over-justification. The point of the constraint isn't to make students less agreeable generally; it's to break the reflex of treating every ask as something that requires an elaborate excuse to decline, and to let them feel, directly, that a simple and warm no doesn't produce the social fallout their anxiety predicts. Most students report the same surprise afterward: the no landed far more lightly, for the other person, than it felt saying it.

## Practical application
Practice saying no without the reflexive apology-and-justification chain. A workable structure is short and warm: acknowledge the ask, decline plainly, and stop — "thanks for thinking of me, that's not going to work for me this time" — without stacking three reasons on top of it that invite negotiation. If you notice yourself defaulting to yes out of habit rather than genuine willingness, treat that as the signal to practice this, not the exception where it's fine to skip it.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's something you said yes to recently that you actually wanted to decline? What did the over-explaining or guilt around it cost you — and what would a short, warm no have cost instead?
