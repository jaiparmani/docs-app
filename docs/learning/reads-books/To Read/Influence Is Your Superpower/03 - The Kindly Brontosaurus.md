# 03 — The Kindly Brontosaurus

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Most people treat an ask like a vending machine: you put in the request, a result drops out — yes or no — and if it's no, the transaction is over and you walk away. Chance argues the far more accurate model is a slot machine: the payout isn't tied to any single pull, it's tied to how many times you pull the lever, and a "no" tells you almost nothing about whether the next pull will land. Treating a single rejection as a verdict on the request, or on you, is a category error — one no is one data point in a series, not a closed case. The skill she's teaching isn't a cleverer pitch, it's a different relationship with the interval between asking and getting: staying in the game, gently, past the first no.

## Why it matters
Most people's real limiting factor isn't persuasive skill, it's asking often enough and directly enough in the first place — and the thing that stops them is a wildly inflated fear of what a "no" costs. Chance's research and coaching repeatedly finds that people underestimate how often a genuine, well-framed ask succeeds, and overestimate how much a refusal will sting or cost them socially. That mismatch quietly shrinks what people even attempt: raises never requested, extensions never asked for, help never sought, because the anticipated pain of "no" outweighs, in the asker's head, the actual and much smaller cost of hearing it. Reframing rejection as ordinary data rather than a personal referendum is what lets people ask for things closer to what they actually want.

## Example from the book
Chance draws on Jia Jiang's "100 Days of Rejection" project, in which he deliberately sought out rejection with a string of odd, low-stakes requests to strangers and companies — asking to borrow money from someone he'd never met, asking a stranger for a lift around the block, asking a store for something absurd — and tracked the results. Despite the requests being unusual enough that refusal seemed almost guaranteed, a meaningful share of them succeeded outright, and several "no"s turned into "yes" the moment he stayed in the conversation and asked a follow-up rather than retreating. Chance pairs this with what she calls the Kindly Brontosaurus approach, drawn partly from how experienced salespeople operate: they routinely return to a prospect five, six, seven times after an initial no, not with pressure or escalating aggression, but with the same warm, unhurried persistence — large, gentle, and simply still there.

## Practical application
Next time you get a no on something that matters, resist the urge to treat it as final and resist the opposite urge to push harder in the same moment. Instead, let a little time pass and come back once more, warmly and without apology, the way the Kindly Brontosaurus would — not cornering the other person, just remaining present. Separately, audit your last month for asks you talked yourself out of making at all, and pick one to actually make this week, treating whatever answer you get as one pull of the lever rather than a verdict.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's an ask you've been sitting on because you've already decided, in advance, what the answer will be? What would it cost you, really, to find out — and to ask again if the first answer is no?
