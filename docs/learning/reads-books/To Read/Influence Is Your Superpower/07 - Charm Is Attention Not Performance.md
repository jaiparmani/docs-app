# 07 — Charm Is Attention, Not Performance

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Chance strips "charm" of its performative connotations — the idea that being charming means being polished, quick-witted, or the most entertaining person in the room — and reduces it to something much simpler and much more learnable: genuine, focused attention on the other person, combined with visible warmth. Her own framing is that charisma isn't something you perform at people, it's something you do by directing your attention fully onto them — really listening, really noticing, rather than waiting for your turn to be interesting. Paired with that is warmth: making sure the other person can tell, plainly, that you like them. Competence and cleverness are almost beside the point; the two things people are actually reading for, mostly unconsciously, are "is this person paying attention to me" and "does this person like me."

## Why it matters
Most people over-invest in the wrong half of this equation. Trying to seem impressive, credentialed, or competent is the instinctive move when you want someone to like or trust you, but Chance's research and coaching point the other way: warmth is a stronger predictor of whether people say yes to you than competence is, and people routinely misjudge this trade-off, assuming they need to prove capability when what actually moves the room is whether they come across as genuinely paying attention and genuinely liking the person in front of them. She flags this is especially true for women, whose competence is frequently taken as a given or scrutinized separately, while warmth ends up doing outsized work in whether they're actually liked and listened to — making the attention-and-warmth skill not a soft add-on but a core lever, particularly when competence isn't the thing in question.

## Example from the book
Rather than a single anecdote, Chance's teaching here is built around a repeated observation from her classroom and coaching work: when she asks people to identify what made someone else charming or influential in a specific interaction, they almost never cite that person's intelligence or credentials — they cite a feeling of having been genuinely seen, heard, and liked in the room. She uses this consistently, across MBA students, executives, and negotiators, to make the same point: the skill people are actually chasing when they try to "be more charismatic" is rarely a personality trait to perform, it's a directable behavior — where your attention goes, and how visibly you let someone know you like them.

## Practical application
In your next important conversation, deliberately withhold the urge to make yourself sound impressive and instead put your full attention on the other person — ask a real follow-up question, and let your face and your words show you're actually glad to be talking to them, not just being polite. Notice how much of what you'd normally spend on "sounding good" is unnecessary once the other person feels genuinely attended to and genuinely liked. This is a rehearsable behavior, not a fixed trait — you can practice directing attention the same way you'd practice any other skill.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of someone you find genuinely charming. Was it their competence that drew you in — or was it that you felt fully seen and clearly liked when you were with them?
