# 08 — The Abortion-Crime Hypothesis

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book's most famous — and most controversial — argument is that the legalization of abortion following *Roe v. Wade* in 1973 was a major contributor to the sharp, unexpected drop in crime the United States saw in the 1990s, roughly a generation later. The mechanism the authors propose isn't about the total number of children born, but about which children were, on average, more or less likely to be born once abortion became a legal option: children who research on risk factors for crime associates with a materially higher chance of later criminal involvement — unwanted pregnancies, teenage mothers, and difficult economic circumstances — were, in aggregate, less likely to be carried to term after 1973 than before.

## Why it matters
This chapter is the book's biggest swing at applying its incentive-and-data method to a question with enormous social and political weight, and it's presented as a case study in following data to a conclusion that virtually no one — least of all criminologists, who in the early 1990s were bracing for a coming wave of youth crime that never arrived — predicted. Whatever you conclude about its validity, the argument is worth understanding on its own terms before moving to the next chunk, which covers how other economists responded to it.

## Example from the book
Levitt and co-author John Donohue's original argument rested on more than the national timing coincidence: crime began dropping nationally in the early 1990s, about eighteen years after 1973, which is roughly the age window in which a cohort would be reaching the years of peak involvement in crime. The authors also point to a natural experiment — five U.S. states (New York, California, Washington, Hawaii, and Alaska) legalized abortion a few years before *Roe v. Wade* made it legal nationally, and Donohue and Levitt found that crime in those early-legalizing states began falling earlier than in the rest of the country, tracking the earlier timing of their legalization. States with higher abortion rates in the 1970s and 1980s, in their analysis, saw larger crime drops in the 1990s than states with lower abortion rates, which the authors present as further evidence for the mechanism rather than mere coincidence.

## Practical application
When you encounter a bold causal claim tied to a policy or social change with a long time lag before its supposed effect, look specifically for whether the argument offers more than a single before-and-after comparison — a natural experiment, a dose-response relationship (more of the cause associated with more of the effect), or a comparison across regions with different timing. Those extra layers of evidence are what separate "correlation that happens to line up" from an argument that's at least trying to rule out obvious alternative explanations — though, as the next chunk covers, having those layers doesn't automatically mean the argument survives scrutiny.
