# 01 — Incentives Are the Root of Everything

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Levitt and Dubner open by arguing that economics, stripped of its jargon, is really just the study of incentives — the things that cause a person to do more of one behavior and less of another. They sort incentives into three overlapping types: **economic** (money — a bonus, a fine, a fee), **social** (the desire to be seen well, or not seen badly, by other people), and **moral** (the internal sense that doing or not doing something is simply right or wrong). Most real behavior is being pushed by some blend of all three at once, and the same lever can work through more than one channel — a fine at a daycare, for instance, can fail as an economic deterrent precisely because it accidentally cancels out a moral one.

## Why it matters
This is the lens the entire rest of the book depends on. Every case study that follows — teachers, sumo wrestlers, real estate agents, drug dealers, parents — gets read the same way: not "what do these people say they're doing" but "what are they actually incentivized to do." The authors' larger claim is that morality represents how people would like the world to work, while economics represents how it actually does — and that gap is where most of the book's surprising findings live. Once you start asking "what's the incentive here" as a reflex, plenty of behavior that looks irrational, mysterious, or morally bankrupt turns out to be a perfectly rational response to whatever is actually being rewarded or punished.

## Example from the book
The clearest illustration is a study of an Israeli daycare that started fining parents $3 for picking their kids up late. Late pickups didn't drop — they roughly doubled. Before the fine, parents were restrained by a *social* and *moral* incentive: showing up late imposed on the teachers' time, and parents felt bad about it. The $3 fine reframed lateness as a transaction — parents could now simply buy the right to be late, and $3 was cheap enough that many did. Worse, when the daycare later dropped the fine, the higher rate of late pickups persisted, because the moral incentive that had been quietly doing the work the whole time didn't come back once it had been replaced by a price.

## Practical application
Next time someone's behavior looks confusing or "irrational" — a coworker cutting corners, a friend not following through, a policy that keeps backfiring — resist the urge to explain it as a character flaw. Instead, write out what that person or system is actually being rewarded and punished for, across all three incentive types, not just the economic one. Often the mystery dissolves: the behavior was the predictable response to the incentive structure the whole time, and the fix is to change the structure, not to lecture the person inside it.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a rule, fee, or policy you've seen backfire — where the intended deterrent seemed to make the unwanted behavior more common instead of less. Which incentive channel (economic, social, or moral) got accidentally crowded out?
