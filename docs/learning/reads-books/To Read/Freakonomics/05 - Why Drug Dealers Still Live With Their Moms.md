# 05 — Why Drug Dealers Still Live With Their Moms

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
A crack-selling gang, examined through its actual financial records, turns out to be organized less like a chaotic street operation and more like a franchise business — a small number of people at the top capturing most of the profit, and a large base of low-level foot soldiers taking almost all of the risk for very little pay. The popular image of the drug trade as an easy path to riches doesn't match what the numbers inside one actual gang showed.

## Why it matters
This chapter is where the book's incentive-thinking runs headfirst into a genuine puzzle: if street-level dealing pays close to minimum wage while carrying a real chance of being killed, why does anyone stay in it? The answer isn't that the foot soldiers are irrational — it's that they're playing a tournament, not a job. Tournaments (a small number of enormous prizes at the top, funded by a mass of participants earning little to nothing) explain a lot of behavior that looks irrational under a simple wage lens, from drug dealing to acting to professional sports, once you see that people are pricing in the chance of the jackpot, not just the going rate for the work itself.

## Example from the book
Sociologist Sudhir Venkatesh gained access to the internal financial ledgers of a Chicago crack-selling gang, which economist Levitt then analyzed. The numbers showed a steep pyramid: the gang's local leader took home roughly six figures a year, while the rank-and-file foot soldiers who actually stood on street corners selling the product earned wages close to or below what they could have made at a fast-food job — all while facing, over several years in the gang, a real risk of arrest, injury, or death. The reason people stayed wasn't ignorance of the odds; it was that the gang worked like a tournament, similar in structure to the market for actors or professional athletes — huge numbers compete for a tiny number of lucrative positions at the top, and even a low probability of reaching one of those positions can make the terrible average payoff worth accepting for people who see few other routes to that kind of upside.

## Practical application
When a group of people's behavior looks financially irrational — staying in a low-paying, high-risk role rather than moving to something safer — check whether they might be pricing in a small chance at a much larger prize rather than the average outcome. That reframing changes the conversation from "why don't they just leave" to "what would make the realistic alternatives to that gamble actually competitive," which is a very different, more useful question.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Have you ever stayed in a low-paying or high-risk situation — a startup, an unpaid internship, a long-shot creative pursuit — because of a small chance at a big prize rather than the realistic average outcome? Looking back, was that tournament bet a rational one given the alternatives you actually had?
