# 06 — What Makes a Perfect Parent

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Working from a large longitudinal dataset tracking thousands of American schoolchildren, Levitt and Dubner separate what parents *are* from what parents *do* — and find that the factors statistically associated with children's test scores are overwhelmingly about the parents' circumstances (education, income, the environment a child is born into) rather than the specific parenting techniques applied day to day. Several practices that conventional wisdom treats as obviously important turn out to show little to no measurable correlation with test outcomes at all.

## Why it matters
This is the book's clearest lesson in not confusing correlation with causation, and in not confusing "measurable" with "irrelevant" either. The point isn't that reading to your child or taking them to museums doesn't matter for reasons beyond test scores — it's that a lot of parenting advice treats specific techniques as the active ingredient in child outcomes, when the data suggests the active ingredient is much more often who the parents already are before any specific technique gets applied. That's an uncomfortable finding precisely because techniques are something a parent can act on directly, while circumstances like education and income are not — but the discomfort doesn't make the correlation go away.

## Example from the book
Using data from the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study, the authors list factors that showed a real statistical association with a child's early test scores — the child has highly educated parents, the parents have high socioeconomic status, the mother was 30 or older at her first child's birth, the child had low birth weight (negatively), the parents speak English at home, and the family has many books in the home — alongside a second list of factors that showed essentially no measurable association despite being widely treated as important parenting choices: the family is intact, the family recently moved to a better neighborhood, the mother didn't work between birth and kindergarten, the child attended Head Start, the parents regularly take the child to museums, the child is regularly spanked, the child watches a lot of television, and the parents read to the child nearly every day.

## Practical application
When you hear a piece of parenting (or any life) advice framed as "do this specific thing and you'll get this specific outcome," ask whether the people who already do that thing might simply be different in other, harder-to-change ways from the people who don't — and whether the study behind the advice actually separated those two possibilities. It's a useful habit well beyond parenting: any time an outcome is correlated with a behavior, check whether the behavior is a cause or just a marker of something upstream doing the real work.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Which item from the "doesn't seem to matter" list surprised you most — and does your surprise say something about which specific parenting techniques you'd assumed were doing more causal work than the data suggests they are?
