# 07 — A Name Is a Signal, Not a Cause

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
A first name can be strongly correlated with a person's later economic and social outcomes without being the cause of those outcomes at all. Using California birth-certificate data, economist Roland G. Fryer Jr. (whose research the authors draw on) found that children with distinctively Black names, on average, ended up with worse life outcomes than children with more common names — but the same data showed that two children born in similar circumstances, one given a distinctively Black name and one given a more conventional one, went on to do about equally well. The name itself wasn't doing the work; it was a marker of the parents' broader circumstances, which were doing the actual work.

## Why it matters
This chapter is the sharpest version of the book's recurring warning against mistaking correlation for causation, because a name is about as pure a case as you can find — it's assigned at birth, before the child does anything at all, so any correlation with later outcomes has to run through something else entirely: the circumstances of the parents who chose it. It also shows how a name functions as a signal to other people (a resume screener, a teacher, an employer) even when it isn't a signal about the child's own inherent traits — which means a name can still shape outcomes indirectly, through other people's reactions to it, even while causing nothing directly itself.

## Example from the book
Fryer's data showed that distinctively Black names became far more common starting in the 1970s, tracking closely with the rise of the Black Power movement, and that parents who chose those names tended to be younger, less educated, and lower-income on average than parents who chose more conventional names — the same demographic factors chunk six identified as genuinely predictive of a child's outcomes. The book also traces how first names trickle downward through the income ladder over time: names that start out popular among high-income, highly educated parents (think "Madison" or "Aiden" in different eras) become progressively more common among lower-income families roughly a decade later, by which point the wealthier parents who wanted a distinctive name have often already moved on to a new one.

## Practical application
Any time you notice yourself forming a snap judgment about someone based on a name, an accent, a zip code, or a similar surface marker, pause and separate two questions: is this trait actually causing the outcome I'm predicting, or is it just correlated with a set of circumstances that are doing the real causal work? The distinction matters practically — a marker can be a useful predictor for a stranger you know nothing else about, while still being completely wrong to treat as a cause, or as a reason to judge the individual in front of you.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Have you ever made an assumption about someone based on their name, and later learned something about their actual circumstances that had nothing to do with the name itself? What was really doing the predictive work in your original assumption?
