# 03 — Real Estate Agents and the Price of Information

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
An expert's advice is only as trustworthy as the expert's own incentives, and information asymmetry — one side of a transaction knowing something the other side doesn't — is one of the most reliable sources of profit in the economy. Experts from real estate agents to doctors to mechanics have historically made money by knowing more than their clients and by being paid in ways that don't quite line up with what's actually best for the client.

## Why it matters
The chapter's real point isn't "real estate agents are dishonest" — it's that you don't need dishonesty for misaligned incentives to change outcomes; a perfectly well-meaning expert operating under a bad incentive structure will still, on average, act differently on their own behalf than on yours. That distinction matters because it means the fix isn't finding a more virtuous expert, it's noticing where the expert's payoff diverges from your own and adjusting how much weight you give their advice at exactly that point. It also explains why the same asymmetry shows up across so many professions that get paid on commission or by the transaction rather than by the outcome delivered — the incentive gap is structural, not personal, which is exactly why it's so persistent and so easy to miss from inside the relationship.

## Example from the book
Levitt compared the sale data for homes real estate agents sold on behalf of clients against homes agents sold that they owned themselves. On a typical home sale, a roughly 6% commission split among agents means an agent who gets a client $10,000 more for a home earns only a few hundred extra dollars for the additional effort and delay of holding out — not worth it to the agent even though it's very much worth it to the seller. Levitt found that agents kept their own homes on the market several days longer on average and sold them for a meaningfully higher price than comparable homes they sold for clients — consistent with agents pushing their own clients toward a faster sale at a lower price than they'd accept for themselves, because the agent's commission on the difference isn't worth the agent's own time.

## Practical application
Before taking advice from anyone paid on commission, a flat fee, or a fixed structure that doesn't scale with your outcome, work out on paper what their payoff actually looks like at different results for you — do they gain much more from you accepting the fast, easy option than from you holding out for the better one? If their curve is flatter than yours, treat their advice as directionally useful but re-anchor the final call on your own numbers, not theirs. This doesn't mean assuming bad faith by default — it means asking a concrete question before you rely on someone's judgment: at the moment they're advising you, does taking the extra time or risk to get you the better outcome actually pay them enough to be worth it to them?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a time you took an expert's advice — an agent, an advisor, a contractor — that in hindsight served their incentive more than yours. What would you have asked differently if you'd mapped out their payoff structure first?
