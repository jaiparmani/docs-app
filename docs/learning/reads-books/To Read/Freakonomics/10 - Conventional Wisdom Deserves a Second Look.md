# 10 — Conventional Wisdom Deserves a Second Look

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Levitt and Dubner close the book by naming what actually ties its scattered chapters together: not a single thesis about crime, or parenting, or names, but a repeatable posture toward the world — treat "everybody knows" explanations as hypotheses to test against data rather than conclusions to accept, and follow incentives to wherever they actually point, even when that's somewhere uncomfortable or unglamorous. Conventional wisdom, in their telling, tends to persist less because it's true and more because it's convenient, dramatic, or flattering to whoever's repeating it — and it rarely survives contact with a careful look at the actual numbers.

## Why it matters
Every case study in the book is really a demonstration of the same move applied to a different domain: cheating teachers and sumo wrestlers, self-interested experts, an organization built on secrecy, a street gang's real ledger, parenting outcomes, first names, and the crime drop — each one takes something people assumed they already understood and shows that the real mechanism was different from, and often more interesting than, the popular explanation. The abortion-crime controversy from the previous chunk isn't a flaw in that project; it's the project working correctly, since being willing to have your own famous conclusion challenged by more data is the same standard the book applies to everyone else's conventional wisdom.

## Example from the book
The authors point back to the criminologists and commentators of the early 1990s who confidently predicted an oncoming wave of juvenile "superpredator" crime based on demographic trends — a prediction that turned out to be almost exactly backward, as crime rates fell sharply through the decade instead. That failed prediction functions as the book's closing cautionary tale: expert consensus, delivered with total confidence and treated as common knowledge at the time, was wrong not because the experts were unintelligent, but because they were reasoning from a familiar narrative instead of asking what the underlying incentives and data actually showed — the exact mistake the entire book has spent its chapters trying to model an alternative to.

## Practical application
Pick one belief you currently hold with real confidence — about your industry, your field, a social pattern, anything — that you've never actually traced back to data versus simply absorbed as "what everyone knows." Spend twenty minutes trying to find out whether the belief holds up: what data would you expect to see if it were true, and does that data actually exist and say what you assume it says? The habit matters more than any single answer — it's the same one this whole book has been trying to install.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Looking back across all ten chunks, which single case study most changed how you think about a piece of "everybody knows" wisdom in your own life or field — and have you actually gone and checked the data behind that piece of wisdom yet, or just felt convinced?
