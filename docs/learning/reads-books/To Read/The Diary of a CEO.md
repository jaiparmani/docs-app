# The Diary of a CEO

<small>3 min read</small>

*Steven Bartlett*

Bartlett built *The Diary of a CEO* into one of the most-listened-to business podcasts by interviewing a wide range of entrepreneurs, scientists, athletes, and public figures over several years; this book is his attempt to distill recurring patterns from those conversations into "33 laws" — a numbered-rules format covering things like self-belief, discipline, relationships, and decision-making under uncertainty. The premise is aggregation: rather than one operator's account of their own company (the Horowitz model) or one founder's own memoir (the Knight model), this is a curator's synthesis pulled across hundreds of other people's tested experience, organized into a framework Bartlett presents as generalizable law rather than personal anecdote.

## The case for it

If the aggregation actually works, there's a real argument for this format: a single operator's memoir is one data point, however well-told, while years of interviewing successful people across very different domains gives Bartlett a wider aperture to notice which pieces of advice actually recur versus which are idiosyncratic to one person's circumstances. The podcast's guest list is genuinely broad, spanning founders, scientists, athletes, and psychologists rather than staying inside one industry, which is the kind of range that could plausibly surface patterns a narrower business book wouldn't catch. Readers who already listen to the podcast are likely to find this a useful consolidation of ideas they've heard scattered across dozens of episodes into one organized reference.

## The tension worth naming upfront

The same thing that makes the aggregation appealing is also the book's central risk. Bartlett isn't reporting his own tested track record the way Horowitz or Knight are — he's synthesizing secondhand what dozens of guests said in conversation, then repackaging it as his own numbered "laws." That's a fundamentally different kind of authority than a founder writing about the company they actually ran: it's curatorial rather than experiential, and podcast-to-book compilations in general have a mixed track record because the connective tissue between guests' original, context-specific claims and a tidy universal "law" is supplied by the author after the fact, not tested by him in practice. A law derived from one guest's experience running a hardware startup and another law derived from a psychologist's research on habit formation can end up sitting side by side with the same rhetorical confidence, even though they rest on very different kinds of evidence. It's also worth noting Bartlett's own primary credential going in is as a media entrepreneur and podcaster rather than an operator with a long track record in the specific domains — sales, product, hiring — that some of the "laws" address, which is a different kind of authority than the person actually having built and scaled the thing being advised on. None of that means the content is wrong, but it's a reasonable prior to hold going in: treat individual laws as prompts worth testing against your own situation rather than as validated doctrine, and expect some unevenness in depth and rigor between chapters, since that unevenness is close to structurally guaranteed by a book built from years of disparate guest conversations rather than one person's own consistent throughline.
