# 02 — Thrown From a Moving Taxi

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book opens not with his birth but with a much later scene: Noah, his mother, and his younger half-brother riding home from church in a minibus taxi when the driver turns threatening, and Patricia makes a split-second decision to get her children out of the vehicle while it's still moving rather than wait to find out how the situation ends. It's an extreme, almost cinematic opening for a memoir, and Noah uses it deliberately — before the reader learns anything else about his childhood, they learn that his mother's love expressed itself as decisive, sometimes frightening action, not comfort or reassurance.

## Why it matters
This scene sets the emotional register for the whole book's portrait of Patricia. She is not a soft, protective presence in the conventional sense — she's someone who assesses danger fast, trusts her own judgment over authority or convention, and acts on it immediately, even when the action itself looks dangerous from the outside. That combination — fierce love expressed through risk-tolerant, sometimes unilateral decisions — recurs throughout the book, in her choices about religion, discipline, and eventually in how she handled an abusive marriage. Understanding her this early primes the reader to read her later choices as consistent with who she already was, rather than as a departure.

## Example from the book
The taxi driver had grown belligerent, and Patricia recognized the situation had crossed from merely uncomfortable into genuinely dangerous. Rather than continue negotiating or waiting for the ride to end, she made the call to jump — pushing her children out of the moving vehicle and following them, prioritizing getting away over the risk of injury from the jump itself. Noah recounts the scene from a child's confused, adrenaline-flooded perspective: he doesn't fully understand why it's happening in the moment, only that his mother has decided, and that her decision is final and immediate.

## Practical application
As a way of thinking about decisions under uncertainty, the scene is a useful model for distinguishing between the cost of an ambiguous risk and the cost of a certain one. Patricia doesn't wait for proof that the danger is real before acting — she acts on a fast, informal read of the situation, accepting a smaller certain risk (jumping from a moving vehicle) over an unknown, possibly much larger one (staying). It's a useful frame outside the literal scenario too: when a situation is escalating and the safe-feeling choice is actually "wait and see," it's worth asking whether that's genuine patience or just a preference for a risk that hasn't been named yet.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a situation in your own life where "wait and see" feels safe mainly because the risk of waiting hasn't been named out loud yet?
