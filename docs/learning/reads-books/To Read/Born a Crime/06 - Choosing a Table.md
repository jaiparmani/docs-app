# 06 — Choosing a Table

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
By the time Noah reaches high school, apartheid has formally ended, but its social geometry hasn't. He describes a multiracial school where the student body is legally free to mix but still self-segregates almost completely by lunchtime — Black students at one set of tables, white students at another, coloured students at a third, and so on — an informal map of a country still sorting itself along the old lines even after the laws requiring it were gone. Because Noah could pass linguistically and socially into more than one group, he had a choice most of his classmates didn't: which table to sit at, and by extension, which identity to perform on a given day.

## Why it matters
This episode captures something the earlier legal history can't: how thoroughly apartheid's categories outlived apartheid itself, migrating from law into unwritten social habit. It also puts Noah in a genuinely uncomfortable position rather than a privileged one — having access to multiple tables didn't mean he fully belonged at any of them, and the freedom to move between groups came with the cost of never being fully claimed by one. The chapter complicates any simple reading of his multilingualism and code-switching as pure advantage; it's also a description of a specific kind of loneliness.

## Example from the book
Noah describes physically observing the lunchroom's self-imposed segregation on his first days at the school, then having to decide, deliberately, where he'd sit — a decision none of his classmates who read as unambiguously one race or another had to make in the same way. He moves between groups over time rather than settling permanently into one, staying legible to multiple communities but not fully embedded in any single one, a pattern that echoes the book's broader argument that his mixed status was less a bridge between two fixed worlds than its own separate, often solitary position.

## Practical application
The transferable lesson is about the real cost of being "the person who can talk to everyone." It's often read as a pure social asset, but Noah's account suggests it can also mean never being fully inside any one group's trust, always slightly a guest at every table. Worth applying to anyone who occupies that role in a workplace or community: fluency across groups is valuable, but it's worth checking in on whether it's coming at the cost of a home base, and being deliberate about building one somewhere rather than assuming broad access adds up to belonging.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you're someone who can move fluidly between different groups, do you actually have a table that's yours — or just standing access to everyone else's?
