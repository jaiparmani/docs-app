# 05 — The Mathematics of Race

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Apartheid didn't just discriminate by race — it required a formal bureaucracy to decide what race a given person legally was, using criteria that ranged from ancestry paperwork to physical tests applied to individual bodies. Noah lays out how absurd and arbitrary this classification system was in practice, and how his own existence — visibly mixed, legally awkward to sort — exposed the seams in a system that depended on everyone fitting cleanly into one of a handful of boxes: white, Black, coloured, Indian. He didn't fit any of them without friction, and the book uses his specific case to make the system's underlying incoherence visible.

## Why it matters
It's easy to think of a rigid racial hierarchy as at least internally consistent, even if it's morally monstrous. Noah's account undercuts that assumption — the system wasn't just cruel, it was also badly engineered, full of edge cases, inconsistencies, and decisions that came down to a bureaucrat's snap judgment about someone's hair or skin tone. Seeing race treated as a math problem with a wrong answer, rather than as a natural category, is central to how the book denaturalizes apartheid: it wasn't a description of reality, it was a system imposed on top of reality that reality kept refusing to fit.

## Example from the book
Noah describes the "coloured" category itself as a kind of dumping ground the state used for anyone who didn't cleanly register as white, Black, or Indian — a legal identity assigned to him at birth largely because a category had to be found for his specific parentage. He grew up around family and community who were legally Black in most of the ways that mattered to daily life, while carrying a different official classification on paper — a mismatch he describes navigating constantly, since the label the state gave him didn't match the world he actually lived in day to day.

## Practical application
The useful habit here is treating any confident-sounding classification system — not just racial categories, but organizational labels, demographic buckets, even personality-type frameworks — as something to test against its edge cases rather than accept at face value. A system that works cleanly for the median case but breaks down, arbitrarily and often cruelly, for anyone who doesn't fit it neatly is telling you something about its design, not about the people it fails to classify. Noah's life is, among other things, a sustained edge case that the system never adequately accounted for.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's a classification system in your own world — professional, social, institutional — that you've never actually tested against its edge cases, and might not survive the test?
