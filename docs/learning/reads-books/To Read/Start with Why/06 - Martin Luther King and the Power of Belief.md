# 06 — Martin Luther King and the Power of Belief

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Sinek uses Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech at the March on Washington to illustrate Why-driven leadership at the level of a single person rather than a company. Roughly 250,000 people traveled to Washington, D.C. on August 28, 1963, to hear him speak — in an era with no social media, no mass-texting, and none of the event-promotion infrastructure a gathering that size would use today. Sinek's point is that they didn't show up for King specifically, in the sense of being loyal to him as an individual — they showed up because he articulated a belief many of them already held, and gave them a moment and a place to act on it together. King, in this framing, wasn't selling people on a belief they didn't have; he was giving voice to one they already carried, which is why so many people could be mobilized so quickly around a single unamplified event.

## Why it matters
This example extends the book's argument beyond marketing into leadership more broadly: the same Why-first mechanism that Sinek argues drives brand loyalty also explains why some leaders can move large numbers of people to act, sometimes at real personal cost, while others with more formal authority or resources cannot. Sinek's framing is that people don't follow a Why-driven leader for the leader's own sake — they follow because the leader's stated belief matches something they already wanted for themselves, and following becomes a way of acting on their own conviction, not a favor done for the leader. This is also why he treats leadership and marketing as versions of the same underlying phenomenon throughout the book, rather than as separate skills.

## Example from the book
Sinek contrasts what he says King's speech was not: it wasn't a detailed ten-point policy plan, and it wasn't the only or even the most detailed civil rights strategy circulating at the time. Other people had well-developed plans; what King had, in Sinek's account, was language that let a belief already shared by hundreds of thousands of people crystallize into something they could act on together, in the same place, at the same time. Sinek uses this to argue that clarity of belief, articulated well, can mobilize people that a purely procedural or policy-first pitch — however well-reasoned — often cannot.

## Practical application
Next time you need to move a group of people toward action — a team, a community, an audience — separate the plan from the belief before you communicate either. Check whether you're leading with the plan (the What: here's what we're going to do) or with the belief the plan serves (the Why: here's what we think should be true, and this is how we get there). Sinek's argument is that the second version recruits people who are already primed to want that outcome, rather than asking them to evaluate a proposal cold.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a time you showed up for something — an event, a cause, a person — mostly because it matched something you already believed, rather than because you were persuaded on the spot. What was it about the ask that let you recognize your own belief in it?
