# 08 — The Split

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Sinek describes what tends to happen to Why as an organization grows past the point where its founder can be personally involved in every decision. In a small, young organization, the founder's Why doesn't need to be written down anywhere — it's felt, because the founder is in the room, modeling it directly in how decisions get made. As the organization scales and adds layers of people who never worked directly with the founder, that felt sense of Why doesn't automatically transfer. Without deliberate effort to articulate and keep communicating it, the organization's day-to-day behavior gradually re-centers on What — quarterly numbers, output, process compliance — because What is far easier to measure, delegate, and manage at scale than Why is. Sinek calls the resulting gap between the founder's original purpose and the organization's actual daily operations "the split."

## Why it matters
The split matters because it's presented as close to the default trajectory of growth, not a rare failure mode — success itself is what creates the conditions for it, by adding the scale and management layers that dilute a felt sense of purpose into an unstated assumption nobody articulates anymore. Sinek's argument is that this is often invisible from the inside: the organization can still be growing, still hitting its What-based targets, while the Why that originally made it distinctive quietly stops driving actual decisions. Customers and employees, in his account, often notice the split before leadership does — a brand or company starts to feel different, more transactional, even while its output looks the same on paper.

## Example from the book
Sinek frames the split as a leadership-succession problem as much as a scale problem: a founder who dies, retires, or is replaced by a caretaker CEO focused on operational metrics can trigger a fast split, because the next generation of leadership may know the company's What and How in detail without ever having felt the original Why the way the founder did. He argues the organizations that avoid this outcome are the ones where a leader — not necessarily the original founder, but someone in the founder's role — treats keeping the Why alive and legible to new employees as an ongoing, explicit job, not something that's supposed to be self-evident from the company's history.

## Practical application
If you lead any group larger than the few people who were there at the start, don't assume the founding purpose is still being felt by everyone acting in the organization's name. Make it explicit and repeat it — in hiring, in how decisions get explained, in what gets celebrated — rather than trusting that culture transmits Why on its own as headcount grows. If you're inside an organization and sense it's drifted toward What without you being able to name when that happened, that drift is itself the split Sinek describes, and naming it is the first step back.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of an organization, team, or community you've watched grow. Did its founding purpose stay legible to new members as it scaled, or did it quietly get replaced by metrics and process without anyone deciding that on purpose?
