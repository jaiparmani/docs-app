# 07 — The Law of Diffusion of Innovation

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Sinek borrows a well-established adoption curve from diffusion-of-innovation research and reframes it through the Why lens. Any population adopting a new idea, product, or technology splits roughly into five segments, from earliest to latest adopters: innovators, early adopters, an early majority, a late majority, and laggards. The innovators and early adopters — a relatively small slice of the total population — are the people who will try something new because they believe in it, before it's proven, convenient, or socially safe to do so. The early and late majority, who together make up most of any market, are fundamentally different: they wait for validation, and they tend to adopt something because other people (often the early adopters) already have, not because they were independently persuaded by the underlying idea. Sinek's argument is that Why-first communication is precisely what recruits the innovators and early adopters — the majority isn't listening for a belief yet, they're watching to see who else jumps in first.

## Why it matters
This reframes a common strategic mistake: designing a pitch, product, or launch aimed at winning over the broad middle of the market first, when the middle of the market structurally doesn't move until it sees the believers move first. Sinek's implication is that you can't skip the early-adopter stage by simply making a better What or How — you have to win the smaller group of people who will buy in on belief alone, because they're the visible proof the majority is implicitly waiting for. Once that early slice tips — often cited in diffusion research as somewhere in the range of 15 to 18 percent of the total market — the majority tends to follow much faster, because at that point adoption itself has become the safer, more socially normal choice.

## Example from the book
Sinek applies the curve to Apple's product launches and to technology adoption generally: a new Apple product typically has a line of committed early adopters at launch, visibly using and talking about it, well before it becomes commonplace — and it's that visible early cohort, not a mass advertising campaign alone, that Sinek credits with pulling the broader market in afterward. He contrasts this with companies that try to launch straight to mass appeal, skipping the belief-first phase, and typically get a more transactional, price-sensitive response because they never built the initial cohort of people who'd advocate for the product on conviction rather than convenience.

## Practical application
If you're introducing something new — a product, an idea inside an organization, a personal project you want others to join — resist optimizing your pitch for the skeptical middle first. Identify who your innovators and early adopters actually are (the people predisposed to believe in the underlying Why before there's proof it works), win them explicitly, and treat their visible enthusiasm as the mechanism that will eventually reach the majority, rather than trying to persuade the majority directly and hoping the early adopters follow.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of something you adopted early, before most people around you had. What convinced you — a belief you already held that it matched, or seeing someone else adopt it first? And which side of that curve are you usually on?
