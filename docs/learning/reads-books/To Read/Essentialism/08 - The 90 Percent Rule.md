# 08 — The 90 Percent Rule

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
McKeown's signature selection tool for the Eliminate phase: when evaluating an option — a project, an opportunity, a request — score it from 0 to 100 against the criteria that actually matter to you. If it doesn't score at least a 90, treat it as a 0 and reject it outright, rather than accepting it because it's decent, convenient, or better than nothing. The rule is deliberately extreme: it's designed to filter out the large middle category of options that are genuinely good but not great, which McKeown argues are more dangerous to an essentialist life than obviously bad options, precisely because "good" is tempting enough to say yes to and therefore crowds out the truly essential.

## Why it matters
Most people already reject obviously bad opportunities without needing a rule for it. The actual problem is the flood of *good* options — the requests, projects, and invitations that are clearly worthwhile in isolation and would be a mistake to decline if evaluated on their own merits. Without an extreme threshold, a reasonable person will accumulate dozens of individually defensible "yeses" that collectively leave no room for anything exceptional. The 90 percent rule works because it forces a comparison against your best possible option, not against doing nothing — a genuinely good opportunity looks easy to accept until you hold it up against what you'd have to give up to take it.

## Example from the book
McKeown frames this as a criterion to apply specifically to the choices that feel hardest to say no to — the ones with no obvious downside, like a flattering opportunity or a well-paying but only tangentially relevant project — precisely because those are the ones an unstructured "does this seem reasonable" evaluation will always pass. By forcing a numeric score against your own explicit criteria, and setting the bar at 90 rather than 60 or 70, the rule is built to reject almost everything that would otherwise sail through on the strength of being merely good, leaving room only for the small number of opportunities that are close to a perfect fit.

## Practical application
Next time you're deciding whether to take on something — a project, a meeting, a commitment — write down two or three criteria that actually matter to you for that category of decision, then score the option against them out of 100 before you decide. If it doesn't clear 90, decline it, even if your gut says it's a reasonable use of your time. Pay particular attention to options that feel hard to say no to specifically because they're good, not bad — those are the ones this rule is built for.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of something you said yes to recently that wasn't a clear "no" but also wasn't a resounding "yes." If you'd had to score it against your own criteria before answering, would it have cleared 90?
