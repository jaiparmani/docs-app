# 03 — The Paradox of Success

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
McKeown lays out a pattern he sees repeatedly in careers and organizations, which he calls the paradox of success, and it runs in four steps: clarity of purpose leads to success; success creates more options and opportunities; more options and opportunities lead to diffused efforts across all of them; and diffused efforts undermine the very clarity that produced the success in the first place. In other words, doing well at something is often what makes it hardest to keep doing well at it — not because the person or organization got worse, but because success itself generates so many new demands, invitations, and directions that the original focus quietly erodes under the weight of its own results.

## Why it matters
This explains a failure mode that pure willpower or better time management can't fix, because the problem isn't laziness or disorganization — it's that success is actively working against continued focus by manufacturing more good options than anyone could reasonably pursue. Without a deliberate counter-effort, a person's or company's trajectory tends to drift from a sharp, differentiated thing toward a diluted, mediocre version of many things, simply because each new opportunity looked reasonable to accept in isolation. Recognizing the pattern is what makes it possible to interrupt: the antidote isn't becoming more capable of handling more, it's getting more disciplined about turning down the very opportunities that success creates.

## Example from the book
McKeown frames this less as a single anecdote and more as a pattern he asks readers to check against their own experience: think of a project, a role, or a company that started with a sharp, narrow focus and got real traction because of it — and then, as it grew successful, absorbed steadily more responsibilities, side projects, and stakeholders, until the thing that made it work in the first place got crowded out by the demands its own success had generated. He treats this as close to inevitable unless someone actively pushes back against it, because nothing about becoming successful naturally makes an organization or a person more selective — if anything, success makes new commitments easier to justify, not harder.

## Practical application
Look at whatever is currently going well for you — a skill, a project, a role — and list what's been added to it since it started succeeding: new responsibilities, new requests, new adjacent opportunities that seemed reasonable to accept one at a time. Ask which of those additions are actually extending the original thing that worked, and which are quietly diluting it. Then pick one addition to actively remove or decline, specifically because it arrived as a result of success rather than because it was part of the original plan.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there something in your life right now that's succeeding specifically because it's narrow — and is that success currently attracting the kind of additions that would eventually make it broad, diffuse, and less effective?
