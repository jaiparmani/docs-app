# 06 — Sleep: The Essentialist's Asset

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
McKeown treats sleep as a strategic asset rather than a personal indulgence, and directly pushes back against the culture that treats exhaustion as proof of commitment. His argument is straightforward: the ability to discern what's essential — to explore clearly, evaluate options well, and eliminate the right things — depends on a functioning brain, and sleep deprivation degrades exactly the judgment an essentialist needs most. Protecting sleep isn't in tension with getting more done; it's a precondition for being able to correctly choose what's worth doing in the first place. Sacrificing it to squeeze in more hours of work is, in his framing, a Nonessentialist trade — more volume of effort at the cost of the discernment needed to spend that effort well.

## Why it matters
This reframes a habit that's usually treated as purely personal — how much you sleep — as directly connected to the book's central skill of selection. A tired brain doesn't evaluate trade-offs well; it defaults to whatever's easiest or loudest, which is the opposite of essentialist decision-making. McKeown's point is that the payoff from protecting sleep isn't felt as rest, it's felt the next time you have to judge whether something is truly essential — and a depleted decision-maker will systematically make worse calls about what to keep and what to cut, no matter how good their frameworks are on paper.

## Example from the book
McKeown contrasts two ways of relating to sleep: the common one, where getting by on little sleep is worn as a badge of how hard someone is working, and the essentialist one, where protecting sleep is itself treated as one of the highest-leverage things a person can do, precisely because so much of the rest of the book's advice — clear exploration, careful elimination, disciplined execution — depends on having the cognitive capacity to actually do it well. He positions the choice to protect sleep as one of the clearest tests of whether someone has actually internalized "less but better," since it means deliberately doing less in the moment (fewer hours awake, fewer hours worked) in service of doing better overall.

## Practical application
Pick one night this week to protect a full night's sleep as a non-negotiable commitment, the same way you'd protect an important meeting — not by hoping it happens, but by actually ending your work and screens early enough to make it possible. The next day, pay attention to how your judgment about what matters differs from a day you're running on less sleep. Use that difference as evidence, not just for how you feel, but for how well you're actually discerning what's essential.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Do you currently treat sleep as something you protect on purpose, or as the first thing that gets sacrificed when everything else feels urgent — and which of those two choices actually produces better decisions the next day?
