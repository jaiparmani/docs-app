# 02 — Less But Better: The Disciplined Pursuit of Less

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book's central thesis, and the phrase McKeown returns to constantly: **less but better**. Most self-improvement and productivity advice is really about the undisciplined pursuit of *more* — more done, more efficiently, more crammed into the same hours — on the assumption that the volume of what you accomplish is what matters. McKeown argues the actual lever isn't efficiency, it's selection: identify the vital few things that contribute disproportionately, and eliminate or decline nearly everything else, rather than trying to make room for everything by doing each thing faster. Essentialism isn't minimalism for its own sake — it's a trade-off logic, where every yes to something is understood as a real no to something else, rather than a free addition that stacks on top of what you're already doing.

## Why it matters
This is the idea that makes every later chapter make sense as a system rather than a pile of separate tips. If you think of your capacity as effectively unlimited — that you can absorb one more commitment without giving anything up — then techniques for saying no or eliminating projects will feel optional, something to reach for only when things get bad. But if you accept that capacity is fixed and every addition displaces something, then selection stops being a nice-to-have skill and becomes the actual determinant of whether your effort goes toward what matters. The payoff McKeown claims isn't just less stress — it's more genuine impact, because effort concentrated on a few things compounds in a way that effort scattered across many things never does.

## Example from the book
McKeown points to the word "priority" itself as a small piece of evidence for how far this thinking has drifted. It entered the English language in the 1400s as a singular noun — there was one prior thing, by definition — and it stayed singular for roughly five hundred years. It wasn't until the 1900s that people began pluralizing it into "priorities," as if an organization or a person could have several *first* things at once. He treats this as more than a linguistic curiosity: it's a symptom of exactly the Nonessentialist confusion from chunk 01 — the quiet assumption that everything important can simply be labeled a top priority without anyone having to actually choose between them.

## Practical application
Take your current list of "priorities" — for work, for the week, for the year — and count how many there actually are. If it's more than two or three, you likely don't have priorities in the original sense of the word; you have a list of things you haven't yet ranked against each other. Pick the single most essential item on that list and ask what you'd need to stop doing, or say no to, in order to give it disproportionate attention rather than equal attention alongside everything else.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you were only allowed to call one thing on your current to-do list a "priority" — in the original, singular sense of the word — which one would survive, and what does that tell you about the rest of the list?
