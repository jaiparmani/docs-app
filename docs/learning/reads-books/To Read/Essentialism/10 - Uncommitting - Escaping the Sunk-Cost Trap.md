# 10 — Uncommitting: Escaping the Sunk-Cost Trap

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The 90 percent rule and the graceful no both apply to decisions in front of you — but McKeown points out that some of the least essential things in a person's life aren't new requests at all, they're old commitments that no longer clear the bar and never get reevaluated, because stopping feels like admitting the original investment was wasted. This is the sunk-cost trap: continuing something specifically because of what's already been put into it, rather than because of what it's actually worth going forward. His antidote is what he calls a reverse pilot — instead of agonizing over whether to fully cut something, deliberately stop doing it for a defined trial period and see what actually breaks. If nothing meaningful is lost, that's your answer.

## Why it matters
Elimination isn't a one-time act applied only to new opportunities — without a mechanism for reevaluating existing commitments, a person's list of obligations only ever grows, because everything that was once accepted quietly earns permanent status simply by already being underway. The sunk-cost bias makes this worse: the more time, money, or effort already invested in something, the harder it psychologically feels to stop, even when continuing has nothing to do with the thing's actual current value. The reverse pilot works because it reframes stopping as a reversible experiment rather than an irreversible admission of failure, which removes much of the emotional resistance to testing whether something is still essential.

## Example from the book
McKeown frames the reverse pilot as the mirror image of how organizations normally test new initiatives — running a small pilot before committing fully. Applied to something already running, the same logic works in reverse: rather than debating in the abstract whether a recurring meeting, a service, or a commitment is still worth keeping, stop it for a set period and observe what actually happens. He argues most people skip this test entirely and instead keep pouring effort into things purely because of what's already been invested — the classic sunk-cost pattern — rather than asking the forward-looking question of whether they'd start this commitment today, knowing what they now know.

## Practical application
Pick one recurring commitment you've kept going mostly out of inertia or because of what you've already invested in it, and run a genuine reverse pilot: stop it for two or three weeks and see what actually breaks. If the answer is "nothing meaningful," treat that as real evidence it wasn't essential, and let it go rather than quietly resuming it out of habit.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there something you're still doing mainly because of how much you've already put into it, rather than because you'd choose to start it today if it didn't already exist — and what would a two-week reverse pilot actually reveal?
