# 07 — Emotional Regulation as a Business Skill

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book treats managing your own nervous system — anxiety, panic, imposter syndrome — not as a private, personal issue kept separate from "real" business skills, but as one of the central skills that actually determines outcomes. The argument is straightforward: decision quality under pressure is most of what running anything actually is, and decision quality under pressure is directly downstream of whether you can regulate your own state.

## Why it matters
Much business and self-help writing treats mental health as a wellness footnote, addressed separately from strategy and execution. This book puts it closer to the center, arguing that a founder who is dysregulated — anxious, panicked, spiraling — will make measurably worse calls than the same founder in a regulated state, on the same information. That reframes emotional regulation from a "nice to have" into something closer to a load-bearing operational skill, on par with reading a balance sheet or hiring well, because it sits upstream of every other skill's actual execution.

## Example from the book
Bartlett writes candidly about experiencing panic attacks and periods of serious anxiety while running Social Chain in his twenties, including stretches where the external picture — a fast-growing company, visible success — looked nothing like his internal state. He frames learning to recognize and work with that anxiety, rather than hiding it as a shameful secret disqualifying him from being seen as a capable leader, as itself one of the book's "laws," on the argument that founders who conceal this rather than actively manage it don't just feel worse — they make worse decisions, because the concealment itself consumes attention and distorts judgment.

## Practical application
Build one small, repeatable practice for checking your own state before high-stakes decisions or difficult conversations — something as simple as naming, out loud or on paper, whether you're about to decide from a regulated or dysregulated place. The book's underlying claim is that the decision is never independent of that state, so making the state visible to yourself first is itself a decision-quality intervention. Where possible, delay decisions you notice you're making from a dysregulated place until the state has actually changed, not just until you've talked yourself out of noticing it.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of your worst recent decision — was it actually a bad decision, or a reasonable decision made from a dysregulated state?
