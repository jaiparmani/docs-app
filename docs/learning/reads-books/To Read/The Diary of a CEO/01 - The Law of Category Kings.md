# 01 — The Law of Category Kings

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A recurring argument across the book: the businesses that win biggest usually aren't the best version of an existing thing, competing feature-by-feature against incumbents — they're the first, or the most definitive, version of a new category, to the point that the category and the brand become interchangeable in people's minds. Bartlett frames "category king" status as more defensible than fighting for share inside an established market, because a true category king isn't competing for a slice of an existing pie. It doesn't compete for a slice of "ride-hailing" or "food delivery" — it becomes what those words mean to most people who use them.

## Why it matters
This distinguishes two very different competitive postures. One is playing a strong second-mover's game: better features, better price, better service than whoever's already there. The other is defining a new mental category before anyone else gets to it. Category kings get disproportionate attention, word-of-mouth, and pricing power for a structural reason, not just a marketing one: customers aren't comparing them to alternatives, because for a while there often isn't a clean alternative to compare them to. The law's practical use is as a diagnostic — it forces the question of whether a venture's entire pitch depends on winning a comparison, or whether it could instead make the comparison irrelevant.

## Example from the book
Bartlett returns repeatedly to his own early path building Social Chain, which he started not as a traditional marketing agency trying to out-compete established ad firms on their own turf, but from a very different starting point — building large, engaged social media audiences at a time when "social-first marketing agency" barely existed as a recognized category of business at all. Because the category itself hadn't been defined yet, Bartlett wasn't fighting incumbents on their terms; he was, in effect, deciding what winning would even mean in a space that was still being invented. He uses this experience to make a broader point that recurs across many of the founders he's interviewed on the podcast: the biggest wins tend to go to whoever gets to define the category, not whoever eventually gets best at competing inside someone else's.

## Practical application
Run a simple stress test on whatever you're building: if you removed every point of comparison to a competitor, would customers still have a clear reason to choose it — or does the entire pitch depend on being measured against alternative X (cheaper, faster, better reviewed)? If the honest answer is the latter, look for a narrower sub-category you could plausibly own outright, even a small one, rather than continuing to compete purely on the existing terms. Owning a small category beats being a strong also-ran in a big one.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What would your business have to become for comparisons to stop being the reason people choose it?
