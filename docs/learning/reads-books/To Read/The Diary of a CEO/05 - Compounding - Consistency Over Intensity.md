# 05 — Compounding: Consistency Over Intensity

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Sustainable output over a long horizon beats short bursts of intensity, because compounding rewards time in the system more than it rewards any single peak effort. Bartlett grounds this in the growth of Diary of a CEO itself: he attributes the show's audience growth not to any one viral episode, but to years of consistent, unglamorous weekly output.

## Why it matters
This pushes back on the "overnight success" narrative and the burnout-inducing belief that meaningful results require constant maximum effort. Instead, it treats reliable, sustainable presence as the actual growth engine — because compounding processes need uninterrupted time to accumulate, and a person who burns out and stops after an intense sprint often ends up behind someone who simply never stopped showing up at a lower, sustainable intensity. It also reframes what "discipline" is actually for: not maximum output on any given day, but protecting the unbroken chain of days the compounding depends on.

## Example from the book
Bartlett describes recording and releasing podcast episodes on a fixed weekly cadence for a long stretch before the show had any significant audience, with no clear external evidence during much of that period that the effort was paying off. He argues explicitly that had he treated those early years as a sprint — pushing at an unsustainable pace and burning out, or stopping once results were slow to appear — the compounding that eventually made the show one of the most listened-to business podcasts would never have had enough time to accumulate. The consistency, not any single episode or moment, is what he credits.

## Practical application
For any goal you're currently tempted to attack with a short burst of intensity, ask what a version of that effort looks like if it had to be sustained for three years, not three weeks. Then check honestly whether you're doing that sustainable version, or a version you already suspect you'll quietly abandon in six weeks — and if it's the latter, scale the intensity down until it's something you can actually keep doing. A smaller commitment you never break beats a larger one you eventually will.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What are you currently doing at an intensity you already know you can't sustain — and what would the sustainable version of it actually look like?
