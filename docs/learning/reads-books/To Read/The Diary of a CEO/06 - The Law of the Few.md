# 06 — The Law of the Few

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Growth and trust, in Bartlett's framing, don't scale by broadcasting to everyone at once — they scale by first earning deep, real trust with a small number of people, who then carry it outward on your behalf. A small, genuine inner circle of advisors, early customers, or collaborators does more for long-term durability than a large, shallow network ever will.

## Why it matters
This pushes against the instinct to network as widely as possible. The law's claim is that depth with a few beats breadth with many, for two compounding reasons: a small trusted circle gives you honest signal — people willing to tell you uncomfortable truths rather than flatter you — and genuine conviction from a handful of people tends to spread further and more credibly than lukewarm reach to a much larger, unconvinced audience. A hundred people who mildly like something rarely outperform ten who are genuinely obsessed with it, because obsession is what actually gets repeated and recommended.

## Example from the book
Bartlett discusses deliberately cultivating a small circle of people he trusts to tell him uncomfortable truths about his business, his podcast, and himself, rather than surrounding himself with people who mostly agree. He credits a meaningful part of Diary of a CEO's early growth to a small number of genuinely convinced early listeners and guests who kept recommending the show on their own initiative, well before any broad marketing push existed — arguing that this kind of organic advocacy from a few true believers is worth more, and travels further, than reach bought or begged from many indifferent ones.

## Practical application
Name the handful of people whose honest, unfiltered opinion you actually trust right now — not people who are simply agreeable, but people who'll tell you something you don't want to hear because they care about the outcome. If that list is empty, or padded with people who mostly nod along, treat closing that gap as a priority before you spend more effort trying to scale reach. Investing in convincing ten people completely is often a better use of a week than a scattershot push toward a thousand indifferent ones.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Who in your life will tell you the truth even when it costs them something socially to say it — and when did you last actually ask them for that?
