# 04 — The Law of 100 Rejections

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
This law reframes rejection from a personal verdict into a probabilistic input. If the realistic base rate for a cold pitch, application, or ask is, say, one in twenty or one in a hundred, then any single "no" isn't a data point about your worth or your idea's quality — it's an expected, already-priced-in cost of doing business. On this framing, the only lever that actually matters is volume and persistence, not how any individual rejection feels.

## Why it matters
A large share of founder and early-career paralysis comes from treating each rejection as diagnostic — as though it tells you something true and final about your idea — rather than as one sample from a distribution where most outcomes were always going to be "no." The reframe doesn't make rejection painless, but it changes what a rejection means: not "stop," but "one closer to the yes the math already implied was coming," provided the underlying approach is sound and you're actually running the numbers game rather than repeating a broken pitch.

## Example from the book
Bartlett discusses the sheer volume of outreach, cold pitching, and repeated asks that underpinned Social Chain's early growth — persistently approaching brands, platforms, and potential partners well past the point most people would have concluded the approach wasn't working. His account of that period treats each rejection as simply moving the count forward, on the premise that the ratio of no's to yeses was roughly fixed and the only real variable he controlled was how many attempts he was willing to make.

## Practical application
Before starting an outreach push, estimate your own realistic base rate from a small early sample rather than assuming it should be much higher. Then set a target number of asks, not a target number of yeses — this turns an emotionally loaded goal ("get a yes") into a mechanical one ("hit the number"), which is far easier to actually execute on a bad day. If your yes rate is well below what a reasonable sample predicts, that's a signal to change the pitch, not proof the whole approach was wrong.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What ask have you stopped making after only a handful of no's — and what would change if you actually knew your real base rate was one in fifty?
