# 09 — Attention as the Real Battleground

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
In a saturated modern market, the book argues, the primary competition for most businesses isn't other companies making a similar product — it's everything else competing for the same finite unit of human attention, since attention has to be captured before any product quality can even be evaluated. A great product nobody notices loses to a mediocre one that gets seen.

## Why it matters
This reorients the question "who is my competitor?" away from same-category rivals and toward anything else competing for the same eyeballs and minutes. On this framing, a coffee brand's real competitor in a given moment might be a phone notification, not another coffee brand — because the notification is what's actually winning the attention that would otherwise have gone toward noticing the coffee brand exists at all. It also explains why businesses with objectively weaker products regularly beat businesses with objectively stronger ones: quality was never the variable being contested first. Attention was, and whoever secured it got to have the quality argument at all.

## Example from the book
Bartlett draws this directly from his own career, which began not in a conventional business but in building large social media audiences from very little — a domain where the entire early game was capturing attention before there was any product at all to sell into it. He describes learning in that period that distribution, meaning the raw ability to get seen, is a skill fully separable from having something good to say, and often has to come first: an audience has to exist before its quality can matter to anyone. He carries that lesson forward into how he talks about Diary of a CEO's own growth, treating the show's reach as something that had to be actively built before the content itself could do any of the work people give it credit for.

## Practical application
Before spending more effort improving your product, ask honestly whether your actual bottleneck is quality or attention. Many teams keep refining something that very few people have even encountered yet, when the higher-leverage fix is a distribution problem, not a quality one — and the two require different work: better content or product doesn't fix an attention problem, and more reach doesn't fix a quality problem. Diagnosing which one you actually have, before investing further, is the whole exercise.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is your current bottleneck really that people don't like what you've made, or that almost nobody has seen it yet?
