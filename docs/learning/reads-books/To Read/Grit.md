# Grit

<small>2 min read</small>

*Angela Duckworth*

Duckworth's central claim is that "grit" — defined specifically as sustained passion and perseverance toward a long-term goal, not just raw effort in the moment — predicts achievement better than talent does, and that grit itself is a distinct, measurable trait separate from IQ or natural ability. The evidence she builds the book around includes West Point cadets (grit scores predicted who made it through the notoriously brutal first summer better than the Academy's own selection metrics did), National Spelling Bee competitors, and a self-report "Grit Scale" she developed as a research instrument. The book's second half turns prescriptive: grit, in her account, isn't fixed — it can be cultivated through deliberate practice, a sense of purpose, and hope in the specific psychological sense of believing effort can change your future, and she extends the argument to how parents and coaches might build it in others.

## Why the underlying research got so much attention

The West Point finding is the book's strongest anchor because it's a hard, real-world outcome — dropout during Beast Barracks — measured against a genuinely novel predictor, and it's the kind of result that's easy to state precisely and hard to dismiss as anecdote. Duckworth is a trained psychologist (the book grew out of her research career and a MacArthur "genius grant" for this work), and the book's appeal is that it gives a vocabulary and a semi-quantified concept for something people already sensed intuitively — that raw talent underperforms relative to people who simply keep showing up — without resorting to pure motivational-speaker abstraction. It's also reasonably honest about its own scope: passion, in her definition, means consistency of interest over years, not intensity of enthusiasm in the moment, which is a more useful and less clichéd definition than the word "passion" usually gets in pop psychology.

## The well-known counter-argument, worth having going in

Grit as a construct has taken real hits in the years since publication. Meta-analyses (notably Credé et al., 2017) found the correlation between grit scores and performance is fairly modest and overlaps heavily with the "conscientiousness" trait already well-established in personality psychology — raising the question of whether grit is a genuinely new construct or a repackaging of something psychology already had a name for. There's also a broader critique, made forcefully by educators and some psychologists, that "grit" as a public-facing idea risks locating the explanation for underachievement inside the individual (did you persevere enough?) rather than in the structural conditions — under-resourced schools, unstable home environments, lack of access — that make sustained effort dramatically harder for some people than others. Duckworth has responded to some of this criticism directly, and it's worth reading the book with that debate in mind rather than taking the West Point and spelling-bee results as the final word on how much grit alone explains.
