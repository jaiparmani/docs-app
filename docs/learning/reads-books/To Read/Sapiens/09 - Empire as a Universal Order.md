# 09 — Empire as a Universal Order

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Harari names empire, alongside money and religion, as one of the three great "universal orders" that have repeatedly unified previously separate peoples across history — a political structure that governs a significant number of distinct peoples, each with its own culture and territory, under one flexible and expanding set of borders. He deliberately strips the word of its purely negative modern connotation: whatever else empires have been — and he's clear-eyed that they've typically been built and sustained through conquest, extraction, and violence — they've also been the primary vehicle through which ideas, technologies, crops, and institutions have spread between peoples who would otherwise have had little or no contact at all.

## Why it matters
This matters because it complicates a simple moral story about empire as pure villain in the history of human unification. Harari's point isn't to excuse imperial violence, which he documents plainly, but to note that most of what today looks like a "national" culture or "native" tradition is, on closer inspection, itself the residue of some earlier imperial project — a language, legal system, religion, or set of borders imposed by conquest generations or centuries earlier that has since been thoroughly absorbed and reclaimed as authentically local. Untangling "authentic" pre-imperial culture from what empire actually built is far harder than nationalist narratives about restoring a pure prior state usually assume.

## Example from the book
Harari points to how thoroughly Hellenistic, Roman, and later imperial cultures were absorbed into the very identities that today present themselves as distinct from and opposed to empire — modern legal systems built on Roman law, alphabets and administrative structures spread by conquest, entire religious and linguistic landscapes reshaped by empires that have long since collapsed. The cultures doing the absorbing rarely experienced this as foreign imposition for more than a generation or two before the imported elements were simply "how things have always been done" — Harari's imagined-order argument applied specifically to the question of what counts as a nation's own heritage.

## Practical application
The useful question to bring to any claim about "traditional" or "native" culture — your own or another's — is how far back the tradition in question actually traces, and whether an earlier imperial or conquering power is somewhere in that lineage, now absorbed and forgotten. Harari's framing doesn't argue this makes the tradition less real or less worth valuing; it argues that the line between "authentically ours" and "imposed by history's winners" is far blurrier, and far more universal a condition, than most cultural narratives admit.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick an element of your own culture that feels distinctly "yours" — a language, a legal concept, a custom. How far back can you trace it, and does an empire or conquest show up anywhere in that history?
