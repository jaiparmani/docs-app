# 12 — Capitalism and the Discovery of the Future

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Harari argues that modern capitalism's real innovation was psychological before it was economic: the willingness to extend trust to the future, in the specific form of credit. Premodern economies largely treated wealth as a fixed pie — if one party gained, another lost — which made large-scale lending to fund new ventures rare and risky, since there was no strong collective belief that the total pie would grow. Capitalism's foundational move was building an economic system around the confident assumption that the future would be larger than the present — that total global wealth would keep expanding — which made it rational, for the first time at real scale, to lend money against profits that didn't exist yet.

## Why it matters
This matters because credit, once trusted at scale, becomes a genuine engine of growth rather than just a bookkeeping trick: a merchant, inventor, or explorer with a promising idea but no capital can borrow against future profits to fund the venture today, and if enough such ventures succeed, the resulting growth justifies the trust that made the lending possible in the first place — a self-reinforcing loop, but one that depends entirely on maintaining collective confidence that growth will continue. Harari is explicit that this is also capitalism's central fragility: the entire system depends on a shared belief about the future that, unlike physical resources, can evaporate suddenly and all at once during a crisis of confidence.

## Example from the book
Harari discusses the Dutch East India Company as a concrete case of this mechanism in action: a joint-stock company that could raise enormous capital from investors by selling shares — bets on future profit from trade and conquest that hadn't happened yet — and used that pooled, credit-based capital to fund fleets, fortresses, and colonial ventures across Asia at a scale no individual merchant or monarch's treasury could have financed alone. The company's power came not from wealth it already possessed, but from collective trust that its future returns would justify the investment made today.

## Practical application
The transferable question is to notice, in any modern venture or system built on credit — a startup funded by investors betting on future profit, a government running on borrowed money against future tax revenue — what specifically the lenders are trusting will be true tomorrow that isn't true yet, and how fragile or well-founded that specific belief actually is. Harari's framing suggests that most large-scale modern growth runs on exactly this kind of forward-looking trust, which makes it worth distinguishing between credit extended against a plausible, examined future and credit extended purely because extending it has become the norm.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Identify something in your own financial or professional life that's essentially a bet on future growth — a loan, an investment, a career choice made on the assumption things will keep expanding. How examined is that assumption, versus how much you're simply trusting the pattern to continue?
