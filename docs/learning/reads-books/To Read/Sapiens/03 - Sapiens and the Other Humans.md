# 03 — Sapiens and the Other Humans

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Homo sapiens was not, for most of its history, the only human species on Earth — a fact Harari treats as one of the book's more disorienting reminders. Neanderthals lived across Europe and western Asia, adapted to cold climates and, on the archaeological evidence, capable of complex tool use and probably basic speech. Homo erectus persisted in Asia for close to two million years, longer than sapiens has existed at all. Smaller species like Homo floresiensis ("the hobbit") survived in isolated pockets. Harari's point is that "human" was, for most of prehistory, a plural category — and sapiens shared the planet with several cousin species for tens of thousands of years after emerging in East Africa.

## Why it matters
It reframes sapiens' rise not as an inevitable march of the smartest species to the top, but as one outcome among several that could plausibly have gone differently — and one that came at a direct cost to close relatives. Every other human species is now extinct, and the timing of most of those extinctions lines up uncomfortably well with sapiens' arrival in their territory. Whether sapiens actively wiped out its cousins, simply outcompeted them for resources, or some mix of both, is a question Harari treats as genuinely open rather than settled, but he's clear that the disappearance wasn't obviously peaceful.

## Example from the book
Harari lays out the two leading theories for what happened when sapiens migrating out of Africa encountered Neanderthals already established across Europe: the "Interbreeding Theory," which holds that the two populations mixed and modern non-African populations are partly descended from Neanderthals, and the "Replacement Theory," which holds that sapiens simply displaced or eliminated Neanderthals with minimal interbreeding. He notes that genetic evidence — comparisons between Neanderthal DNA extracted from fossils and DNA from living humans — points to a small but real amount of interbreeding, with non-African populations carrying a small percentage of Neanderthal-derived DNA, while insisting the bulk of the story still looks more like replacement than merger.

## Practical application
The useful habit of mind here is resisting the tidy version of any origin story. When a narrative about "how we got here" resolves neatly into a single clean cause — the smartest species simply won — it's worth asking what got smoothed over to make the story that clean, and whether the actual evidence supports a cleaner or messier picture. Sapiens' rise to sole surviving human species is a case where the honest answer involves genuinely competing theories, not a settled consensus, and treating it as settled is itself a small act of oversimplification worth noticing.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If sapiens shared the Earth with several other intelligent human species for tens of thousands of years, and none of them survived contact, what does that suggest about how much of sapiens' current dominance is really a story of merit versus a story of circumstance?
