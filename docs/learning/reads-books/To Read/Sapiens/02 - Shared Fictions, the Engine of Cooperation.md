# 02 — Shared Fictions, the Engine of Cooperation

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
This is the load-bearing idea of the entire book: Homo sapiens' single defining advantage over every other species on Earth, including other human species with comparable or larger brains, is the ability to believe collectively in things that exist only in the imagination. Harari calls these "shared fictions" or "imagined orders" — money, nations, corporations, human rights, legal systems, religions. None of these correspond to anything you can point to in the physical world; a corporation has no atoms, a nation has no edges you can touch, a dollar has no intrinsic worth. They exist only because a critical mass of people simultaneously believe they exist, and that collective belief is enough to make them functionally real — real enough to go to war over, real enough to build economies on.

## Why it matters
Chimpanzees, our closest living relatives, can cooperate with the few dozen individuals in their troop that they know personally, but their cooperation collapses past that scale — put a hundred strange chimps in one place and you get chaos, not a functioning society. Sapiens can cooperate flexibly with millions of total strangers they will never meet, because all of them believe in the same fiction: the same currency, the same legal code, the same national identity. This is what let sapiens build pyramids, armies, and trade networks spanning continents, while every other social species on the planet remained capped by how many individuals could personally know each other.

## Example from the book
Harari's clearest illustration is the modern limited liability corporation. Peugeot, the French car company, exists as a legal person — it can own property, sue and be sued, outlive every one of its employees and founders — despite having no body, no mind, and no physical existence whatsoever. If every human on Earth simultaneously stopped believing Peugeot existed as a legal entity, it would vanish instantly, with no rubble left behind, unlike a physical building. Yet for as long as enough people keep believing in it, it behaves, functionally, as if it were as real as a mountain.

## Practical application
Because this is a history and anthropology book rather than a self-help one, the practical move is a way of seeing: when you encounter an institution that feels permanent and objectively real — a currency, a border, a corporate brand, a legal category — try running Harari's test on it. Ask what would happen if everyone simultaneously stopped believing in it. If the answer is "it would simply cease to function," you're looking at a shared fiction, not a fact of nature, and that reframing tends to make otherwise-permanent-feeling institutions look considerably more contingent and more changeable than they first appear.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Name one institution in your own life that you treat as simply real — not a fiction, just how things are. What would have to happen for enough people to stop believing in it that it actually stopped functioning?
