# 07 — Imagined Orders Are Enforced, Not Natural

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Once societies grew past what personal relationships and gossip could hold together, they needed a different kind of glue: an "imagined order" — a set of rules, hierarchies, and values that everyone accepts not because it's objectively true, but because it's been consistently taught, reinforced, and enforced until it feels like simple reality. Harari's key move is refusing to treat any imagined order as more "natural" or "true" than any other, however self-evident it feels from the inside — hierarchies of caste, gender, race, and class are all, on his account, imagined orders rather than facts about the physical world, maintained through education, law, architecture, and, when necessary, force.

## Why it matters
This is a genuinely uncomfortable claim to sit with, because imagined orders don't feel imagined from inside them — they feel like the way things simply are. Harari's point is that this feeling of self-evidence is itself the mechanism by which imagined orders survive: a hierarchy that had to be constantly, consciously justified as "just a useful fiction we've all agreed to" would be far more fragile than one that's been thoroughly internalized as objective fact. The strength of an imagined order is measured precisely by how invisible it's become as a choice.

## Example from the book
Harari places two founding documents side by side: Hammurabi's Code, from roughly 1776 BCE Babylon, which openly organizes society into a hierarchy of superiors, commoners, and slaves with explicitly different values placed on each group's life; and the American Declaration of Independence, roughly 3,500 years later, which asserts that "all men are created equal" and endowed with unalienable rights. Harari's argument is that both documents describe imagined orders rather than objective truths about human beings — there's no physical or biological fact that makes either hierarchy or equality "true" in the way that, say, a chemical formula is true. Both are foundational fictions a society chose to organize itself around, and both required constant institutional reinforcement — law courts, schools, armies — to stay real in practice.

## Practical application
The habit worth building here is noticing which arrangements you treat as simply "how things are" versus which you can readily identify as choices — and then applying the same scrutiny to both categories. Harari's comparison suggests that the arrangements that feel most self-evidently true are often exactly the ones that have been reinforced longest and most successfully, not the ones with the strongest independent justification, and it's worth actively asking what evidence beyond social consensus and reinforcement is actually backing a given "obvious" truth.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick a social hierarchy or value you've always taken as simply natural rather than constructed. What institutions — schools, laws, media, architecture — have been reinforcing it, and what would it take for it to stop feeling self-evident?
