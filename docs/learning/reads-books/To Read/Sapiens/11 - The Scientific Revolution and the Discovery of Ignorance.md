# 11 — The Scientific Revolution and the Discovery of Ignorance

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Harari argues the Scientific Revolution, starting around the 16th century, wasn't primarily a revolution of new knowledge — humans had been accumulating knowledge for millennia — but a revolution of admitted ignorance. Earlier knowledge traditions, religious and philosophical alike, generally assumed that everything important was already known, contained in scripture or classical texts, and the scholar's job was interpretation, not discovery. The genuinely new move was the willingness to say, collectively and institutionally, "we don't know" — and to treat that admission not as an embarrassment but as the starting point for a systematic, evidence-based method of finding out.

## Why it matters
This reframing matters because it locates the Scientific Revolution's power in a psychological and institutional shift rather than a purely technical one — a society has to first believe that admitting ignorance is safe and even admirable before it will invest resources in the kind of open-ended inquiry that produces genuine discovery. Harari connects this directly to why the Scientific Revolution took off specifically in early modern Europe rather than elsewhere: it wasn't superior individual intelligence, but a specific and unusual combination of intellectual humility about current knowledge paired with enormous ambition about what could eventually be known and controlled.

## Example from the book
Harari points to European exploration voyages as physical embodiments of this new stance — expeditions that set out not to confirm existing maps and texts, but explicitly to discover what wasn't yet known, filling in blank spaces on maps that earlier cartographic traditions would have populated with confident, invented detail rather than left honestly empty. That willingness to draw a blank space and admit "we don't know what's here yet" was, in his account, a genuinely new intellectual posture — and one tightly bound up with the exploration and conquest that funded and was funded by the same scientific institutions.

## Practical application
The transferable habit of mind is treating a confident admission of "we don't know" as a sign of intellectual strength rather than weakness, in your own thinking and in institutions you evaluate — Harari's argument suggests that the knowledge traditions that stall are usually the ones that can't tolerate acknowledged gaps, while the ones that advance are the ones that can institutionally reward someone for finding and naming a gap rather than papering over it. Applied to any field or organization, the question worth asking is whether "I don't know" is treated as an acceptable, fundable answer, or as a failure to be hidden.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> In an area of your own work or thinking, are you more often rewarded for projecting confidence about what you already know, or for openly naming what you don't? What would change if the incentive flipped?
