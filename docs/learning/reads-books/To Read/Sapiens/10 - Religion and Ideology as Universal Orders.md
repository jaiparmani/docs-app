# 10 — Religion and Ideology as Universal Orders

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Harari defines religion unusually broadly: any system of human norms and values founded on a belief in a superhuman order, that is not the product of human whim, and that demands a certain code of conduct in response. Under that definition, he treats the shift from polytheism (many local, tribal gods) to monotheism (one universal god, with universal rules applying to everyone regardless of tribe) as the crucial move that let religion, like money and empire, become a genuine unifier of previously separate peoples — because a universal god's rules, unlike a tribal god's, are meant to apply to strangers and insiders alike. He then extends the same "religion" label, provocatively, to modern secular belief systems like liberalism, communism, and even capitalism itself — natural-law ideologies that similarly claim a universal, non-negotiable moral order that isn't up to any individual's personal preference.

## Why it matters
This reframing matters because it disrupts the common modern assumption that "religion" and "secular ideology" belong to two fundamentally different categories — one about faith, the other about reason. Harari's point is that both function the same way sociologically: both assert values as objectively binding rather than optional, both organize large numbers of strangers around shared moral commitments, and both require the same kind of institutional reinforcement — sacred texts, courts, schools, rituals — to stay real across generations. Recognizing secular ideologies as functioning like religions, in this specific structural sense, changes how one reads modern ideological conflict.

## Example from the book
Harari traces the historical shift from tribal polytheism, where each god governed a specific people or place and made no universal claims, to monotheistic traditions asserting one god's rules apply to all humans everywhere — a structural change that made religiously-motivated conversion, and religiously-justified universal moral claims, possible for the first time. He then applies the same "superhuman order, non-negotiable code of conduct" test to communism's belief in historical laws determining society's inevitable direction, and to liberal humanism's belief in inherent, universal human rights — arguing both meet his functional definition of religion just as much as any faith tradition does, whatever their practitioners would say about the comparison.

## Practical application
The applicable habit is running Harari's functional test — is this belief system claiming a universal, non-negotiable moral order beyond any individual's personal say-so? — against ideologies that don't present themselves as religious at all, including ones you personally hold. It's a useful check against treating your own deeply-held convictions as simply "rational" or "obvious" while treating other people's deeply-held convictions as "belief" or "faith" — Harari's framework suggests the two are structurally closer than either side of that comparison typically wants to admit.

## Something to sit with

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Apply Harari's test — a superhuman order, not made up by any individual, demanding a code of conduct — to one of your own strongest political or moral convictions. Does it pass? What does it change to think of it that way?
