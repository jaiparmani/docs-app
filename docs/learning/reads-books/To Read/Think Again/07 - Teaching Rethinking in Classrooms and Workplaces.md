# 07 — Teaching Rethinking in Classrooms and Workplaces

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Grant argues that rethinking isn't just an individual habit — it's a skill institutions can either train or quietly stamp out, depending on how a classroom or workplace is structured. Most schools reward students for arriving at the right answer and defending it, not for changing their mind partway through an assignment; most workplaces reward employees for sounding certain in a meeting, not for updating a position in front of colleagues. If confident, unwavering answers are what get praised and promoted, people will optimize for performing certainty rather than practicing rethinking, regardless of what they privately believe.

## Example from the book
Grant draws on his own experience teaching at Wharton to describe how he restructured parts of his course around getting challenged rather than around being seen as the authority. He deliberately solicits pointed, critical feedback from students rather than only general course evaluations, and treats a student successfully identifying a flaw in his own argument or course design as something to reward openly in front of the class, not something to deflect or minimize. The signal this sends is structural, not just personal: if the person with the most authority in the room visibly updates in response to being challenged, it changes what students believe is safe and rewarded for them to do too. He extends the same logic to organizations, describing workplaces where leaders explicitly ask for the strongest disagreement in the room before finalizing a decision, rather than treating consensus in the meeting as a sign the decision is sound.

## Why it matters
This closes a gap the earlier, more individual-focused chapters leave open: even someone who has fully internalized the scientist mindset will struggle to practice it inside a classroom or workplace that actively punishes visible uncertainty or a changed position. Culture sets the incentive gradient everyone in the room is operating under, often more powerfully than any individual's personal disposition toward open-mindedness. A team of naturally curious people will still calcify into defensiveness if raising a counterargument gets treated as insubordination or being unprepared, rather than as the valuable, expected contribution Grant argues it should be.

## Practical application
If you're in any position of authority — a manager, a parent, a teacher, even just the person who called the meeting — look for a concrete way to reward someone for changing their mind or for successfully challenging your own position, and do it visibly rather than privately. If you're not in a position of authority, look for the version of this you can still do: explicitly thank a colleague or friend for pushing back on you, out loud, in front of others, since that alone signals the behavior is welcome and starts to shift what feels safe for the group as a whole.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> In the groups you're part of — at work, at home, among friends — is changing your mind in front of others treated as a sign of strength or a sign of weakness, and how much of that is actually set by you?
