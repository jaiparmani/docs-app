# 09 — Helping Others Rethink

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The last stretch of the book turns the question around: given how hard it is to rethink your own beliefs, how do you help someone else reconsider theirs — especially on a topic they feel strongly and defensively about, like vaccine hesitancy or a firmly held political position? Grant's answer draws on **motivational interviewing**, a technique originally developed for addiction counseling, built on a counterintuitive premise: people are far more likely to talk themselves into changing their mind than to be talked into it by someone else. The technique replaces preaching and prosecuting with genuinely curious, open-ended questions and reflective listening, asking the other person to articulate their own reasons for and against a position rather than supplying reasons for them.

## Why it matters
This closes the loop on the whole book's argument: the scientist mindset, confident humility, and psychological safety all describe how to update your own thinking, but most real disagreements involve someone else whose mind you'd also like to see change — and lecturing, arguing, or presenting more facts is often exactly the preacher- or prosecutor-mode approach that makes a defensive person dig in harder. Grant's point is that the instinct to counter someone's resistance with a stronger argument frequently backfires, because it puts them in the position of defending their identity rather than examining their belief. Asking questions instead of making statements keeps the other person in the driver's seat of their own rethinking, which is the only seat from which it reliably happens.

## Example from the book
Grant discusses research on conversations between health workers and parents hesitant about vaccinating their children, where lecturing parents with statistics and safety data tends to entrench resistance rather than reduce it. The motivational-interviewing alternative asks open questions instead — inviting a parent to explain, in their own words, what specifically concerns them, and then asking what, if anything, might make them somewhat more willing, rather than pushing directly for full agreement. A signature move Grant highlights is asking someone to rate their willingness on a scale and then asking why they picked that number and not a lower one — a question that requires the person to generate and voice their own reasons in favor of the position, rather than simply absorbing reasons handed to them by someone else.

## Practical application
Next time you want someone to reconsider a position you think is wrong, resist the urge to open with your best counterargument. Start instead by asking a genuinely open question about why they hold the view and what it would take to shift it even slightly — not as a rhetorical trap, but as real curiosity about their reasoning. If they express any openness at all, however small, ask them to explain that openness in their own words rather than immediately supplying your evidence. The goal isn't to withhold your own view forever — it's to let them do the work of finding their own reasons to move first.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of someone whose mind you've tried and failed to change by arguing harder. What question could you have asked instead that would have let them articulate their own doubts, rather than asking you to accept yours?
