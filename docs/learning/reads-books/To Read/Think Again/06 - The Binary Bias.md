# 06 — The Binary Bias

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Grant names a specific cognitive shortcut he calls the **binary bias**: our tendency to simplify a complex, continuous issue into two opposing camps, because a two-sided frame is easier to process and easier to take a side on than a spectrum with a dozen defensible positions on it. The bias shows up most visibly in politics and identity-linked topics, where a nuanced view — one that borrows from both "sides" of a debate, or rejects the framing entirely — gets treated as suspicious or evasive, while a clean, binary stance reads as principled. The bias doesn't just distort how we see an issue; it changes how we see the people who disagree with us, sorting them into an in-group and an out-group rather than into people who happen to hold a different position on a specific question.

## Why it matters
This matters because it explains why identity-linked topics are so much harder to rethink than almost anything else in the book. On a technical or professional question, updating your view mostly costs you being wrong. On a politically or socially charged topic that's been flattened into a binary, updating your view can feel like switching teams — abandoning not just a conclusion but a group you belong to. Grant's point is that a lot of apparent stubbornness on hot-button issues isn't really resistance to evidence; it's resistance to the social cost of appearing to defect from your side, which the binary bias manufactures by insisting there are only two sides to begin with.

## Example from the book
Grant discusses how this plays out in conversations about contested social and political issues, where people on opposing sides of a genuinely multidimensional question — one with legitimate tradeoffs, uncertain evidence, and reasonable disagreement about values, not just facts — get sorted by others (and by themselves) into one of exactly two camps. He points to the discomfort many people report when they hold a mixed position on a hot-button issue: agreeing with one side on part of the question and the other side on another part, and finding that this nuance doesn't read as thoughtful to either camp — it reads as fence-sitting or as a stealth version of the opposing team. The binary framing actively punishes the more accurate, more complicated answer.

## Practical application
When you notice a topic being discussed as though there are exactly two legitimate positions, try explicitly counting how many distinct sub-questions are actually bundled into it — a genuinely complex issue is rarely reducible to one axis. State your view on each sub-question separately rather than picking an overall "team," and notice whether doing so changes how defensive you feel about the conversation. If you catch yourself assuming someone's entire worldview from one stated position on one issue, that's the binary bias sorting them into a camp rather than treating their view as one data point among many.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick a topic where you feel pressure to pick a "side." If you separated it into its actual sub-questions, would your honest answer to each one still map neatly onto one team — or would it turn out to be more mixed than the binary framing allows you to say out loud?
