# 05 — Rethinking Under Pressure

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Everything so far has been about rethinking with time to spare — a belief you can afford to sit with and reconsider. Grant pushes the same idea into the hardest possible setting: a genuine, fast-moving crisis, where the instinct to stick with a plan you've already committed to is strongest exactly when the situation most demands you abandon it. Under acute stress, people don't just fail to generate new ideas — they lose the ability to let go of familiar ones, clinging to a course of action, a tool, or a role even after the evidence in front of them says it no longer fits. The danger isn't only having the wrong plan; it's the inability to notice, mid-crisis, that the plan needs to change.

## Example from the book
Grant draws on the well-documented case of the Mann Gulch wildfire, where a crew of smokejumpers led by foreman Wagner Dodge found themselves outrun by a fast-moving blaze in steep, dry terrain. With the fire closing in, Dodge did something no one on his crew had ever seen: he lit a small fire in the grass ahead of himself, let it burn away the fuel in a patch of ground, and lay down inside the burned-out area as the main fire passed around him. It worked — he survived largely unharmed. But his crew, sprinting for the ridgeline instead, didn't follow his lead or understand what he was doing in the seconds it mattered; thirteen of them died. Separately, Grant discusses the broader pattern researchers have found in firefighting crises more generally: crews that die in these situations are disproportionately likely to have kept carrying their heavy tools — chainsaws, backpacks — while running from a fire, even when dropping the gear would have let them move faster and survive. The tool wasn't just equipment; it was tied to their identity and role as firefighters, which made it psychologically much harder to abandon than the physics of the situation demanded.

## Why it matters
This connects back to confident humility and the four mindsets under maximum pressure. In a crisis, the cost of staying in preacher or prosecutor mode about your current plan isn't a lost argument — it's measured in outcomes that can't be undone. Grant's point is that rethinking capacity isn't only a personality trait you either have or don't; it can be actively destroyed by stress, tunnel vision, and attachment to a role or identity ("I am a firefighter, and firefighters carry their tools"), at precisely the moment clear thinking is most needed. The skill of questioning your own plan has to be practiced well before the crisis, because there's rarely capacity to build it from scratch in the moment.

## Practical application
For any plan you're executing under real pressure — a product launch going sideways, a negotiation that's not going as expected, a deadline crisis at work — build in an explicit prompt to ask "what would I need to see to abandon this approach entirely, and am I seeing it right now?" before the pressure gets high enough to make that question feel impossible to ask. It also helps to identify your own version of "the tool you won't drop" in advance: the process, title, or habitual response you're most attached to for identity reasons rather than practical ones, since that's the thing you're least likely to question exactly when questioning it matters most.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Under real pressure, what's the equivalent of "the tool you won't drop" for you — the habit, role, or approach you'd keep holding onto even after it stopped serving you, simply because letting go of it would feel like losing part of who you are?
