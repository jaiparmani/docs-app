# 10 — The Freaky Friday Management Technique

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Named after the movie premise of a parent and child swapping bodies and suddenly understanding each other's perspective, Horowitz's technique is a literal response to a specific kind of organizational conflict: two department heads who are locked in a persistent turf war, each convinced the other's team is the source of the dysfunction between them. Instead of mediating between them or issuing a top-down ruling, Horowitz's move was to swap their roles — put each executive in charge of the other's department. Forced to actually run the team they'd been blaming, each one quickly encountered the real constraints and incentives the other side had been operating under the whole time.

## Why it matters
Cross-team conflict at the executive level often isn't a personality problem, it's a structural one — two functions with genuinely different incentives and constraints talking past each other, each certain the other side is simply being unreasonable or incompetent. Normal conflict resolution (a mediated conversation, a CEO ruling in one team's favor) tends to paper over the structural mismatch rather than fix it, and it leaves both sides feeling unheard. Actually inhabiting the other role, even briefly, replaces secondhand assumptions about "why they keep doing that" with direct, first-hand experience of the actual constraints.

## Example from the book
At Opsware, Horowitz describes an ongoing conflict between the Sales Engineering team and Customer Support: Sales Engineering felt Support was too slow to resolve issues, while Support felt Sales Engineering escalated every bug as top priority, making it impossible to actually triage anything. Rather than picking a side, Horowitz swapped the two department heads into each other's roles. Both were initially shocked and unhappy about it — but within about a week of actually running the other function, they had independently diagnosed the real underlying issues and worked out a set of shared processes that fixed the conflict from both sides at once.

## Practical application
When two teams or leaders are in a recurring, mutual-blame conflict that normal mediation hasn't resolved, consider whether the disagreement is really about facts or about each side lacking direct experience of the other's actual constraints. A literal swap is a drastic tool for a drastic case, but the underlying move scales down: have each side spend real time actually doing part of the other's job, rather than just hearing a summary of their complaints, before trying to negotiate a resolution.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a team or person you're currently in friction with where your read of "why they keep doing that" is based on secondhand assumption rather than any real firsthand experience of their actual constraints?
