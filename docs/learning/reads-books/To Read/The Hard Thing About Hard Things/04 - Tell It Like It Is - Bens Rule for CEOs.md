# 04 — Tell It Like It Is — Ben's Rule for CEOs

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Horowitz argues that a CEO's single most important communication habit is telling the truth about bad news, promptly and without spin, both up to the board and down through the organization. He's specific that this isn't the same as being harsh or having no tact — it's about refusing to let optimism, discomfort, or the desire to protect morale distort what people are actually told about the state of the business. The instinct to soften bad news, delay it, or bury it in qualifications feels protective in the moment but corrodes the thing a CEO depends on most: people's ability to trust what they're told, which determines whether they'll bring you their own bad news early enough for it to still be fixable.

## Why it matters
A company running on incomplete or softened information can't actually respond to its real problems — by the time a diluted version of bad news reaches the people who could act on it, it's often too late to act on it well. Horowitz treats honesty about difficulty as a structural requirement for a functioning organization, not a personality trait or a nice-to-have. If employees learn that the CEO's public updates are consistently rosier than reality, they'll start doing the same thing in their own reporting up the chain — hiding problems, hedging bad news — which means the CEO ends up making decisions on the same distorted information they created.

## Example from the book
Horowitz describes this discipline as something he had to hold to specifically during Loudcloud's worst stretches, when the honest state of the business — shrinking customer base, uncertain runway, a market that had turned against the company's entire model — was the kind of news that would have been far easier to soften for the sake of morale. He argues the CEOs who avoid delivering hard truths clearly aren't protecting their people; they're protecting their own short-term comfort at the cost of the organization's ability to actually respond to what's happening.

## Practical application
Before delivering an update on something difficult, notice the impulse to round the news toward optimism or bury the hard part in caveats, and treat that impulse as something to override rather than follow. The practical version of this rule is to state the actual situation plainly, including the parts that reflect badly on your own decisions or the company's prospects, and to do it as soon as the facts are solid enough to share — not once you've found a way to frame them comfortably. The payoff isn't just trust for its own sake; it's that people closer to the ground start bringing you their own bad news sooner, while it's still fixable.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a piece of bad news right now — about a project, a team, or your own decisions — that you've been quietly softening on the way up or down the chain, and what would it cost to just say it plainly?
