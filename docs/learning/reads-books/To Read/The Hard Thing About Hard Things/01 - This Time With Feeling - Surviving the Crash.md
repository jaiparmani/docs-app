# 01 — This Time With Feeling — Surviving the Crash

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book opens not with a framework but with the story that everything else in it is built on. Horowitz co-founded Loudcloud in 1999 with Marc Andreessen and others, renting out data-center infrastructure to other companies before "cloud computing" was a category or even a phrase anyone used. Loudcloud went public in March 2001, one of the last dot-com-era companies to IPO before the market shut the door entirely — and it did so just as its customer base, made up largely of other venture-backed startups, began collapsing under it. The core idea Horowitz wants readers to sit with before anything else: the company's near-death wasn't a single dramatic event with a clean beginning and end. It was a slow, compounding erosion — competitors going bankrupt, customers going bankrupt, capital markets closing, and a business model that required continuous, heavy spending just to keep serving the customers who were left.

## Why it matters
Most accounts of a company's hardest period get written after the fact, once the ending is known, which makes the crisis read like foreshadowing rather than genuine uncertainty. Horowitz is explicit that he's trying to avoid that. He wants the reader to understand what it actually felt like to run the business without knowing whether it would exist in six months — because that felt experience, not the eventual outcome, is what a founder in a real crisis needs to recognize as normal rather than as a sign they're uniquely failing.

## Example from the book
Loudcloud's IPO happened during one of the worst windows in stock market history for a young tech company to go public — the Nasdaq was in freefall, and public investors had turned hostile to exactly the kind of capital-intensive infrastructure business Loudcloud was. At the same time, Exodus Communications, once a roughly $50 billion competitor, was heading toward its own bankruptcy, and a number of Loudcloud's own customers — mostly other dot-com-era startups — began failing and taking their contracted revenue down with them. Horowitz describes having to project stability to employees, to the board, and to remaining customers while privately not being certain the company would survive the year.

## Practical application
When you're inside a crisis made of several bad things happening at once, it's tempting to respond to the general sense of dread rather than to the specific numbers that actually determine whether you survive. Horowitz's implicit lesson here is to isolate the few metrics that genuinely decide the outcome — cash on hand, burn rate, committed revenue you can actually count on — and manage attention and communication around those, rather than letting every piece of bad news carry equal emotional weight. It's a habit worth building before you're in a crisis: know in advance which numbers are the ones that would tell you the company is actually in danger, so you're not discovering that in the middle of the emergency.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If your organization (or project, or finances) were quietly heading toward a real crisis right now, which two or three numbers would tell you first — and are you actually looking at them, or just at the general mood?
