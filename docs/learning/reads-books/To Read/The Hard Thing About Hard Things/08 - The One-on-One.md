# 08 — The One-on-One

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Horowitz makes the case that the regular one-on-one meeting between a manager and each direct report is one of the highest-leverage disciplines in an organization, and that most managers run it wrong by treating it as their own meeting — a status check, a place to hand down direction — rather than the employee's meeting. His version flips the default: the one-on-one exists specifically as free-form space for whatever the employee needs to raise that doesn't fit neatly into status reports or email — frustrations, half-formed ideas, concerns about a peer or a process, or bad news they might otherwise sit on. The manager's job in the room is mostly to listen and ask questions, not to run an agenda.

## Why it matters
Formal reporting channels are structurally bad at surfacing the kind of information that actually predicts problems — a talented employee's quiet frustration, an early warning sign about a project, a piece of bad news nobody wants to be the one to escalate. A status report gets edited for the audience; a well-run one-on-one is one of the few settings where an employee has both the privacy and the explicit permission to say something honest and unpolished. Horowitz treats this as a direct extension of the "tell it like it is" instinct applied downward through the organization — if you want people to bring you real problems early, you need a venue built for exactly that.

## Example from the book
Horowitz describes deliberately structuring one-on-ones so the agenda comes from the employee rather than from him, resisting the natural pull for the more senior person in the room to fill the silence with their own priorities. He frames the discipline as one of the more reliable ways to make an organization a genuinely good place to work, precisely because it gives people a standing, protected channel to raise the things that would otherwise never make it up the chain until they'd already become serious problems.

## Practical application
If you manage people, protect one-on-one time as non-negotiable rather than the first thing that gets cancelled when the calendar gets tight, and go in without your own agenda dominating the conversation — ask open questions and let silence sit rather than filling it. If you're the one being managed, treat your one-on-one as your venue: bring the frustration, the half-formed idea, or the bad news you've been sitting on, rather than saving it for a status update where it doesn't fit.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there something you've been meaning to raise with your manager, or that a direct report has been meaning to raise with you, that's been quietly waiting for a venue that never quite arrives?
