# 11 — The EDS Deal — Saving the Company

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
By 2002, Loudcloud was in a position most companies don't survive: its hosting business — the entire original reason the company existed, generating essentially all of its revenue and employing the vast majority of its staff — was burning cash faster than the company could sustain, in a capital market that had completely closed to funding it further. Horowitz's solution was radical: negotiate to sell the hosting business itself, the core product Loudcloud had been built around, to outsourcing giant EDS, while keeping the internal software Loudcloud had built to run that infrastructure — a piece of the company most people, including much of Loudcloud's own board and staff, hadn't thought of as the main asset. That software became the new company, Opsware, sold separately as an enterprise product rather than a hosting service.

## Why it matters
This is the clearest example in the book of Horowitz's broader point that there's often no good option in a real crisis, only a least-bad one, and that finding it requires being willing to give up the thing the company was originally built to do. Selling off the core business wasn't a fallback after other options failed — it was a genuine strategic bet that the tooling built to run the hosting business was actually more valuable, as a standalone product, than the hosting business itself. Getting there required seeing the company's real asset clearly, separate from the product it had always been sold as, at a moment of maximum pressure to just keep operating as before.

## Example from the book
The negotiation with EDS ran for roughly seven weeks and nearly fell apart more than once over that stretch, given the complexity of the deal: EDS would pay Loudcloud $63.5 million in cash and take on the hosting business's liabilities and ongoing cash burn, while also licensing Loudcloud's internal automation software to run both Loudcloud's former infrastructure and parts of EDS's own much larger operation, worth roughly $20 million a year to the newly independent software company. Of Loudcloud's roughly 450 employees, about 440 worked in the hosting business being sold — meaning the deal determined the fate of nearly the entire workforce, not just the company's balance sheet. Opsware, the software business that resulted, went on to become a company HP acquired for $1.6 billion in 2007.

## Practical application
When a business is in genuine survival mode, it's worth asking a harder version of "what can we cut": not just where to trim costs, but whether the thing the company has always been known for is actually its most valuable asset, or whether something built in service of that original thing has quietly become more valuable on its own. That reframe is uncomfortable precisely because it means treating the company's identity, not just its budget, as up for negotiation — which is exactly why most companies in crisis don't consider it until it's too late.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you had to give up the part of your work you're most identified with in order to save what actually matters, would you be able to see that trade clearly enough to make it — or would identity get in the way?
