# 03 — Wartime CEO, Peacetime CEO

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Horowitz argues that most management advice is written for one specific condition — a company that's growing, has room to experiment, and can afford some inefficiency — and that this advice can be actively wrong for a company fighting for survival. He splits leadership style into two modes. A peacetime CEO is expanding a market that's already working, has the luxury of empowering people to make detailed decisions on their own, treats competitors as distant ships that may never engage, and can afford to spend real time deliberately shaping culture. A wartime CEO is focused on a single existential threat, cares intensely about details that would be beneath a peacetime leader's attention if they threaten the company's survival, treats competitors as an active danger rather than background noise, and will break normal protocol when protocol gets in the way of winning. Most of his own tenure, he says, was as a wartime CEO — a mode most existing leadership writing simply doesn't describe.

## Why it matters
The danger Horowitz is naming is that a leader in a genuine crisis who keeps applying peacetime instincts — deferring decisions, protecting process, giving people room to fail and learn — can let a survivable crisis become a fatal one. Conversely, a leader who runs wartime mode during ordinary growth periods burns people out and creates unnecessary conflict where none was needed. The framework isn't a claim that one mode is better; it's a diagnostic tool for figuring out which mode the actual situation calls for, since misreading it in either direction has real costs.

## Example from the book
Horowitz describes his own shift into wartime mode during Loudcloud's crisis years, when the company's survival depended on decisions that couldn't wait for consensus or the normal deliberateness of a peacetime organization — decisions about which parts of the business to keep, which to sell, and how fast to move, made with incomplete information under real time pressure. He contrasts this explicitly with the kind of CEO behavior that gets celebrated in most business writing: patient, empowering, focused on long-term culture-building — behavior that assumes a level of stability wartime simply doesn't afford.

## Practical application
Before defaulting to your usual leadership style, ask honestly which mode the situation actually is: is this a period where the business is fundamentally working and the job is to scale it, or is this a period where survival itself is genuinely in question? The answer should change how much deliberation is appropriate, how much detail you personally get involved in, and how much you're willing to bend normal process to move fast. The mistake to watch for isn't picking the wrong mode once — it's staying in the wrong mode after the underlying situation has already changed.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> In your current role or project, are you leading like it's peacetime when the real situation calls for wartime — or the reverse, running everyone ragged over a threat that isn't actually existential?
