# 06 — Firing an Executive

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Horowitz argues that firing a senior executive is a materially different act than a normal termination, and that most first-time CEOs get it wrong by treating it the same way. An executive typically has deep relationships across the organization, real influence on morale and direction, and — because of that visibility — a firing that's handled clumsily doesn't stay contained to one person; it becomes a company-wide event that shapes how everyone reads leadership's competence and judgment. His guidance is to prepare thoroughly before the conversation happens: know exactly why the person is being let go, be ready to explain it honestly to the rest of the team without either trashing the departing executive or hiding the real reason, and move decisively once the decision is actually made rather than letting it linger once it's clear the fit isn't working.

## Why it matters
A poorly handled executive departure creates uncertainty that ripples through every team that executive touched — people start wondering what really happened, whether they're at risk themselves, and whether leadership actually knows what it's doing. Because executives sit at the center of relationships and information flow, the fallout from a bad process is disproportionate to the fallout from a normal employee departure. Horowitz treats getting this right as a direct test of a CEO's judgment under pressure, precisely because it's one of the more emotionally difficult calls a CEO has to make and one of the easiest to delay past the point where delay causes real damage.

## Example from the book
Horowitz writes about the discipline of separating the personal difficulty of letting a senior person go — someone who may be a friend, may have been loyal, may have real strengths — from the clear-eyed judgment of whether they're still right for the role the company now needs. He's direct that CEOs often let this drag on specifically because of that personal difficulty, holding onto an executive who isn't working out longer than the business can afford, out of loyalty or a hope that things will improve without the harder conversation actually happening.

## Practical application
Before the conversation, be able to state in one clear sentence why the person is being let go — if you can't, you're not ready to have the conversation. Plan what you'll tell the rest of the organization in advance, aiming for honesty about the business reason without turning it into a public account of the person's failures. And treat "we should probably make a change eventually" as already the decision — the gap between recognizing an executive isn't working and actually acting on it is usually where the most damage accumulates.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a role on your team right now where you already suspect the person isn't the right fit, but you've been telling yourself it's not urgent yet?
