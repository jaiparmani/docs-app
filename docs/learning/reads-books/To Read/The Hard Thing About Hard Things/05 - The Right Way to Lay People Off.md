# 05 — The Right Way to Lay People Off

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Horowitz treats layoffs as one of the hardest and most consequential things a CEO does, and argues most leaders make them worse than they need to be by trying to soften the process instead of executing it cleanly. His guidance runs against the instinct to delay the decision once it's clearly necessary, to announce it vaguely, or to let it drag out over weeks of rumor and anxiety. Once a layoff is the right call, he argues for moving fast, being direct with both the people leaving and the people staying about why it's happening and how it was decided, and treating everyone affected with real dignity in the mechanics of how it's carried out — rather than trying to make the news itself feel less severe than it is.

## Why it matters
A layoff handled badly damages the company twice: once through the loss of people and capability, and again through the corrosive effect on everyone who stays, who will judge the company's character by how it treated the people who left. Employees who remain after a poorly handled layoff tend to disengage, distrust leadership's honesty, and start quietly job-hunting themselves, on the assumption they could be next and won't be treated any better. A layoff handled with real clarity and respect, by contrast, can actually reinforce trust in leadership even though it's terrible news, because people see that the company is willing to be honest and humane about hard things rather than evasive.

## Example from the book
Loudcloud went through layoffs more than once during its crisis years, cutting significant portions of its workforce as the business contracted under the weight of the dot-com collapse. Horowitz describes the responsibility of explaining these decisions honestly to the whole company — not just the people being let go — including why the cuts were necessary and how the company arrived at the specific decisions, rather than letting the layoff become a rumor-driven event that employees had to piece together for themselves.

## Practical application
If a layoff is genuinely necessary, Horowitz's implicit checklist is: decide quickly once the need is clear rather than delaying out of discomfort; be specific with the whole organization about the business reason, not just a vague reference to "restructuring"; give people who are leaving as much dignity and practical support as the situation allows; and address the people who remain directly, rather than letting silence do the explaining. The common thread is that clarity and respect are not in tension with speed — trying to be gentle by being vague or slow tends to produce the worst outcome on both fronts.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If your organization had to make a significant cut tomorrow, would the people staying trust the explanation they were given — or would they be left to fill in the story themselves?
