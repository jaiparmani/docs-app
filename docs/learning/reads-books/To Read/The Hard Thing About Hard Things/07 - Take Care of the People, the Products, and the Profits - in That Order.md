# 07 — Take Care of the People, the Products, and the Profits — in That Order

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Horowitz offers this as a literal priority ordering for what a CEO should focus attention and resources on, and argues most struggling companies have the order backward without realizing it. People first: build an organization that's genuinely a good place to work, where people are trained, developed, and treated with respect, because a company can't build good products with people who are disengaged or being managed by broken processes. Products second: a great product, built by a well-functioning team, is what eventually produces revenue — chasing revenue directly, ahead of the product and the people who build it, tends to produce short-term results at the expense of the thing that would have generated durable revenue anyway. Profits, in his framing, are the outcome of getting the first two right, not something to optimize directly at their expense.

## Why it matters
Under pressure, the instinct in a struggling company is often to focus first on the numbers — cut costs, chase revenue, protect the profit line — treating people and product quality as line items to trim when things get tight. Horowitz's argument is that this ordering is self-defeating: degrade the people and the product to protect short-term profit, and you've undermined the actual source of the company's ability to generate profit in the first place. The ordering is a discipline specifically for moments when it's tempting to invert it, not a claim that profits don't matter.

## Example from the book
Horowitz describes deliberately investing in things like structured employee training and genuine career development even while Loudcloud was under severe financial pressure, on the logic that a company that stops taking care of its people during a crisis will find that its best people — the ones most able to leave for something more stable — are the first to go, leaving a weaker organization to solve exactly the hardest problems it's facing. The same logic applied to protecting product quality rather than letting it erode for the sake of short-term cost savings.

## Practical application
When resources are tight and something has to give, use this ordering as a forcing function: ask whether a proposed cut is coming out of the people, the product, or the profit margin, and be suspicious of any plan that protects near-term profit by degrading the other two, since that trade tends to show up as a bigger problem later. Concretely, this can mean protecting training and development budgets, or product quality standards, even when a spreadsheet says cutting them would help this quarter's numbers.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Somewhere in your work right now, is a short-term financial or numbers pressure quietly eating into either the people or the product — and if so, which one, and what would it cost to protect it instead?
