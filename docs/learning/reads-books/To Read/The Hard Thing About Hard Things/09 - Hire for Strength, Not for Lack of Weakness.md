# 09 — Hire for Strength, Not for Lack of Weakness

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Horowitz argues that hiring decisions made by committee quietly select for mediocrity, because a group process naturally optimizes for the candidate nobody strongly objects to rather than the candidate who's genuinely exceptional at the thing that matters most for the role. His alternative principle: hire for a specific, decisive strength in the core skill the job actually requires, and be willing to tolerate real weaknesses elsewhere, rather than hiring the well-rounded candidate who's merely good across the board and outstanding at nothing. Someone with exceptional technical ability and only average communication skills, in his framing, is usually a better hire than someone who's solidly good at both but exceptional at neither — because it's the exceptional core skill that actually moves the business.

## Why it matters
A hiring process that lets every interviewer effectively veto a candidate for any flaw pushes outcomes toward the safest, least objectionable choice, which is rarely the same as the strongest choice. This matters especially for small or growing companies, where a handful of key hires disproportionately shape what the company becomes — settling for well-rounded-but-unremarkable in those seats compounds over time in a way that's hard to see hire by hire but very visible in aggregate. Horowitz pairs this with a related idea about ambition: hire people whose ambition is primarily about the company's success, with their own advancement following from that, rather than people whose ambition is about their own trajectory regardless of what happens to the company around them.

## Example from the book
Horowitz describes deliberately pushing back against consensus-driven hiring processes at Loudcloud and Opsware, where he'd involve multiple people in evaluating a candidate but reserve the actual decision rather than letting it default to whoever in the room had the strongest objection. He frames this as a direct response to watching hiring-by-committee produce safe, competent, forgettable hires when what the company actually needed in a given seat was someone genuinely great at one specific, critical thing.

## Practical application
When evaluating a candidate, separate the question "is there anything wrong with this person" from the question "is this person exceptional at the one or two things this role most needs" — and weight the second question much more heavily. In a group hiring process, make sure the final call rests with one person accountable for the outcome rather than defaulting to consensus, since consensus tends to erode toward the least objectionable option rather than the strongest one.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a hire (or a choice among options more generally) where you picked the safest, least objectionable option — what would you have chosen instead if you'd been optimizing purely for the single most important strength?
