# The Richest Man in Babylon

<small>2 min read</small>

*George S. Clason*

## What it actually is

Clason wrote this as a series of pamphlets in the 1920s, distributed by banks and insurance companies as financial literacy material, and later collected into the book form most people know. The mechanism is parable rather than argument: instead of stating principles directly, it tells stories set in ancient Babylon — a poor scribe learning from a wealthy friend, a camel trader working his way out of debt — that dramatize a small set of rules. Pay yourself first, before any other expense, by saving a fixed portion of what you earn. Put that money to work rather than letting it sit idle. Guard it from loss by seeking advice from people who actually have expertise in what you're doing with it, not from whoever's nearby. None of this is complicated, and that's the point — the book's whole bet is that financial trouble is usually a behavior problem, not a knowledge problem, so the fix is a handful of rules simple enough to actually follow.

## Why it's plausibly worth the read

The parable format is doing real work, not just decoration. Abstract advice like "save 10% of your income" is easy to nod at and hard to actually act on; wrapping it in a story about a specific character failing and correcting course gives it a stickiness that a bullet-point list doesn't have, which is presumably why banks handed these out as pamphlets in the first place — they're built to be remembered, not just read. It's also short, which matters for a book whose entire value proposition is "internalize a few rules," and it's stayed continuously in print for a century, which for a personal finance book is itself a signal that the core mechanics haven't gone stale even as markets and instruments have changed completely.

## The honest heads-up

The most common criticism, especially from readers who come to it after more analytically rigorous personal finance writing, is that it's simplistic and repetitive — the same handful of principles restated across different Babylonian characters, without the empirical grounding or nuance that something like behavioral-finance writing brings to similar territory. It's prescriptive rather than evidence-based: "seek wise counsel" and "make your money multiply" are true but thin compared to a book that shows you the data on why people fail to do those things. The ancient-Babylon framing, while memorable, also means the specific financial mechanics on offer (usury rates, gold-lending) are archaic and not meant to be taken as literal instruction — the value is entirely in the underlying behavioral principles, not the setting. Read as a first, memorable framing of "pay yourself first" it holds up; read expecting the rigor or nuance of more modern, evidence-driven personal finance writing, it will feel thin.
