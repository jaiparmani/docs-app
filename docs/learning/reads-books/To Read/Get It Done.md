# Get It Done

<small>2 min read</small>

*Ayelet Fishbach*

## What it actually is

Fishbach, a motivation science researcher, is making a specific and somewhat counter-intuitive claim: motivation isn't a fixed trait or a reservoir of willpower that some people have more of and others less — it's a structural property of how a goal is set up, and it can be engineered. The book's territory is the mechanics of that engineering: how you frame progress (as advancing from a starting point versus closing a remaining gap), how deadlines and incentives interact with intrinsic interest in a task, how breaking a large goal into milestones changes persistence, and how the specific way you talk about a goal to yourself changes whether you follow through on it. The premise, in other words, is that "I just don't have the motivation" is usually a diagnosis of bad goal architecture, not a fact about someone's character.

## Why it's plausibly worth the read

The distinguishing feature relative to the broader habits-and-motivation shelf is that it's built from Fishbach's own experimental research program rather than being a synthesis of other people's anecdotes or a memoir of one person's transformation. That matters for a topic like this because motivation advice is unusually prone to surviving on charisma rather than evidence — a compelling personal story about willpower is easy to write and easy to over-generalize from. A book that instead organizes around what's been tested (which framings of progress actually increase follow-through, which kinds of incentives undermine intrinsic motivation rather than support it) is answering a more falsifiable version of the question, and the promise is a toolkit you can apply to a specific goal rather than a general inspirational read.

## The honest heads-up

Academic-grounded doesn't mean uncontroversial — a lot of the motivation and goal-setting literature this book draws on (mental contrasting, implementation intentions, the specific effects of incentives on intrinsic motivation) has real internal debate and replication questions within psychology, and a popular treatment necessarily smooths over some of that nuance to stay readable. The other thing to watch for is scope: findings from lab studies and short-term experimental tasks don't always transfer cleanly to messy, long-horizon real-world goals (a career change, a health habit sustained over years), and the book's practical force will depend on how honestly it flags that gap versus presenting lab-scale findings as settled prescriptions for life-scale problems. Worth reading with an eye for which claims are backed by the cited research directly and which are reasonable extrapolations dressed as findings.
