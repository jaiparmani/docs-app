# 07 — The Empty Chair — Customer Obsession as Doctrine

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
"Customer obsession" shows up in nearly every company's values slide, which is exactly why Stone's account is interesting where it describes how Amazon tried to make it an enforced practice rather than a poster on a wall. The book describes an empty chair kept in some meetings, meant to represent the customer — a literal, physical reminder that there was a stakeholder in the room whose interests had to be argued for even though they weren't present, and who outranked whoever else was talking. It's a small mechanism, but it's a mechanism, which is the difference between a stated value and an operating rule: a value everyone can nod along to and then ignore under deadline pressure; a rule changes what's actually said in the room.

## Why it matters
Most companies say they care about customers. What Stone's account documents is Amazon trying to close the gap between saying it and being structurally forced to act on it — building it into rituals, correspondence habits, and escalation paths rather than leaving it as a sentiment leaders were supposed to remember on their own. Whether or not the empty-chair practice alone made Amazon more customer-focused than competitors, the instinct behind it — that a value only functions as a value if it has a mechanism attached — is portable well beyond Amazon or e-commerce.

## Example from the book
Beyond the empty chair, Stone describes Bezos's own habit of forwarding customer complaint emails directly to the relevant executive with nothing added but a single question mark — an escalation so minimal it required the recipient to figure out the problem and report back quickly, with the implicit understanding that a question mark from Bezos was not something you could let sit. The effect was to short-circuit whatever normal internal process might have buried or slow-walked a single customer's complaint, making sure it reached someone with the authority to actually fix it, fast.

## Practical application
Pick one value your own team claims to hold that currently has no mechanism attached to it — no ritual, no escalation path, nothing that would visibly break if the value were ignored under pressure. Design one small, concrete mechanism for it, something as simple as a standing agenda item, a required field in a template, or a direct escalation habit like Bezos's question-mark emails. The size of the mechanism matters less than whether it actually changes behavior when things get busy, which is exactly when stated values without mechanisms tend to quietly disappear.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Which of your team's or company's stated values has no real mechanism behind it — and what's the smallest ritual you could add that would make it costly to ignore under deadline pressure?
