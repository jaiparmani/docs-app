# 09 — Reported, Not Authorized

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
It's worth being explicit about what kind of book this is, because it shapes everything else in it. Stone, a Bloomberg Businessweek journalist, built *The Everything Store* from several hundred interviews with current and former Amazon executives, competitors, and members of Bezos's own family — but without Bezos's full authorized cooperation, and without editorial control resting with Amazon or Bezos at any point. That's a fundamentally different kind of book from a founder's memoir, where the subject chooses what to include, controls the framing, and often has final say over the manuscript. This is unauthorized, third-party reporting: a company's history assembled by someone with no stake in how flattering the final picture turns out to be.

## Why it matters
The distinction matters more here than it might for most business books, because Amazon's own public narrative — customer obsession, long-term thinking, "Day 1" — is a story Bezos has spent decades actively curating in his own shareholder letters and public statements. Stone's book sits deliberately outside that curation, which is exactly why it contains material a founder-controlled account likely wouldn't: the Gazelle Project's original name, the specifics of the Diapers.com price war, blunt firsthand accounts of harsh treatment in meetings. None of that is necessarily false because it's unflattering, but it's a different kind of source than a memoir, and it should be read with a different set of expectations about what got included and why.

## Example from the book
The clearest point of contrast is *Shoe Dog*, Phil Knight's memoir of building Nike, written decades after the events it describes, entirely in Knight's own voice and entirely under his own editorial control. Reading the two side by side is instructive precisely because they're answering different questions: *Shoe Dog* answers "how does the founder want this story told," while *The Everything Store* answers "what did the people who were actually in the room say happened," including people whose accounts don't flatter the founder at all. Neither format is inherently more honest — a memoir can be candid, and outside reporting can lean on disgruntled sources — but they're not interchangeable, and conflating them means missing what each is actually built to do.

## Practical application
When reading any business biography, check first whether it's authorized or independently reported, and let that shape how you weight specific claims — an unflattering anecdote in an authorized book is more surprising (and arguably more trustworthy) than the same anecdote in an unauthorized one, and vice versa for flattering claims. If you read *The Everything Store*, consider pairing it with something written from inside Amazon's own framing — a shareholder letter, an authorized account — not to find the "true" version, but to see the same events from the vantage the outside reporting deliberately doesn't have.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Next time you read a business biography, do you know before you start whether it's authorized or independently reported — and has that distinction ever actually changed how much weight you gave a specific claim inside it?
