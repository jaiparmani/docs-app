# 01 — The Regret Minimization Framework

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
In 1994, Bezos was a senior vice president at D.E. Shaw, a quantitative hedge fund, with a compensation package most people wouldn't walk away from. He came across a statistic that the web was growing at something like 2,300 percent a year, and started sketching out what a company built around that growth might look like. To decide whether to actually quit, Stone describes him using what Bezos called a "regret minimization framework": project yourself forward to age 80, and ask which choice you'd regret more — trying and failing, or never trying at all. Framed that way, the immediate risk (a good job, a bonus, a predictable career) shrank next to the long-term risk (wondering for the rest of his life what would have happened).

## Why it matters
Most consequential decisions get evaluated on a short time horizon — will this work in the next year or two, will it look smart at the next performance review. The regret-minimization move is a deliberate change of timescale: it swaps "will I succeed" for "will future-me be glad I tried," which is a different question with a different answer. It's also a way of neutralizing the specific fear that blocks most people from leaving something stable — not fear of failing exactly, but fear of visibly failing in the near term. Stretching the frame to a whole life makes near-term visibility matter less.

## Example from the book
Stone recounts Bezos pitching the idea to David Shaw on a walk through Central Park. Shaw reportedly told him it sounded like a good idea for someone who didn't already have a good job — which was, in effect, a warning that walking away from D.E. Shaw was not obviously the smart move. Bezos used the regret-minimization framework to talk himself into it anyway, gave his 30-day notice, and — in the story that's become part of Amazon lore — he and his wife MacKenzie drove from New York to Seattle, with Bezos drafting the early business plan and revenue projections on a laptop during the trip while she drove.

## Practical application
Take a decision you're currently sitting on that feels risky in the near term — a job change, a move, starting something on the side. Instead of asking "will this work," write down the answer to the actual regret-minimization question: at 80, having tried and failed, versus at 80, having never tried — which version of yourself do you expect to be more at peace with? Notice that this question is silent on probability of success; it's deliberately asking about a different axis than "is this a good bet."

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a decision you're currently avoiding specifically because failure would be visible in the next year, that you suspect wouldn't bother you at all in the next decade? What is the visibility actually costing you?
