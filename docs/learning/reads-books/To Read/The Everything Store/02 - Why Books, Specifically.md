# 02 — Why Books, Specifically

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Bezos didn't start Amazon because he loved books. Stone describes him working through a list of roughly twenty possible product categories for his internet-growth thesis and landing on books for structural, almost boring reasons. Books had an enormous catalog — millions of titles in print, far more individual items than any other retail category, which meant no physical store could ever stock more than a sliver of what existed. And critically, two large wholesale distributors, Ingram and Baker & Taylor, already had digitized catalogs covering most of what was in print. That meant Amazon didn't need to build relationships with thousands of individual publishers to have something to sell — it could plug into infrastructure that already existed and effectively resell against a catalog someone else had already assembled.

## Why it matters
The books decision is a case study in a specific kind of opportunity: a category where the hard infrastructure work — sourcing, cataloging, distribution — has already been done by someone else, but no one has built a usable interface on top of it. That's a different kind of bet than "found something nobody has built yet." It requires almost no capital to test relative to building supply-chain infrastructure from scratch, and it lets a small company punch above its size by borrowing scale it didn't build. The catalog-size argument also mattered for a second reason: a store that can plausibly claim to have "every book" has a completely different value proposition than a store that has a curated selection, no matter how good the curation is.

## Example from the book
Stone describes Bezos's screening process during the drive from New York to Seattle — working through candidate categories on the road and converging on books specifically because of the combination of catalog size and existing wholesale infrastructure. The company launched from a Bellevue, Washington garage in 1994 and went live to the public in July 1995, initially selling almost nothing but books despite Bezos's ambitions for the company having always been broader than that — the name "Amazon," chosen for a river that dwarfs its rivals in volume, was picked to signal scale from the start, not to describe what the company actually sold at launch.

## Practical application
When evaluating a new product or business idea, separate two different questions that often get collapsed into one: "is this a good market" and "does the hard infrastructure for this market already exist somewhere I can plug into." A mediocre market with existing infrastructure can be a better first move than a great market where you'd have to build the supply chain from zero — not because the market matters less, but because it changes how much capital and time you need before you can test anything.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your own field does the equivalent of Ingram and Baker & Taylor already exist — infrastructure someone else built that you could plug into rather than replicate — and have you dismissed it as "not the real business" because it's not the flashy part?
