# 08 — The Bezos Standard

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Stone's book portrays Amazon's internal culture as built around an extremely high, sometimes brutally enforced bar — a "standard" in the sense of a line that work either clears or doesn't, applied without much softening for effort or intent. One concrete manifestation the book describes is the requirement that meetings begin not with a slide presentation but with everyone silently reading a six-page narrative memo, on the theory that prose forces clearer thinking than bullet points and that a memo that survives silent reading has to actually hold together as an argument rather than gesture at one. Alongside that came a management style Stone characterizes, through numerous former-employee accounts, as demanding to the point of harshness — direct, often blunt criticism delivered in front of a room when work fell short of the bar.

## Why it matters
This is one of the places where the book's warts-included approach diverges most sharply from a sanitized corporate narrative, and it's worth sitting with the tension rather than resolving it too quickly in either direction. The narrative-memo discipline is a genuinely useful practice independent of who used it — forcing complete sentences and complete arguments does surface muddled thinking that a bullet list can hide. The harsher accounts of meeting-room criticism are a separate matter, and Stone doesn't present them as harmless: they're offered as part of the human cost of the standard, not an incidental detail. A high-standards culture and a psychologically brutal one aren't the same thing, even when the same organization produces both, and the book largely leaves the reader to decide how much one required the other.

## Example from the book
The six-page memo practice replaced PowerPoint at senior meetings specifically because Bezos reportedly found slide decks let people hide gaps in their thinking behind bullet points and formatting. Former employees quoted in the book describe meetings where work that didn't meet the bar drew pointed, sometimes cutting criticism directly from Bezos, in front of colleagues — accounts that contributed to Amazon's reputation, at the time of the book's writing, for high turnover and a demanding, occasionally fear-driven internal atmosphere.

## Practical application
Separate the two threads before adopting anything from this chunk. The narrative-memo discipline is worth trying on its own merits: for your next important decision, write the case in full sentences and paragraphs instead of bullets, and see what gaps in the argument the format itself exposes. The harsh-delivery half is worth examining critically rather than emulating — ask whether the standard itself (the six-page memo, the bar for quality) is doing the useful work, independent of whether the feedback style around it needs to be severe to be effective.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a standard you hold your own team to where you've assumed the standard and the harsh way you enforce it are a package deal? Could you keep the bar exactly where it is and change only how missing it gets communicated?
