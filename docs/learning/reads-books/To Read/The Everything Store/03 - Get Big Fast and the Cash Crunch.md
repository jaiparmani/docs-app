# 03 — Get Big Fast and the Cash Crunch

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
"Get Big Fast" was Amazon's own internal shorthand, as Stone describes it, for a strategy that looks reckless viewed from any single year in isolation: prioritize growth and market share over profitability, on the theory that scale itself was the moat, and that a company which got there first and biggest would be nearly impossible to dislodge later. It meant plowing revenue and raised capital back into growth — new categories, new distribution centers, marketing — rather than turning a profit. For years, Amazon lost money on purpose, by design, as a matter of strategy rather than struggle.

## Why it matters
This is the strategy that very nearly killed the company, which is why it belongs in the same chunk as the near-collapse rather than as an unqualified success story. Betting everything on future scale means the company is permanently exposed to any interruption in its ability to keep raising capital — and Amazon hit exactly that wall when the market turned. The lesson Stone's account leaves you with isn't "growth over profit is right" or "wrong," it's that the strategy only survived because of one specific, well-timed financing decision, and that a slightly different timeline would have ended differently.

## Example from the book
In 1999, before the crash, Amazon raised $1.25 billion through a convertible bond offering — a large amount of debt financing that gave the company a substantial cash cushion. When the dot-com crash hit in 2000 and 2001, Amazon's stock fell from over $100 a share to around $6, and a widely read Lehman Brothers analyst report by Ravi Suria openly questioned whether Amazon would run out of cash and go under, spooking the market further. Many of Amazon's contemporaries — companies that had also grown fast without securing that kind of cash buffer — did not survive the crash. Amazon did, largely because the bond raised months earlier gave it runway that competitors without similar foresight or luck didn't have.

## Practical application
If you're running something — a company, a project, a personal financial plan — that depends on continued access to capital or continued goodwill from some external party, stress-test it against the specific scenario of that access disappearing for eighteen months with no warning. Amazon's survival wasn't proof that "Get Big Fast" was safe; it was proof that the company got lucky on timing with one specific decision. Separate the strategy from the outcome before concluding the strategy was sound.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If the capital or support you're currently counting on for your own "grow first, figure out profitability later" bet disappeared for eighteen months starting today, what actually happens — and have you built in anything resembling Amazon's 1999 bond raise, or are you relying on the crash simply not coming?
