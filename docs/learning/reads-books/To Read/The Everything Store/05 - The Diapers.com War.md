# 05 — The Diapers.com War

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Quidsi, the parent company of Diapers.com, built a fast-growing business selling baby products online and had turned down earlier acquisition interest from Amazon. Stone describes what happened next as a deliberate, sustained price war: Amazon rolled out steep discounts on diapers and related baby products — reportedly through a program aimed at new and expecting parents — cutting prices low enough that Amazon was knowingly losing large sums of money on every sale in that category, sustained over months, specifically in the categories where Quidsi competed hardest. The pressure wasn't subtle, and it wasn't meant to be: it was a demonstration to Quidsi's investors and leadership that competing head-on with Amazon on price was not sustainable.

## Why it matters
This episode is a clean illustration of how the exact same action — cutting prices — reads completely differently depending on which side of it you're standing on. To an Amazon customer buying diapers during that window, it looked like customer obsession working exactly as advertised: lower prices, faster delivery. To Quidsi, it was a deliberate war of attrition from a company with far deeper pockets, designed to make independent survival impossible rather than to compete on the merits long-term. Both descriptions are accurate at once, which is what makes the episode useful — it shows that "good for the customer today" and "anticompetitive in effect" aren't mutually exclusive, and a company can pursue both simultaneously without any contradiction in its own messaging.

## Example from the book
Stone reports that Amazon's price cuts on diapers and baby products cost the company tens of millions of dollars over the course of the campaign — money it was willing to lose specifically to change Quidsi's strategic calculus. Quidsi, which had reportedly also drawn interest from Walmart, ultimately agreed to sell to Amazon, in a deal Stone puts at roughly $500 million, closing in 2010. The pricing pressure stopped being necessary once the acquisition was complete.

## Practical application
When you're on the receiving end of unusually generous pricing or terms from a much larger competitor or platform, it's worth asking a second question beyond "is this a good deal for me right now": is this price sustainable for the company offering it, or is it priced specifically to change my behavior rather than to reflect the actual cost of serving me. The Diapers.com case is a reminder that scale can fund a temporary loss that a smaller player can't match indefinitely, and that "the customer wins" can be true in the short run while still being the mechanism by which a competitor exits the market in the long run.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Have you ever benefited from a price or deal that, in hindsight, was clearly funded by someone trying to eliminate a competitor rather than by genuine cost efficiency? Did knowing that (or not knowing it at the time) change how you feel about having taken the deal?
