# 04 — The Gazelle Project

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Stone's book describes an internal Amazon program aimed at extracting better financial terms — deeper discounts, better payment terms — specifically from small and mid-sized publishers, the ones with the least leverage to push back. The project's original internal name was blunt enough that it later had to be changed to something blander once word of it got out. The premise was straightforward and unsentimental: publishers with market power (the largest houses) could resist Amazon's demands, but small publishers, who depended heavily on Amazon for sales volume and had no comparable alternative channel, mostly couldn't.

## Why it matters
This is one of the clearest places in the book where the gap opens up between Amazon's public-facing value ("customer obsession," low prices, endless selection) and how it operated on the supplier side of the business, where the same drive for efficiency showed up as leverage applied against the weakest counterparties rather than the strongest. It's a useful corrective to reading "customer obsession" as a synonym for "benevolent" — the obsession was specifically about the customer, and the book documents that other stakeholders, including small publishers who depended on Amazon for their livelihood, were not treated with the same deference.

## Example from the book
According to Stone, the program was internally named the "Gazelle Project," a reference reportedly tied to a comment attributed to Bezos about how Amazon should approach negotiations with small publishers — the kind of predator-and-weakest-prey framing that made the name toxic once it circulated more widely inside and outside the company. After the name became an embarrassment, it was rebranded internally to something anodyne, like the "Small Publisher Negotiation Program," while the underlying negotiating posture stayed the same: better terms extracted specifically from the publishers least able to say no.

## Practical application
In any negotiation or ongoing relationship where you hold structural leverage over the other side, notice whether your terms change based on how much the other party actually needs you versus how much they could resist you if they wanted to. That's not automatically wrong — leverage is real and using it is normal business — but the Gazelle Project is a useful check on the story a company tells about why it treats different counterparties differently. If the honest internal name for a practice would be embarrassing if it leaked, that's information about the practice, independent of whether it's technically defensible.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a relationship — a client, a vendor, an employee — where you currently extract more from them specifically because they have fewer options than someone else you deal with would? Would you be comfortable if the internal name for how you treat them became public?
