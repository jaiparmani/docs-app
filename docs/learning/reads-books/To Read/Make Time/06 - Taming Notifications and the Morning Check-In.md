# 06 — Taming Notifications and the Morning Check-In

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Two closely related Laser tactics target the moments distraction most reliably wins: notifications that interrupt you mid-task, and the reflexive habit of checking phone, email, or feeds first thing in the morning before you've chosen a Highlight. The book's recommendation for both is the same underlying move — turn off what you can, and delay what you can't turn off — so that other people's messages and platforms' prompts stop being the thing that sets the day's first priority.

## Why it matters
Notifications are interruptions by design: each one is a small, engineered claim that something needs your attention right now, and answering that claim — even just glancing at the lock screen — costs far more than the few seconds it visibly takes, because attention takes real time to fully return to what it was doing beforehand. The morning check-in does something worse: it hands the very first slice of the day's attention, when willpower and focus are typically at their highest, to whatever's sitting in an inbox or feed rather than to the Highlight you haven't chosen yet. Skipping it isn't about discipline for its own sake — it's about protecting the one part of the day when choosing your own priority is easiest, before the accumulated asks of other people have had a chance to set the agenda for you. The two tactics reinforce each other: notifications make it hard to avoid the morning check-in even if you intend to, since a buzzing phone on the nightstand is itself a kind of check-in, so silencing notifications is often the precondition that makes delaying the check-in actually achievable.

## Example from the book
The authors describe turning off notifications for essentially everything except actual person-to-person communication that's genuinely time-sensitive (calls and texts from people they'd want to hear from immediately), letting email, social platforms, and most apps sit silent until checked deliberately. On the morning side, the tactic is simply to not look at phone, email, or any feed until after the Highlight has been chosen and, ideally, clock-blocked — even a modest delay of the first check by thirty minutes to an hour changes what's setting the tone for the day.

## Practical application
Pick one app today and turn off its notifications entirely — not "reduce," off. Separately, tomorrow morning, commit to choosing your Highlight and blocking time for it before you look at your phone at all; if you use your phone as an alarm, physically get up and away from it before checking anything else. Notice, honestly, how much of the resistance to doing this comes from genuine necessity versus habit. If turning off notifications for a work app feels too risky to do outright, try it for a single afternoon first and see what, if anything, you actually missed — the book's whole approach is testing a tactic in a small, reversible way before deciding whether it earns a permanent place.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Of the notifications currently enabled on your phone, how many are from something or someone that genuinely needs a same-minute response — and how many have you just never gotten around to turning off?
