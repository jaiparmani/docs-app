# 03 — Clock Blocking: Turning the Highlight into Calendar Time

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
"Clock blocking" is the book's term for the immediate next step after choosing a Highlight: instead of leaving it as a vague intention to "get to today," you put an actual block of time on your calendar for it, treating that block with the same seriousness as a meeting someone else scheduled with you. The authors are specific that this should happen early — ideally the block goes on the calendar first thing, before the day's meetings and requests fill the available slots — and that the block itself should be sized to the Highlight, typically somewhere in the 60-to-90-minute range rather than left open-ended.

## Why it matters
A Highlight that exists only as a mental note competes, all day, against every other thing that has an actual claim on your time — a meeting invite, a coworker's request, a notification — and mental notes lose that competition constantly, because nothing defends them. Putting the Highlight on the calendar converts it from an intention into a scheduled commitment with the same structural weight as any other appointment, which means declining a conflicting request during that window doesn't feel like blowing off "nothing," it feels like protecting an existing commitment. This is a small mechanical shift, but it's doing real work: it moves the fight for your time from a decision you have to make fresh in the moment (again and again, against your own depleting willpower) to a decision you made once, calmly, in the morning.

## Example from the book
The authors describe scheduling the Highlight block deliberately at the start of the day, before checking email or accepting meeting invites, specifically so that other people's requests have to route around an already-occupied slot rather than the Highlight having to be squeezed into whatever's left over. They also note the block doesn't need to be a heroic multi-hour stretch — a defended 60-to-90-minute window, done consistently, accomplishes more over a month than an aspirational but rarely-realized "deep work day" that keeps getting cancelled.

## Practical application
Tomorrow, before you look at your inbox or accept any meeting, open your calendar and block time for your Highlight — treat it as non-negotiable for the purposes of that day, the same way you would an appointment with someone else. If a conflicting request comes in later, notice your own instinct to reflexively say yes and treat the existing block as the reason to say no or reschedule, out loud if needed ("I've got something blocked then, can we find another time"). If your calendar is visible to coworkers, label the block honestly rather than leaving it blank or vague — a named block is easier to respect, both for you and for anyone deciding whether to schedule over it.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> The next time a meeting request lands on top of unblocked "free" time versus a Highlight block, would you actually treat the two differently — or does time only feel defended on your calendar once someone else's name is attached to it?
