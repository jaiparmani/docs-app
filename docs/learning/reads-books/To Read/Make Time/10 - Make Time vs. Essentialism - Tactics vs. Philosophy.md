# 10 — Make Time vs. Essentialism: Tactics vs. Philosophy

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
*Make Time* and *Essentialism* (Greg McKeown) are both, at a glance, books about protecting what matters from everything competing for your attention — but they attack the problem from opposite directions. *Make Time* is bottom-up and tactical: a daily, repeatable four-step loop (Highlight, Laser, Energize, Reflect) plus a menu of 80-plus small, individually testable tactics you sample and keep or discard based on results. *Essentialism* is top-down and philosophical: it argues from a discipline of elimination — deciding, structurally, what's essential and systematically saying no to the rest — organized around a broader framework (Explore, Eliminate, Execute) rather than a daily checklist.

## Why it matters
The two books are solving genuinely different failure modes, and mistaking one for the other means reaching for the wrong tool. *Make Time* is well-suited to a day that's fundamentally fine in its priorities but keeps getting eaten by reactive distraction — your actual commitments are reasonable, but email, notifications, and infinite feeds keep consuming the time that should go to them. *Essentialism* is well-suited to a life that's structurally overcommitted — too many genuine obligations, roles, and "yeses" competing for the same finite time, where no amount of turning off notifications fixes the problem because the problem is upstream of any single day's execution. Applying Make Time's daily tactics to an Essentialism-shaped problem (chronic overcommitment) treats a structural issue as a distraction issue and will feel like it's not working, because it isn't addressing the actual cause; applying Essentialism's elimination framework to a Make-Time-shaped problem (a reasonable set of priorities constantly derailed by infinity pools) is solving a problem you don't have while the actual one — a phone full of infinite feeds — goes untouched.

## Example from the book
This contrast isn't drawn explicitly by either book, since they were written independently, but it's visible in how each is structured: *Make Time* opens by naming the two enemies of a focused day (constant busyness and infinite distraction) and spends the rest of the book on small, tested countermeasures against them, one tactic at a time. *Essentialism* opens by arguing that saying yes to almost everything is itself the core problem, and spends the rest of the book on a framework for deciding what to eliminate before execution even begins — a "less but better" filter applied upstream of any single day's schedule, rather than a set of in-the-moment tactics for defending whatever's already on that schedule.

## Practical application
Use the two books in sequence rather than picking one: apply Essentialism's elimination lens periodically (monthly or quarterly) to decide what actually deserves a place among your priorities and commitments in the first place, then apply Make Time's daily loop to actually protect and execute on whatever survives that filter, day by day. If your honest problem right now is "I don't know what I should be prioritizing," Essentialism is the more relevant book; if it's "I know what matters but my days keep getting eaten anyway," Make Time is.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think about your current frustration with how your time gets used — is the root cause that you've said yes to too many genuinely competing priorities (an Essentialism problem), or that a handful of reasonable priorities keep losing to distraction day after day (a Make Time problem)? Would your answer change what you actually try next?
