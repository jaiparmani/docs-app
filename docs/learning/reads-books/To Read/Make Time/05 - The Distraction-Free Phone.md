# 05 — The Distraction-Free Phone

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
One of the book's most concrete, specific Laser tactics is redesigning your phone's home screen so it no longer functions as a launcher for Infinity Pools. The recommendation is to remove the pool apps — social media, email, news, anything with an endlessly refreshing feed — from the home screen entirely (deleting them outright if you can tolerate it, or at minimum burying them a folder or two deep), leaving only genuine tools: maps, camera, calendar, notes, a music or podcast app — things you open with a specific purpose and finish using, rather than things you open reflexively and lose time inside.

## Why it matters
The home screen is the default first move in almost every idle moment — waiting in line, waiting for a page to load, a lull in conversation — and whatever's easiest to tap in that moment gets tapped, almost without a decision being made. A distraction-free home screen doesn't require you to resist temptation in each of those moments; it removes the one-tap option that made giving in effortless in the first place. This is the same friction logic as the Infinity Pools tactic, applied to the single device most people reach for hundreds of times a day, which makes it disproportionately effective for the amount of setup it requires — it's a change made once, not a discipline exercised repeatedly. It's also a good illustration of the book's general preference for environment design over willpower: rather than asking you to notice the urge to check a feed and resist it every single time, the tactic changes the environment once so the urge has nowhere convenient to land.

## Example from the book
The authors describe their own phones reduced to a spare home screen of a handful of utility apps, with the pool apps either deleted or moved off-screen where reaching them takes a deliberate search rather than a single reflexive tap. They pair this with related small tactics — turning off badge numbers and red notification dots (which are specifically engineered to create a nagging sense of unread business), and in some cases switching the display to grayscale, since color is part of what makes feeds and icons visually compelling to return to.

## Practical application
Tonight, before you go to bed, redo your phone's home screen: remove every app that functions as an Infinity Pool, leaving only tools you use with a clear purpose and a clear stopping point. If deleting an app entirely feels too extreme to start, move it off the home screen into a folder at minimum — the point is inserting a deliberate step between impulse and access, not achieving total abstinence on day one. Give it a week before judging the result; the first few days of reaching for a home screen icon that's no longer there is itself useful information about how automatic the habit had become.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you look at your current home screen honestly, how many of the apps on it are tools you open with a specific purpose, versus apps you open reflexively with no particular reason in mind?
