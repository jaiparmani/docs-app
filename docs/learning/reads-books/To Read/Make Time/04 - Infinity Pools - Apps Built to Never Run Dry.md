# 04 — Infinity Pools: Apps Built to Never Run Dry

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
"Infinity Pools" is the book's name for any app or site engineered to refill itself endlessly the moment you look at it — social feeds, email inboxes, news homepages, group chats — so that there is always, by design, one more thing to scroll past or check. Knapp and Zeratsky's point is that these aren't ordinary distractions competing for your attention on equal footing; they're products built by teams whose job is specifically to make the "one more check" feel worthwhile, using techniques (variable rewards, infinite content, social notifications) that are effective against anyone, not just people with weak willpower.

## Why it matters
This reframing matters because it changes where the blame — and the fix — belongs. Most productivity advice treats distraction as a personal failing to be solved with more discipline, but the Infinity Pools concept argues that losing an hour to a feed isn't a willpower failure so much as a predictable outcome of a product doing exactly what it was designed to do. That reframing is liberating in a practical sense: instead of trying to out-willpower a system built by a large team to defeat willpower, the book's actual advice is to change your environment and access to the pool itself — the same move as Clear's "reduce friction" idea in Atomic Habits, but aimed specifically at the class of app that never runs out of content.

## Example from the book
The authors describe recognizing an Infinity Pool by a simple test: does it refresh itself with new content every time you open it, with no natural stopping point? Email, Twitter/X, Facebook, Instagram, Reddit, and most news homepages all pass that test, which is exactly why "just checking quickly" for any of them so often turns into fifteen or twenty minutes with no memory of the individual scrolls that got you there. Rather than proposing you quit these outright, the book's Laser tactics are mostly about inserting friction between you and the pool — removing easy access rather than promising yourself you'll have more willpower next time.

## Practical application
List your own two or three biggest Infinity Pools honestly — the specific apps or sites you open reflexively, not the ones you assume are the problem. For each one, add one piece of friction this week: log out instead of staying signed in, move the app off your home screen, or delete it from your phone entirely and only check it from a browser at a set time. The goal isn't eliminating the content, it's breaking the one-tap, zero-friction path that makes "just checking" effortless enough to happen dozens of times a day without a decision ever really being made.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick the app you open most reflexively — the one you sometimes find yourself opening a second time within a minute of closing it. What single piece of friction, if you added it today, would most cut into that reflex?
