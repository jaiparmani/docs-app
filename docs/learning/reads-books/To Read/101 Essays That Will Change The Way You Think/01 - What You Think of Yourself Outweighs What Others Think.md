# 01 — What You Think of Yourself Outweighs What Others Think

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
One of the most repeated moves across the collection is a reframe of where approval actually needs to come from: not from other people, but from the story you privately tell yourself about who you are and what you deserve. Essay after essay circles back to some version of the claim that your self-concept is the lens every external input passes through — so the same piece of criticism, the same rejection, the same compliment, lands completely differently on two people depending on what each of them already believes about themselves going in. If your internal narrative is shaky, external validation becomes a temporary patch that has to be reapplied constantly. If it's solid, external opinion has much less power to wound or inflate you.

## Why it matters
This is less a single argument than a lens the book keeps returning to because it explains so much downstream behavior: people-pleasing, chronic reassurance-seeking, over-apologizing, staying in relationships or jobs past the point they still serve you, all trace back to outsourcing self-worth to an audience that was never going to be a reliable source of it. The essays treat this not as a character flaw but as a habit of attention — you can notice, mid-moment, that you're scanning a room for approval instead of asking what you actually think, and that noticing is itself the intervention. It's also why the book's short, standalone essay format suits the idea: this isn't a thesis that needs 300 pages of evidence, it's a reminder that works better delivered in a five-minute hit you can return to when you catch yourself doing the thing again.

## Example from the book
A genuinely characteristic essay in this vein opens with an over-specified, relatable scenario — turning down an opportunity because of what a specific person might think, rehearsing a defense of a decision nobody actually challenged — and then pivots into direct second-person address: you, not "people," are the one doing this. The essay doesn't build an argument so much as name the pattern plainly enough that recognizing it does the work, and it typically closes on a compact, quotable line meant to be the takeaway you carry out of the piece even if you forget everything else in it. This structure — scenario, reframe, quotable close — recurs often enough in the collection that it's worth recognizing as a form, not just a coincidence of a few pieces.

## Practical application
The next time you notice yourself hesitating before a decision because of how it will look, pause and separate the two questions that are getting merged: "what do I want to do" and "what will this person think of me for doing it." Answer the first one fully, out loud or on paper, before you let the second one back into the room. If you find you can't answer the first question at all without immediately jumping to the second, that's diagnostic — it means the habit of checking with an internal audience has crowded out the habit of checking with yourself, and that's the thing actually worth working on, more than the specific decision in front of you.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a recent decision you second-guessed. Whose imagined reaction were you actually weighing — and had you asked that person, or were you just assuming?
