# 07 — Alone Is Not the Same as Lonely

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Another recurring distinction the collection draws is between being alone, a factual, neutral state of not being around other people, and being lonely, an emotional state that can occur regardless of how many people are actually present. The essays make the point that loneliness is much more often a function of disconnection — from other people emotionally, or from yourself — than of literal solitude: it's entirely possible to feel intensely lonely inside a crowded relationship or a full social calendar, and it's equally possible to spend extended time completely alone and feel connected, at ease, and un-lonely throughout it. Conflating the two, the essays argue, is what drives people to over-fill their schedules with company that doesn't actually address the underlying disconnection.

## Why it matters
This distinction matters practically because it redirects the fix: if loneliness were simply the absence of other people, the solution would be to add more people, and for a lot of readers this doesn't work — they surround themselves with company and the feeling persists, because the actual cause was a lack of genuine connection or self-relationship, not a lack of bodies in the room. The essays position solitude, correctly used, as one of the more direct routes back to the self-relationship that prevents loneliness in the first place — time alone with no performance required for anyone else is where a lot of the self-awareness the rest of the book asks for actually has room to happen.

## Example from the book
A characteristic essay here will contrast two scenarios directly: someone in a full house or a long-term relationship who still feels unseen and disconnected, against someone who spends a weekend entirely alone and comes out of it feeling more grounded than they have in weeks. The pieces tend to frame solitude not as something to be endured or filled with distraction, but as a specific kind of practice — being with yourself without performing, numbing, or scheduling around the discomfort of quiet — that most people avoid precisely because it's the fastest way to notice what's actually missing.

## Practical application
Next time you feel lonely, resist the instinct to immediately fill the feeling with company or noise, and instead ask what specifically feels missing — is it the presence of other people, or is it a particular kind of understanding, attention, or self-relationship that more people in the room wouldn't actually fix? If you suspect it's the latter, try deliberately spending unstructured time alone, without a screen as a buffer, and notice what surfaces. The discomfort that shows up in the first twenty minutes of genuine solitude is usually informative about what the loneliness was actually pointing at.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a time you felt lonely surrounded by people, versus a time you felt fine completely alone. What was actually different between the two — was it the people, or something else?
