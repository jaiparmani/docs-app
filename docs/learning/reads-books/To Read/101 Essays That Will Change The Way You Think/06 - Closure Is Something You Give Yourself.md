# 06 — Closure Is Something You Give Yourself

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A recurring theme takes on the common assumption that closure — a clean, satisfying resolution to a hurt, a relationship, or an unanswered question — has to come from the other person: an explanation, an apology, an admission that finally makes sense of what happened. The essays argue this is largely a trap, because it puts the end of your own pain in someone else's hands, and that person frequently has no intention of, or capacity for, providing the tidy resolution you're waiting on. Real closure, in this framing, is a decision you make unilaterally to stop needing the explanation, not an event that gets delivered to you by someone else.

## Why it matters
This reframe matters because waiting for external closure keeps people stuck in an open loop indefinitely — rehearsing conversations that will never happen, refreshing for a text that isn't coming, holding a relationship or decision in permanent limbo because the "real" ending hasn't arrived yet. It's a particularly sharp instance of the book's broader move of relocating power from other people back to the reader: you don't control whether someone apologizes, explains themselves, or ever understands what they did, but you do control whether you keep your own resolution hostage to something you don't control. The essays treat this less as advice to feel better and more as a fairly literal description of the only closure that's actually available.

## Example from the book
Essays in this territory typically walk through a familiar unresolved scenario — a relationship that ended without explanation, a friendship that faded without a conversation, a wrong that was never acknowledged — and reframe the reader's continued waiting as the actual source of the ongoing pain, more than the original event itself. The pieces tend to be blunt about the fact that some people are never going to give you what you're waiting for, not out of malice necessarily, but because they're not equipped to or simply don't see it as their responsibility, and that this reality doesn't change no matter how long or how justifiably you wait for it to.

## Practical application
Identify one situation where you're still waiting on someone else — an apology, an explanation, an acknowledgment — before you'll let yourself move on. Write down, as specifically as you can, what you imagine that resolution would actually give you: proof you were right, an admission of wrongdoing, an answer to "why." Then consider constructing that same understanding for yourself, in writing, without their input — not to pretend it's equivalent to actually receiving it from them, but to test whether your own account, once written, actually reduces the charge of the open loop. Often it does more than expected, precisely because the story you'd construct is usually already the one you believe.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What are you still waiting for someone else to say or do before you'll consider a situation resolved — and what happens if that never comes?
