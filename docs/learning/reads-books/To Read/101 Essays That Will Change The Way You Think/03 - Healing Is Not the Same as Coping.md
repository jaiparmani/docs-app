# 03 — Healing Is Not the Same as Coping

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Several essays draw a sharp line between two things people routinely conflate: coping, which is anything that makes a feeling more bearable in the moment, and healing, which is actually resolving whatever produced the feeling in the first place. Coping mechanisms — busyness, overworking, doom-scrolling, staying constantly entertained, even some forms of "self-care" — can look, from the outside and even to the person doing them, indistinguishable from being fine. The essays' point is that a well-managed coping strategy can keep someone functional indefinitely without ever touching the underlying issue, and that the absence of visible distress isn't the same as the presence of resolution.

## Why it matters
This distinction matters because it explains why some people can go years without an apparent crisis and still feel like something is unaddressed underneath — the coping is working exactly as intended, which is precisely the problem, because it's suppressing the signal that would otherwise prompt them to deal with the actual source. It also reframes what "doing well" should mean: not the absence of hard feelings, but evidence that hard feelings have actually been metabolized rather than routed around. This is one of the places where the book pushes against a cultural default (staying busy and positive as inherent virtues) rather than just affirming it.

## Example from the book
A characteristic essay in this territory will list out common coping behaviors that are usually praised or at least socially neutral — staying endlessly productive, always being the one who's fine, filling every unstructured hour — and reframe them as possible avoidance rather than automatic strength. The move isn't to condemn these behaviors outright, since the essays generally acknowledge that coping has a real and sometimes necessary function, but to ask the reader to notice when a coping mechanism has quietly become a permanent substitute for actually processing something, rather than a temporary bridge to get through it.

## Practical application
Take an area of your life where you feel like you're "handling it" — a loss, a disappointment, a conflict you've moved past — and ask honestly whether you've processed it or just built a stable enough routine around not feeling it. A rough test the essays imply: does the feeling still surface, uninvited, in moments when the coping mechanism isn't available (unstructured time, quiet, being alone with your thoughts)? If so, that's a signal the underlying thing hasn't actually been dealt with, no matter how well you've been functioning around it. Healing, in this framing, usually requires deliberately removing the coping mechanism long enough to let the feeling show up and be worked through, rather than waiting for it to resolve on its own.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's something you'd say you've "moved on" from — and does it still surface when you're not busy enough to outrun it?
