# Algorithms to Live By: The Computer Science of Human Decisions

<small>3 min read</small>

*Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths*

The book's premise is that computer science has already solved formal versions of problems humans face informally every day, and that the actual algorithms — not metaphors loosely inspired by them, but the real mathematics — have specific, often counterintuitive answers worth borrowing directly. Optimal stopping theory gives a precise rule (roughly: spend 37% of your search window looking, then commit to the next option better than anything you've seen) for problems like apartment hunting or hiring, where you can't go back to a rejected option. Caching algorithms inform how to organize a closet or a desk. Scheduling theory addresses how to handle an overloaded to-do list. Bayesian reasoning informs how to update beliefs given sparse evidence. Christian, a writer with a computer science and philosophy background, and Griffiths, a cognitive scientist, co-wrote it explicitly to show where computer science and human cognition converge on the same problems from different directions.

## The concrete mechanism

What distinguishes this from typical decision-making books is that the "algorithms" aren't loose analogies — they're actual, provable results from computer science with known optimality guarantees under stated assumptions, and the book is careful to walk through both the math and the assumptions it depends on. The 37% rule for optimal stopping, for instance, is a real result with a real derivation, not a rounded-off rule of thumb invented for the book. That gives the recommendations a different kind of grounding than most advice literature: the claim isn't "successful people do this," it's "under these formal conditions, this is provably the best strategy," followed by an honest discussion of how well real-world decisions actually match those conditions.

## Why it's plausibly worth the read

This sits in a different register from most of the rest of the shelf — closer to a popular-science book about computer science than a self-help book, and appreciably more rigorous as a result. For anyone with even passing interest in CS or math, the appeal is in seeing familiar life problems (procrastination, clutter, indecision) reframed as instances of well-studied formal problems with known, sometimes surprising, answers — the kind of book that changes how you think about a decision even when you don't apply a specific rule to it. It's also genuinely well-sourced; both authors are careful about what the underlying research actually supports.

## The honest heads-up

The tradeoff for that rigor is directness of application. Real decisions rarely match the clean assumptions behind an algorithm — apartment hunting doesn't actually have a fixed, known pool size the way optimal stopping theory assumes, and life rarely hands you a problem in a form clean enough to plug straight into a formula. The book is generally honest about this gap and treats the algorithms as reframing tools and intuition-builders rather than literal instructions, but a reader looking for the kind of concrete, will-repeat-tomorrow action items that dominate the productivity-book genre may find this more intellectually satisfying than directly actionable — it's a book that's more likely to change how you think about tradeoffs than to hand you a checklist.
