# 03 — The Comparison Trap in a Feed-Driven World

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A recurring theme likely to run through the book, based on Warikoo's public commentary on social media and consumption, is that today's teenagers make spending and self-worth decisions against a benchmark no previous generation had: a constant, algorithmically curated stream of other people's best moments and best purchases. Where comparison used to be local and occasional — the kid down the street, the classmate with the newer shoes — it's now continuous and global, and it's specifically the *curated* version of everyone else's life, not the full picture. The book's likely argument is that a lot of teenage spending and self-image decisions are actually comparison decisions wearing a practical disguise.

## Why it matters
Left unnamed, comparison-driven spending and self-worth feel like personal preference — "I just wanted it" — rather than what they often are: a response to an external, manufactured benchmark. Naming the mechanism matters because it's the only way to interrupt it. A teenager who can recognize "I want this because of what I saw online, not because I actually want it" has a decision point that a teenager who can't make that distinction doesn't have. This is also where the money theme and the confidence theme in the book are likely to intersect most directly — comparison spending is usually really a confidence problem showing up as a shopping decision.

## Example from the book
It would be irresponsible to claim a specific example, screenshot, or anecdote from the book's actual pages given how little is confidently knowable about this particular title's contents. What's reasonably inferable from Warikoo's other public commentary is the general shape of the argument: he tends to point at the mechanics of the feed itself — the fact that it shows highlights, not averages — rather than moralizing about social media use in the abstract, and tends to push readers toward a specific, actionable question rather than a blanket "spend less time online."

## Practical application
A workable version of the underlying skill: before a purchase or a moment of feeling behind, ask whether the standard being measured against is something you observed directly in your own life, or something you absorbed from a feed. The two deserve different weight. It's also worth periodically auditing who you follow and what feeling that following habitually produces — not as a one-time cleanup, but as a recurring check, since the accounts and content that make you feel behind tend to change over time.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of the last time you felt like you were falling behind — in looks, money, achievements, or anything else. Was the benchmark something from your actual life, or something you scrolled past?
