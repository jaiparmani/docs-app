# 06 — Marks Are a Measure, Not the Whole Story

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Given the book's title directly contrasts itself with "the syllabus," a core thread is almost certainly a reframing of exam results and grades: they're a real, useful measure of a specific, narrow kind of performance, but they get treated by most students, parents, and schools as a proxy for something much larger — worth, intelligence, future prospects — that they don't actually determine on their own. Warikoo's own well-documented career path, leaving a conventional trajectory to build a public platform and business, is public evidence for the underlying claim even if the book itself doesn't lean on his personal story here: exam performance correlates with certain outcomes but doesn't foreclose or guarantee most of the ones that actually matter over a life.

## Why it matters
Teenagers are immersed in an environment — school, often family, often peer comparison — where marks function as the dominant, sometimes only, visible scoreboard, which makes it easy to internalize a single test result or report card as a verdict on capability rather than as one data point about one narrow skill on one occasion. That internalization has real costs: it inflates the emotional stakes of every exam far beyond what's warranted, and it can crowd out attention to the skills — the ones this book is about — that don't show up on a report card at all but matter more over a longer horizon.

## Example from the book
Without confident knowledge of the book's specific stories, the fair thing to say is that Warikoo's own public biography — a well-known, widely discussed departure from a conventional high-achievement track toward an unconventional, ultimately successful path — is the kind of reference point his other content returns to often, and it would be unsurprising if some version of that same argument, that a single measured outcome didn't determine the rest of the story, appears here in a form pitched at a teenage reader worried about their own marks.

## Practical application
A useful reframe for exam anxiety: treat a grade as feedback on a specific piece of work under specific conditions, not as a summary judgment of ability or worth. Concretely, after a disappointing result, separate the question "what does this tell me about how I studied for this specific test" from the question "what does this tell me about me" — the first is useful and actionable, the second usually isn't, and conflating them is where most of the unnecessary suffering around grades comes from.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a grade or result that felt like a verdict on you rather than feedback on one piece of work. What would it have looked like to separate the two in the moment?
