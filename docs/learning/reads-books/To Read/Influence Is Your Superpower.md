# Influence Is Your Superpower

<small>2 min read</small>

*Zoe Chance*

## What it actually is

The full title — *Influence Is Your Superpower: The Science of Winning Hearts, Sparking Change, and Making Good Things Happen* — signals the reframe up front. Chance, who teaches one of Yale's most popular courses on influence, is deliberately positioning this against the older, more manipulation-flavored genre of persuasion writing (most notably Robert Cialdini's *Influence*, a different and much older book that this one is easy to conflate with by title alone). Her core claim is that the most effective and durable influence isn't about extracting compliance through psychological leverage — scarcity, reciprocity, social proof used as levers on someone else — but about finding outcomes where the other party is genuinely better off too, and that treating influence as something done *with* people rather than *to* people is both more ethical and, over repeated interactions, more effective.

## Why it's plausibly worth the read

The gap this is aimed at is real: a lot of popular persuasion writing either teaches tactics that work once and burn trust on the second use, or it teaches confidence-building without much underlying mechanism. Chance's angle — drawing on her own research and years of teaching and coaching executives, entrepreneurs, and negotiators — is to ground "getting to yes" in what actually happens in the other person's head when they're asked for something, and to treat persuasion skill as learnable rather than as a fixed trait some people have and others don't. That's a genuinely different starting premise from the trait-based framing ("some people are just naturally persuasive") that a lot of people carry into this topic, and if the book delivers on it, the payoff is a more repeatable, less manipulative toolkit than the genre typically offers.

## The honest heads-up

Being newer and less thoroughly stress-tested than Cialdini's, its specific frameworks haven't had decades of independent scrutiny and replication the way Cialdini's six principles have — some of which, worth noting, have themselves come under replication pressure in recent years. There's also a structural tension worth watching for in any "ethical influence" book: the line between "genuinely mutual outcome" and "persuasion that just feels less transactional while still being asymmetric" can blur in practice, and how rigorously the book interrogates that line versus simply asserting good intentions is the thing to evaluate while reading, not assume going in. Popular books in this genre also tend to lean on vivid personal and classroom anecdotes as evidence, which are illustrative but not the same as controlled evidence — worth keeping in mind for how much weight to put on any single story-driven claim.
