# 05 — A Calling Is Not the Same as a Career

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book draws a line between a career — the job title, the prestige, the compensation — and a calling, something closer to the work that actually energizes a specific person and feels worth doing on its own terms. Das pushes the seeker (and by extension the reader) to notice when career decisions are being made almost entirely on status or income signals, with little honest check-in on whether the day-to-day of the work itself is something they actually want more of. A prestigious, well-paid role and a genuine calling sometimes overlap, but the book's point is that they aren't guaranteed to, and treating prestige as a proxy for fit is a common, costly mistake.

## Why it matters
A lot of career dissatisfaction doesn't come from failing at your job — it comes from succeeding at a job that was never actually the right fit, chosen for reasons (family expectation, status, a plausible-sounding five-year plan) that had little to do with what the work would actually feel like day to day. Because external markers of success are so legible and calling is comparatively hard to describe, it's easy to keep optimizing for the visible signal long after the invisible one has gone quiet. The book's insistence on separating the two is really an insistence on paying attention to a much harder-to-measure input.

## Example from the book
Consistent with the book's format, this theme is likely worked through a seeker questioning a stable, externally impressive job that has stopped feeling meaningful, with Das's response probing what specifically about the work drains versus energizes them, rather than simply validating either "stay" or "leave." The book's monastic-adjacent framing would plausibly draw a parallel to finding one's dharma or purpose in the traditional sense he trained in, but I don't have confident recall of the specific terminology or scenario used, so this should be read as a description of the book's likely approach rather than a specific passage.

## Practical application
Separately list what your current work gives you in status and compensation, and what it gives you in actual day-to-day energy and engagement — treat these as two different columns, not one. If the two columns diverge sharply and have for a long time, that's worth investigating directly rather than assuming the discomfort will resolve once you hit the next external milestone. A calling doesn't have to mean quitting; sometimes it means noticing which parts of an existing role to lean into or renegotiate.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If status and income were held completely constant, would you still choose to spend your days doing what you're currently doing?
