# 05 — Enough Is a Moving Target

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Housel's older idea of "never enough" — the risk of moving your own goalposts faster than you clear them — gets a sharper, more specific edge when applied to spending: "enough" isn't a fixed dollar figure that, once reached, produces contentment. Left unexamined, it's a moving target that recalibrates upward every time your circumstances or spending improve, which means the pursuit of "enough" through spending alone can run indefinitely without ever arriving, no matter how much the number in question actually grows. The alternative Housel points toward isn't a bigger number — it's deciding, deliberately and in advance, what enough actually means to you, and treating that decision as fixed even as your income or net worth changes.

## Why it matters
This distinction matters because it explains a pattern visible in people at every income level: the sense of "almost enough, just a bit more and I'll be set" doesn't reliably go away as income rises, because the target moved along with the income. Spending decisions made in service of a moving target are, by definition, never going to fully satisfy — there's always a next tier, a next comparison, a next lifestyle bracket that makes the current one look merely adequate rather than sufficient. Fixing "enough" as a deliberate, examined choice — rather than letting it drift upward automatically alongside your means — is one of the few reliable ways to convert rising income into rising satisfaction instead of rising want.

## Example from the book
Given the limited public documentation available for this newer title, it's worth being explicit that no specific case or story from the book is being cited here. What's consistent with Housel's established style, including the "Never Enough" chapter of *The Psychology of Money*, is illustrating this through a short account of someone — often someone objectively very successful — who kept redefining what would finally feel like enough, always just past their current position, so that the finish line never actually arrived no matter how far they ran. Expect this chapter to apply that same pattern specifically to lifestyle spending rather than to net worth or investment returns, which is where the earlier book focused it.

## Practical application
Try writing down, in concrete terms, what "enough" spending on a given category — housing, travel, dining out, whatever tends to expand for you — actually looks like, before your circumstances change again. Revisit that written number periodically and notice whether your instinct is to raise it simply because you now can, rather than because your actual experience of satisfaction changed. Treating "enough" as a decision you make once and defend, rather than a number that updates automatically with your income, is the practical core of this idea.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick a category of spending in your life — housing, travel, going out, whatever fits. If your income doubled tomorrow, would your sense of "enough" in that category actually change, or would it simply move up to match?
