# 04 — The Treadmill That Resets Your Baseline

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
There's a persistent gap between what money can buy and what actually produces lasting satisfaction, and the mechanism behind that gap is adaptation: whatever standard of living you get used to quietly becomes your new normal, and your sense of what counts as "good" resets to match it. The bigger house stops feeling spacious once you're used to it. The nicer car stops feeling exciting after a few months of ordinary driving. This isn't a personal failure of gratitude — it's closer to a built-in feature of how humans experience circumstances, and Housel's point is that spending decisions made without accounting for it are quietly self-defeating from the start.

## Why it matters
The treadmill matters because it breaks the intuitive model most people use for spending, which assumes that a better version of something produces a proportionally better experience, indefinitely. In practice, most upgrades deliver a real but temporary lift, followed by a return to roughly the same baseline satisfaction you had before, just at a higher cost of maintenance. That means a huge share of lifestyle upgrades aren't buying lasting improvement — they're buying a brief spike, plus a permanently higher bar for what will trigger the next one. Left unexamined, this pattern can keep someone spending more and more while their actual day-to-day satisfaction stays flat, which is precisely the trap it's easy to miss from the inside.

## Example from the book
Because this is a newer and less thoroughly documented Housel title, it's more honest to describe the kind of illustration he characteristically reaches for than to claim a specific one. Consistent with his style in *The Psychology of Money* and *Same as Ever*, expect this idea to be carried by a short story about someone who achieved a spending upgrade they'd anticipated for years — and found the anticipated payoff lasted weeks, not years, before life simply felt normal again at the new, more expensive level. That kind of anecdote, paired with a plainly stated behavioral pattern rather than a cited study, is the register his books consistently use; take any more specific claim about this book's actual examples as unconfirmed.

## Practical application
Before a significant lifestyle upgrade, it's worth asking not "will this make me happier" but "will this make me happier once I'm used to it" — because the honest answer, for most purchases in this category, is that the boost is real but temporary. That doesn't mean upgrades are never worth it; it means weighing them against their ongoing cost (money, maintenance, the higher baseline they set) rather than against the one-time excitement of the purchase, which is the part that fades fastest and least reliably predicts long-run satisfaction.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of the last upgrade you made — to your home, car, or daily routine — that you were genuinely excited about. How long did the excitement actually last before it became your new normal, and would you have made the same decision knowing that in advance?
