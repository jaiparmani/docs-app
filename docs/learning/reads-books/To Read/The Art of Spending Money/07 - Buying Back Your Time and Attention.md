# 07 — Buying Back Your Time and Attention

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
If there's a single best use of money that Housel keeps returning to across his work, it's this one: the highest-value thing money can buy isn't a possession or an experience, it's control — specifically, control over your own time and attention. This idea carries directly over from the "Freedom" chapter of *The Psychology of Money*, where Housel argued that the ability to wake up and decide what to do with your day is the highest dividend wealth pays. Applied specifically to spending, the idea becomes a filter: spending that buys you more control over your time and attention tends to hold up as worthwhile far better than spending that merely buys a nicer version of something you already have.

## Why it matters
This matters because "buy time" is one of the few pieces of spending advice that seems to survive the adaptation treadmill described elsewhere in this book. A nicer car or a bigger television delivers a temporary lift and then becomes the new normal, but reclaiming an hour of your day — by paying someone else to do a task you dislike, by choosing a shorter commute over a bigger house, by having the financial slack to say no to work you don't want — tends to keep paying off in a way that possessions don't, because the value isn't in the thing itself but in what you get to do with the freed-up time and attention, which renews every single day. It reframes "expensive" purchases that buy convenience or autonomy as potentially better value than cheaper purchases that buy status or novelty, even when the sticker price runs the other way.

## Example from the book
Consistent with the caveat that applies throughout this set of notes, this is a newer and less externally documented book, so no specific story is being asserted here. What's safe to say, because it's a direct continuation of an idea Housel has stated explicitly in his earlier work and interviews, is that this book very likely revisits and extends the "Freedom" argument from *The Psychology of Money* — probably through another short, concrete anecdote about someone who traded money for time (a shorter commute, delegated chores, the ability to leave an unpleasant job) and found that trade held its value in a way that a comparable amount spent on possessions did not.

## Practical application
When evaluating a purchase, add a specific question to the usual cost-benefit thinking: does this buy me more control over my time and attention, or does it just buy me a nicer version of something I already have enough of? Purchases that free up time — even indirectly, by reducing friction, decision fatigue, or a daily irritant — deserve to be weighted more heavily than their price tag alone suggests, precisely because their value compounds every day you have them, unlike most possessions.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a recent purchase that gave you back time or attention — even a small amount — versus one that gave you a nicer object. Which one, honestly, are you still glad about today?
