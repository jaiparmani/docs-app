# 08 — The Two-Pizza Team

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The "two-pizza team" is Amazon's own name for a specific org-design rule: teams should be small enough to be fed with two pizzas — roughly six to ten people. The letters and surrounding commentary describe this as more than a joke about team size; it's a structural mechanism for preserving the autonomy and speed of a small startup team inside a company that had grown into tens of thousands of employees. Two-pizza teams are typically given a specific, measurable business or technical objective, real ownership over how they pursue it, and enough independence from other teams that they don't need extensive cross-team coordination or approval to move.

## Why it matters
Coordination cost grows nonlinearly as an organization scales — every additional team that has to be consulted, aligned with, or approved by before a decision can be made adds friction, and that friction compounds. The letters connect the two-pizza team structure directly to the high-velocity decision-making and Type 2 decision themes discussed elsewhere: a small team with real ownership of a defined problem can make its own fast, reversible decisions without routing them through the layers of alignment that a single large, centralized team of the same total headcount would require. The tradeoff is real and acknowledged elsewhere in Amazon's culture — smaller autonomous teams can duplicate effort or diverge from each other — but the letters treat the speed and ownership gained as worth that cost for most of the company's work.

## Example from the book
The two-pizza team structure is described as underlying much of Amazon's internal engineering organization, including the early infrastructure teams that eventually became Amazon Web Services — small, empowered teams building specific services with clear interfaces to what other teams needed, rather than one large monolithic engineering organization making every infrastructure decision collectively. The letters frame this internal structure as a direct precursor to AWS's external API-driven service model: the discipline of building small, independently owned, well-interfaced internal teams and services translated naturally into offering those same well-defined services externally to other companies.

## Practical application
Look at a team or project you're part of that feels slow specifically because of coordination overhead — needing sign-off from multiple groups, waiting on shared resources, needing broad alignment before small decisions. Ask whether the underlying work could be split into a smaller, more independently owned unit with a narrower, clearer mandate, even if that means some duplicated effort elsewhere — and whether the speed gained would be worth that tradeoff for this particular piece of work.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your own work does most of your time go to coordinating with other people rather than doing the actual work — and if that coordination overhead were cut in half, what would you be able to ship that you currently can't?
