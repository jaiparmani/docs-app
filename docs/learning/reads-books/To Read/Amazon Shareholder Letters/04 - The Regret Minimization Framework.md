# 04 — The Regret Minimization Framework

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The regret minimization framework is the decision tool Bezos has described using for major life and career choices — most famously, the decision to leave a stable, well-paying finance career in New York to start Amazon in 1994. The method: project yourself forward to age 80, and ask which choice you'd regret more not having tried. The letters and surrounding commentary describe Bezos applying this specifically to the Amazon decision — reasoning that at 80, he wouldn't regret having tried and failed at an internet bookstore venture, but he would regret never having tried at all, especially given how certain he was that the internet was going to be a big deal.

## Why it matters
Most decision frameworks optimize for minimizing near-term risk — will this fail, will I look foolish, will it cost me something I currently have. Regret minimization deliberately changes the time horizon and the question: it's not "what's the safest choice right now" but "which choice will I be able to live with, decades from now, having made." This reframing matters most for exactly the decisions where near-term risk and long-term regret point in opposite directions — leaving a stable job for an uncertain venture, saying something difficult now to avoid a bigger problem later, taking a harder path because it's the one you'd actually respect having taken. The framework is a way of forcing the long-term self's preferences into a decision that would otherwise be dominated by the near-term self's fear.

## Example from the book
The framing appears in Bezos's own account of the founding decision: in 1994, he was working a secure, high-paying job at a hedge fund in New York when he came across statistics on the early growth rate of internet usage and decided to act on it by starting an online bookstore. Applying the regret minimization framework, he reasoned forward to age 80 and concluded that regretting a failed attempt would be far less painful than regretting never having tried something he believed in — a calculation that, notably, wasn't about predicting success (he's said elsewhere he expected the odds of failure were high) but about which kind of regret he'd rather carry.

## Practical application
For a decision you're currently avoiding because it feels risky in the near term, run the same exercise explicitly: picture yourself decades from now, and ask which outcome — having tried and possibly failed, or never having tried — you'd regret more. Write both scenarios out in a sentence or two rather than just imagining them; making them concrete on paper tends to surface which one you actually mean when you say you'd "regret" it.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a decision you're currently avoiding specifically because of near-term risk, where you already suspect that at 80 you'd regret not having tried more than you'd regret having tried and failed?
