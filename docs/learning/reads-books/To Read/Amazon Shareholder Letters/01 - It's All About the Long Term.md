# 01 — It's All About the Long Term

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The 1997 letter to shareholders — Amazon's first, written the year the company went public — opens with the line "It's All About the Long Term" and lays out, in explicit terms, that Amazon would make decisions based on long-term market leadership and customer value rather than short-term profitability or Wall Street's quarter-to-quarter expectations. Bezos states plainly that the company would continue to make bold investment decisions, would weigh them against long-term leadership rather than short-term profit or reaction, and would not necessarily optimize for GAAP accounting profits if doing so worked against long-term cash flow and market position. This single letter became the foundational document of the entire collection: Amazon reprinted it as an appendix to every annual letter for roughly two decades afterward, a practice unusual enough among public companies that it became a recognizable signature of how the company described itself to investors.

## Why it matters
Public companies are structurally pulled toward short-term thinking — quarterly earnings calls, analyst expectations, and a shareholder base that can rotate every few months create pressure to smooth results and avoid short-term pain even when it would produce better long-term outcomes. The 1997 letter functions as a pre-commitment device: by stating the long-term orientation explicitly and publicly, in writing, before the pressure arrived, Bezos gave Amazon (and himself) a documented standard to point back to whenever a decision — entering a low-margin business, investing heavily in infrastructure before it was needed, absorbing years of losses in a new segment — drew criticism from investors expecting faster returns. Reprinting the letter every year wasn't nostalgia; it was a reminder to shareholders, employees, and Bezos himself that the original terms of the relationship hadn't changed.

## Example from the book
The 1997 letter lists concrete decision rules that follow from the long-term framing: Amazon would continue to invest aggressively to extend and solidify its market leadership position, even measuring some of that investment against future rather than current profitability; it would make decisions and weigh trade-offs differently than some companies might, favoring cash flow and market share over quarterly earnings and Wall Street reactions; and it would share both successes and failures candidly rather than only the flattering parts. Later letters return to this passage explicitly — the 2000 letter, written just after the dot-com crash wiped out most of Amazon's market value, references the original long-term framing directly as the reason the company didn't change course despite the stock collapse.

## Practical application
Write your own one-paragraph "long-term letter" for a project, role, or goal you're currently accountable for — the standard you're committing to before short-term pressure arrives, stated specifically enough that you could reread it in a year and know whether you'd held to it. The value isn't the exercise of writing it; it's having a fixed reference point to check decisions against later, when the in-the-moment pressure to optimize for the next quarter, the next review cycle, or the next immediate reaction is strongest.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you wrote down, right now, the standard you'll hold yourself to under pressure — and reread it a year from now — how confident are you that you'd have actually held to it, versus quietly revised the standard once the pressure showed up?
