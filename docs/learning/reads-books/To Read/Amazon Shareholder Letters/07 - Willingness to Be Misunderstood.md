# 07 — Willingness to Be Misunderstood

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A recurring line across the letters, first appearing in the 1997 letter and echoed in later ones, is that Amazon would need to be willing to be misunderstood for long periods of time in order to pursue genuinely long-term, non-obvious opportunities. The letters frame this not as an incidental cost but as a near-requirement of the long-term strategy described elsewhere: if a decision were immediately understood and applauded by the market, it likely wasn't ambitious or non-obvious enough to be a real source of long-term advantage — genuinely valuable long-horizon bets tend to look questionable or even wrong from the outside until enough time has passed for the payoff to become visible.

## Why it matters
Being misunderstood is uncomfortable in a specific, corrosive way for a public company: it shows up as falling stock price, critical analyst notes, press skepticism, and internal pressure from employees and investors who want reassurance that the strategy is working. The letters treat the willingness to sit through that discomfort — without capitulating to it by reversing course just to relieve the pressure — as the actual, load-bearing test of whether the long-term commitment described in the 1997 letter was real or just rhetorical. It's one thing to state a long-term philosophy in a letter; it's another to hold to it through several years of a falling stock price and public doubt, which is closer to what actually happened to Amazon multiple times, most visibly after the dot-com crash.

## Example from the book
The clearest test case the letters point back to is the period after the 2000 dot-com crash, when Amazon's stock lost roughly 90% of its value and widespread commentary treated the company as a cautionary tale of internet-era overreach rather than a durable business. The letters from that period explicitly reference the original 1997 long-term framing as the reason the company continued investing in infrastructure and expansion rather than retrenching to preserve short-term numbers — a choice that, from outside the company at the time, looked to many observers like denial rather than conviction, and only later read as the latter.

## Practical application
Before starting something ambitious and non-obvious, write down in advance what the skepticism or misunderstanding will probably look like from the outside — the specific criticism you expect to hear while it's still in progress and not yet vindicated. Having written it down beforehand makes it recognizable as the expected cost of the bet when it actually arrives, rather than something that reads as new evidence you were wrong.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a decision you've quietly reversed, or avoided making at all, mainly because you weren't willing to be misunderstood by people whose good opinion you wanted to keep — even though part of you suspected the decision was right?
