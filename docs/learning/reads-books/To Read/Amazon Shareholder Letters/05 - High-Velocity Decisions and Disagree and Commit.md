# 05 — High-Velocity Decisions and Disagree and Commit

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The letters describe high-velocity decision-making as a deliberate defense against organizational slowdown as a company grows — the idea that most decisions should be made with roughly 70% of the information you wish you had, rather than waiting for 90%, because waiting for near-certainty is usually slower than the cost of being occasionally wrong. Paired with this is "disagree and commit": a principle for what happens when a decision-maker and a team member genuinely disagree about a course of action. Rather than requiring full consensus before moving, the letters describe a leader saying, in effect, "I know we disagree on this, but will you gamble with me on it" — and the team member committing fully to the decision despite the disagreement, rather than either forcing more debate or quietly under-investing in a decision they didn't personally choose.

## Why it matters
As organizations grow, the natural tendency is for decisions to slow down — more stakeholders, more process, more desire for consensus before committing resources. The letters treat this slowdown as a primary symptom of the "Day 2" decline they warn against elsewhere, and high-velocity decision-making as the direct countermeasure: most decisions are reversible (discussed further as "two-way doors" in a related theme), so the cost of moving too slowly on them usually exceeds the cost of occasionally getting one wrong and correcting course. Disagree and commit solves a related but distinct problem — the danger that requiring full agreement before acting either kills good ideas that don't have unanimous support, or creates a culture where people who disagreed quietly withhold effort once a decision goes the other way, which undermines it regardless of whether the decision was right.

## Example from the book
The letters describe disagree and commit being used on real internal disagreements about show or product decisions — including specifically noting that Amazon Studios greenlit a particular series over the explicit disagreement of a senior team member, who nonetheless committed fully to its execution once the call was made rather than working half-heartedly or continuing to relitigate the decision. The letters frame this as saving enormous time and energy compared to the alternative of escalating every disagreement until unanimous consensus was reached, while also noting that disagree and commit is not a one-way tool used only by senior people on juniors — it's described as most powerful when used in both directions.

## Practical application
Next time you're part of a decision you disagree with but that has already been made by someone with legitimate authority to make it, try the explicit move the letters describe: state your disagreement clearly and on the record once, then commit fully to making the decision succeed rather than either re-litigating it repeatedly or quietly under-investing. Separately, for decisions you're sitting on waiting for more certainty, ask whether you're actually near 90% information or whether you've been at 70% for a while and are just uncomfortable moving without more.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a decision at work you disagreed with but that got made anyway. Did you commit fully to it, or did some part of you keep quietly working against it — and if the latter, what would it have taken to actually let it go?
