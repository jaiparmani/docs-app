# 06 — Type 1 and Type 2 Decisions

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The letters describe a distinction between two categories of decisions, sometimes called Type 1 and Type 2, and elsewhere described with the "one-way door" and "two-way door" metaphor. A Type 1 / one-way-door decision is consequential and essentially irreversible — once you walk through, you can't easily walk back — and these genuinely deserve careful, slow, highly deliberate analysis involving broad input. A Type 2 / two-way-door decision is reversible: if it turns out to be wrong, you can walk back through and undo most of the damage. The letters argue that most decisions in a growing organization are actually Type 2, but get treated with the same heavyweight process appropriate to Type 1 — and that this mismatch is a primary cause of organizations slowing down as they scale.

## Why it matters
The practical failure mode the letters describe is a large organization applying a single, uniform decision-making process to every decision regardless of type — the same number of approvals, the same amount of analysis, the same committee review — because that process was originally calibrated for the genuinely irreversible, high-stakes decisions where it's warranted. Applied indiscriminately to reversible decisions, the same process becomes pure friction: it slows down experiments and course-correctable choices that would benefit from being made quickly by a single empowered person or small team, precisely because being wrong is cheap and correctable. The letters treat correctly sorting decisions into these two buckets — and building different processes for each — as one of the more actionable, mechanical fixes for organizational slowdown.

## Example from the book
The letters describe watching decision-making authority get pushed to fewer and fewer senior people as an organization grows, with even reversible, low-stakes decisions routed through senior approval "just in case" — a pattern that produces slow decisions, unambitious risk-taking (since senior time is scarce and expensive, only decisions that seem safely justifiable get proposed), and, worse, worse decisions overall, since a single senior decision-maker with limited time and context often knows less about a specific situation than the person closer to it. The letters advocate explicitly pushing Type 2 decisions down to smaller teams or individuals, closer to the actual information, and reserving the slow, broad-input process for genuine Type 1 decisions.

## Practical application
Take a decision currently stuck waiting on approval or more analysis, and explicitly classify it: is it truly a one-way door (hard or costly to reverse), or a two-way door dressed up as one because that's the default process available? For anything that's genuinely Type 2, consider whether it could be made faster by a single accountable person acting on incomplete information, with a clear plan to course-correct if it turns out wrong, rather than routed through a process built for irreversible stakes.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a decision you're currently slow-walking through approvals or analysis. If you're honest about it, is it actually a one-way door — or have you been treating a two-way door like one because that's just how decisions get made where you are?
