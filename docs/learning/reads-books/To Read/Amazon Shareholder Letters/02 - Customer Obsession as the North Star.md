# 02 — Customer Obsession as the North Star

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Across the letters, Bezos repeatedly draws a distinction between being "competitor-focused" and being "customer-focused," and states a clear preference: Amazon aims to be the most customer-obsessed company on the planet. The letters describe this as more than a values statement — it functions as an actual decision-making filter. When a choice arises between what a competitor is doing and what would genuinely serve the customer, the letters describe customer obsession as the tiebreaker, and Bezos argues that customer-focused companies are more resilient than competitor-focused ones because they're responding to something durable (what customers actually want) rather than something reactive (what a rival just did).

## Why it matters
Competitor-focused strategy has a built-in ceiling: it can only ever produce a response to what already exists, which means a competitor-focused company is structurally incapable of getting far ahead of the market, since its own compass is set by what others are doing rather than by an independent read of unmet need. Customer obsession, in the letters' framing, is what allows a company to invent things customers didn't know to ask for — because the question being asked isn't "what is our competitor doing" but "what would genuinely be better for the customer," a question with no ceiling set by anyone else's current roadmap. The letters treat this as the actual explanation for Amazon's expansion into areas like third-party marketplace sales (letting outside sellers compete directly against Amazon's own retail listings) and free/fast shipping investments that depressed near-term margins — decisions that looked irrational from a competitor-focused lens but followed directly from a customer-focused one.

## Example from the book
The letters point to third-party sellers on Amazon's own marketplace as a clear test of whether customer obsession was real or just stated. Allowing outside merchants to list products directly alongside, and sometimes cheaper than, Amazon's own inventory was resisted internally by teams whose job was to sell Amazon's own retail inventory — it looked like inviting competition onto your own platform. The letters describe the decision surviving that internal resistance because it served the customer's actual interest (more selection, better prices, the ability to compare) even though it complicated a competitor-style analysis of who was "winning" a given sale.

## Practical application
In your own work, notice the next time you catch yourself asking "what is our competitor / the other team / the market doing" as the first question when a decision comes up. Try re-asking it as "what would genuinely be better for the end user or customer here" instead, before looking at what anyone else has done, and see whether the two questions actually point to different answers — that gap is often where the more durable decision lives.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a recent decision you made mostly by looking at what a competitor, peer, or rival was doing. If you'd instead started from "what does the person I'm serving actually need," would you have landed somewhere different?
