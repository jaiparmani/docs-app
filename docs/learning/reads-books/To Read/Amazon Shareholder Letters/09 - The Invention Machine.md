# 09 — The Invention Machine

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Several letters describe Amazon's approach to innovation as requiring a large volume of experiments, most of which will fail, in order to produce the small number of big wins that actually matter at scale. The letters state this relationship explicitly as a matter of arithmetic rather than bad luck: because the potential payoff from a genuine success (a new business line, a new product category) is asymmetric and can be enormous, while the downside of a failed experiment is bounded and limited, a portfolio of many bets is expected — by design, not by mistake — to include far more failures than successes, and that's treated as evidence the company is taking big enough swings rather than evidence something is wrong.

## Why it matters
Most organizations are structurally averse to visible failure — a failed product or initiative shows up in performance reviews, budget discussions, and public commentary as a mistake to be explained, which creates strong incentives to only attempt things nearly certain to work. The letters treat this aversion as directly incompatible with genuine invention: if every project you greenlight succeeds, the letters argue, that's actually evidence you weren't taking enough risk, not evidence of good judgment, because the highest-value inventions are by nature uncertain bets that could plausibly fail. The letters explicitly try to reset the frame investors and employees judge failures by — not "did this specific bet pay off" but "is the overall portfolio of bets, including the necessary failures, producing enough big wins to justify the losses on the rest."

## Example from the book
The letters point to Amazon's own visible failures alongside its successes as evidence the philosophy was real rather than rhetorical — the Fire Phone is discussed as a costly, publicly visible failure, written off at meaningful expense, sitting in the same period as the early success of the Kindle and the buildout of AWS. The letters frame the Fire Phone not as an embarrassment to explain away but as the expected cost of a company still willing to make big, uncertain bets — explicitly noting that the payoff from a single success like AWS or Prime can be large enough to cover the cost of many failed experiments like the Fire Phone many times over.

## Practical application
Look at your own recent track record of attempted initiatives, projects, or bets. If close to all of them succeeded, treat that itself as a signal worth investigating — it likely means you've been selecting only for near-certain outcomes rather than genuinely uncertain, higher-upside ones. Consider deliberately including at least one bet in your current portfolio of efforts where you're genuinely unsure it will work, sized so that a failure is affordable, specifically because the absence of any such bets is itself evidence of excess caution.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If nearly everything you've attempted in the last year succeeded, what does that actually tell you about the size of the bets you've been willing to make — and what's the biggest bet you've avoided specifically because you weren't sure it would work?
