# 09 — Dabasir and the Debt

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
One of the book's most concrete chapters follows Dabasir, a camel trader who spent beyond his means, fell deeply into debt, and — unable to face his creditors — fled Babylon entirely rather than pay what he owed. His escape doesn't free him: he's captured and ends up enslaved in a foreign land, herding camels for a desert chief. There, the chief's wife, Sira, presses him on why a man who calls himself free still carries himself like someone in chains, and draws out of him the truth: he ran from debt rather than facing it, and in his own mind he's been a slave to it long before he was ever sold as one. Her challenge to him is that being truly free again means going back and repaying what he owes, not merely escaping the people he owes it to.

## Why it matters
This parable exists to answer the question the earlier cures don't fully address: what do you actually do if you're already in debt, not just failing to save? Dabasir's path back isn't a clever trick — it's a specific, disciplined budget applied consistently over time: he resolves to live on a portion of his earnings, direct another portion to steadily repaying each creditor a little at a time rather than all at once, and still set aside something for himself so the plan doesn't collapse the moment it gets hard. The chapter's real argument is that debt is escaped by a repayment system sustained over years, not by a single windfall or by running further away.

## Example from the book
Once Dabasir commits to the plan, he works his way into trading camels again, using the same skill that once supported his extravagant lifestyle, but this time under a strict split of his earnings — a portion for daily living, a portion paid systematically toward his old debts back in Babylon, and a portion kept and saved for himself, so that even a man deep in debt is still practicing the first cure rather than waiting for the debt to clear before he starts. Over time, and through consistent repayment rather than any single dramatic reversal, he clears what he owes, earns back his standing, and eventually returns to Babylon a free man in every sense, not just a legal one.

## Practical application
If you're carrying debt rather than trying to build savings from a clean slate, the book's advice is to still apply a version of the same split rather than treating debt repayment and saving as mutually exclusive: set a workable percentage for necessary living, a percentage systematically directed at repayment (spread across what you owe rather than panicked lump payments), and a small percentage still kept for yourself, so the plan is sustainable rather than something you abandon the first hard month. The core reframe is Sira's: avoiding your creditors isn't the same as being free of them, and the way out is a plan you can actually sustain, not a plan you hope to escape from.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you're carrying any debt right now, is your current approach to it closer to Dabasir before he met Sira — avoidance — or closer to his budget afterward — a sustained, specific plan?
