# 03 — Pay Yourself First

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
The first cure — "start thy purse to fattening" — is the book's most famous idea and the seed everything else grows from: save at least one-tenth of everything you earn, set aside before any other expense, and let it accumulate untouched. Arkad's version of this rule is blunt about sequencing. It isn't "save what's left after expenses"; it's "pay yourself first, then figure out how to live on the rest." The order matters more than the number, because expenses have a way of expanding to consume whatever income is available to them unless a portion is removed before that can happen.

## Why it matters
This is the behavioral insight underneath the whole book: most people don't have a savings problem because they lack income, they have one because saving is treated as optional and last, after every other claim on their money has already been honored. By the time bills, rent, and daily wants have taken their share, there's rarely anything left to set aside — not because the amount saved would have been large, but because it was never protected in the first place. Flipping the order so saving happens first, automatically, removes the need to rely on willpower at the moment of every individual spending decision.

## Example from the book
When Arkad asks Algamish what one part means, exactly, the money lender is characteristically blunt: not nine-tenths, not eleven-hundredths — one-tenth, kept for himself, no matter how small his earnings feel. Arkad admits later that at first this seemed impossible; his wages barely covered his needs as it was. But he tries it, finds that he can, in fact, live on nine-tenths of what he'd been spending on ten-tenths, and that the purse that used to feel flat begins, however slowly, to fatten. The lesson Arkad passes on to his own students is that the amount saved matters far less at the start than the discipline of never skipping it.

## Practical application
Set the fraction you'll save before you look at what you "need" to spend, and make it automatic rather than a decision you re-litigate every payday — a standing transfer the moment income arrives, not a hopeful intention to save whatever's left at the end of the month. If a full tenth feels unworkable right now, the book's own arc suggests starting smaller and increasing it is far better than waiting for an income level where saving will supposedly feel easy — for Arkad's friends, that income level never actually arrived, because the habit, not the number, was the missing piece.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If a fixed percentage of your income were removed the moment it arrived, before you saw it, would your current lifestyle actually be impossible — or just less convenient than the one you have now?
