# 10 — Simple, Timeless, and Thin

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
It's worth being honest about where this book sits relative to other personal finance writing, including books already in this collection. Clason originally wrote these as a series of separate pamphlets in the 1920s, distributed by banks and insurance companies as financial-literacy handouts, later collected into the single volume most people know today. That origin explains both its strengths and its most common criticism. It's genuinely praised for being simple and durable — the core principles (save a portion of everything you earn, protect what you save, seek real expertise before investing, own rather than only rent) have stayed essentially unchanged in relevance for a century, even as the specific financial instruments around them have changed completely. The most common complaint, especially from readers who come to it after more analytically rigorous personal finance writing, is that it's repetitive and simplistic — the same handful of principles restated across different Babylonian characters, without much variation or deeper mechanism underneath them.

## Why it matters
This isn't a knock so much as a fact about what kind of book it is, and it's useful to know going in rather than discovering it partway through. The parable format is built for memorability, not for rigor — it's designed to make "pay yourself first" stick the way a bullet point never would, which is presumably exactly why banks handed these out as pamphlets rather than reports. But that same design choice means the book rarely goes deeper than restating its principles in a new story; it doesn't show you data on why people fail to save, doesn't examine the psychological mechanisms that make budgeting hard, and doesn't grapple with nuance or edge cases the way more evidence-driven writing does.

## Example from the book
The contrast is sharpest against a book you've already read: Morgan Housel's writing (The Psychology of Money, Same as Ever) covers overlapping territory — save consistently, avoid unnecessary risk, respect compounding — but grounds each idea in behavioral research, historical case studies, and an explicit interest in why people behave irrationally with money even when they know better. Clason's approach is the opposite: state the principle, dramatize it through an ancient Babylonian character, and trust that the story itself does the persuading. Both arrive at similar conclusions about saving and patience, but Housel's version is built to explain the mechanism, while Clason's is built to be remembered.

## Practical application
Read this book for what it's good at: a first, highly memorable framing of a small number of principles that are still true, useful as a way to actually internalize "pay yourself first" rather than just nod along with it. Don't expect it to hold up as an evidence-based treatment of financial behavior, and don't expect much new information once you've absorbed the core cures — the value here is repetition and stickiness, not depth. If you want the same conclusions with the empirical grounding underneath them, that's what the more research-driven books on the same shelf are for.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Which format actually changes your behavior more reliably — a memorable story that tells you what to do, or a research-grounded explanation of why you don't already do it?
