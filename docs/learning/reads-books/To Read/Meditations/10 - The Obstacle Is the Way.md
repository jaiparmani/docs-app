# 10 — The Obstacle Is the Way

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
In one of the journal's most compact and influential passages, Marcus arrives at an idea that later gave Ryan Holiday's popular book its title: that what stands in the way of an action can, with the right mindset, become the action itself. An obstacle blocking one path forward doesn't have to simply stop you — the mind has the capacity to treat the obstacle as new material to work with, adapting the goal around it rather than being defeated by it. What impedes a specific plan does not necessarily impede the underlying intention behind the plan, and a resourceful mind can convert the very thing blocking it into the next step, rather than treating the blockage as pure loss.

## Why it matters
This idea matters because it's the most actively practical extension of everything else in the book — it takes the discipline of perception (events are neutral, judgment adds the difficulty) and applies it specifically to setbacks and failures, the moments where that discipline is hardest to remember. Most people experience an obstacle as evidence that a plan has failed. Marcus is offering an alternative frame: the obstacle is simply the new shape of the situation, and the task is now to work with that shape rather than mourn the old one. This doesn't require pretending the obstacle is good, or that it doesn't cost anything — it requires only refusing to let the obstacle be the end of the story, since the mind retains the ability to redirect around it as long as it stays engaged rather than giving up.

## Example from the book
Marcus writes that a fire, given fuel, doesn't merely tolerate what's thrown into it — it consumes the material and grows brighter because of it, using the very thing that might have been an obstacle to a smaller flame as the means of becoming a larger one. He extends this to the mind facing external difficulty: a disciplined mind can treat whatever happens to it, including setbacks and hindrances, the same way — as material to be used rather than a wall to be defeated by. He returns to a version of this repeatedly when describing frustrations in his own reign — political setbacks, personal losses, physical illness — treating each not as a pure subtraction from his life, but as the current, specific material his reasoning capacity now has to work with.

## Practical application
When a plan hits a genuine obstacle — a setback, a rejection, a resource that disappears — resist the first-pass conclusion that the obstacle has ended the effort. Instead ask a more specific question: what does this obstacle make newly possible, or what does it force you to do differently, that the original plan never would have required? The answer isn't always comfortable and isn't guaranteed to be as good as the original plan would have been, but the discipline itself — treating the blockage as material rather than verdict — keeps you actively working rather than stalled.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a current obstacle you've been treating as pure loss. What would it look like to treat it instead as new material — the actual shape of the problem you now get to work with?
