# 05 — Memento Mori and the Present Moment

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Marcus thinks about his own death constantly — not out of morbidity, but as a deliberate discipline he calls himself back to. The reminder ("memento mori," remember you will die) appears in some form in nearly every book of the journal: life is short, could end at any moment, has probably already used up most of its allotment, and none of that is a tragedy so much as a fact that clarifies what actually matters. The companion idea, which follows almost mechanically from the first, is that the present moment is the only piece of life anyone ever actually has. The past is gone and can't be lived again; the future isn't guaranteed and can't be lived in advance. Only the present is genuinely yours to act in — which means a long life and a short one both, in the end, only ever consist of a series of present moments, no more and no less.

## Why it matters
Put together, these two ideas do real psychological work rather than just sounding somber. If death could arrive at any point, then postponing what matters — treating people well, doing meaningful work, being present — in favor of some future version of yourself who will finally get to it is a bet against your own likely lifespan. And if only the present moment is actually available to be lived, then anxiety about the future and rumination about the past are both, quite literally, spent on something that isn't real right now. Marcus isn't using mortality to induce despair; he's using it as a forcing function, a way of collapsing all the "someday" thinking down into "now," because now is the only place action has ever actually happened.

## Example from the book
Marcus tells himself repeatedly that he could leave life this very hour, and that this thought should govern every act and word rather than paralyze him — since being able to depart from life without complaint at any moment is itself a kind of freedom rather than a threat. He also reasons that even the longest possible life and the shortest possible life ultimately lose the same thing at the moment of death, because both only ever consisted of the present instant repeated — a person who lives to ninety and a person who dies at twenty are each, in a strict sense, only ever giving up "now," since neither the future nor the past was ever truly in their possession to lose.

## Practical application
Rather than using mortality as an abstract fact to acknowledge and set aside, try using it the way Marcus does: as a live filter for the day in front of you. Before deferring an apology, a piece of honest work, or time with someone you care about to "eventually," ask whether that deferral assumes a guarantee you don't actually have. Separately, when you catch yourself spending the day rehearsing the past or auditioning imagined futures, treat that as a specific, correctable habit — the discipline is simply noticing when your attention has left the only moment that was ever actually available to you, and bringing it back.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What are you currently deferring to "someday" that memento mori would suggest doing now instead — and what's the actual reason for the delay, once you look at it honestly?
