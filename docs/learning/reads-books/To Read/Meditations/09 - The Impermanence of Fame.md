# 09 — The Impermanence of Fame

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Writing as one of the most famous and powerful people alive in his era, Marcus repeatedly reminds himself how quickly fame, reputation, and even memory of a person fade — usually within a generation or two, and completely within a few centuries, regardless of how significant that person's actions once seemed. He runs through examples of famous rulers, generals, and thinkers from earlier periods, noting how few people in his own time still remember their names accurately or care about their accomplishments, and applies the same logic forward to himself: whatever posthumous reputation he imagines he's building will erode at the same rate, no matter how central his life currently feels to the world around him.

## Why it matters
This matters as a check on one of the more persistent sources of unnecessary striving: living for the good opinion of others, whether contemporaries or an imagined future audience judging your legacy. If reputation is guaranteed to fade — and Marcus treats this as close to a physical law, not a pessimistic guess — then organizing your choices around securing it is a bet on something that has never once paid off for anyone, however famous. This doesn't push Marcus toward nihilism about his actions; instead it pushes him toward doing what's right because it's right, independent of whether anyone notices or remembers, since the noticing and remembering were never going to last regardless of what he did.

## Example from the book
Marcus lists past emperors, philosophers, and notable figures whose names, in his own lifetime, are already fading from common memory or persist only as a name detached from any real understanding of who they were or what they did — and reasons that his own name will follow the identical path. He reminds himself that even universal, current fame covers only a small portion of the globe at any one time, and that within it, most of those people don't agree on their assessment of you anyway, and won't remember the assessment for long even where they do. He treats the desire for lasting glory as one of the most persistent temptations to release, precisely because it disguises itself as an important, higher-order goal rather than what it actually is — another external outcome outside his control.

## Practical application
Notice how often a decision is quietly shaped by how it will look, be remembered, or be judged by others — in the moment or in some imagined future retelling — rather than by whether it's the right thing to do. When you catch that pattern, try running Marcus's test: imagine the relevant audience, whoever they are, forgetting entirely within a generation, which is roughly accurate, and ask whether the action still seems worth doing on its own merits once the imagined credit is subtracted. If it does, that's a good sign it was the right call regardless of who ends up watching.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a recent decision that was partly shaped by how it would look to others. Subtract the audience entirely — imagine no one would ever know or remember either way — and ask whether you'd still choose to do it.
