# 02 — The Dichotomy of Control

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The organizing idea underneath nearly everything else in Meditations is a strict division between two categories: things that are "up to us" and things that are not. What's up to us is narrow — our judgments, intentions, desires, and our own responses. What's not up to us is almost everything else — other people's opinions and behavior, our reputation, our health, external events, even the length of our own life. Marcus, inheriting this framework from Epictetus, keeps returning to the discipline of sorting whatever is troubling him into one of these two bins, because most suffering comes from treating the second category as if it belonged in the first — as if you could control an outcome, a person's opinion, or the timing of events through sheer will or worry.

## Why it matters
This distinction matters because misplacing something in the wrong category is where anxiety and frustration actually come from. Worrying about whether people will approve of a decision, straining against a diagnosis, being consumed by a political outcome you can't influence — all of these are attempts to control the uncontrollable, and Marcus treats that effort as not just futile but corrosive, because it burns energy that belongs somewhere else. The flip side is liberating rather than resigned: once something is correctly sorted as "not up to me," continuing to fight it is simply a category error, and the only sane move is to redirect that same energy toward the narrow zone that actually is yours — your own judgment, effort, and response.

## Example from the book
Marcus repeatedly reminds himself, in different phrasings across several books, that other people's wrongdoing is their business, not his — that he can control whether he acts justly, but not whether others do, and that expecting otherwise is like expecting a fig tree not to produce figs. He applies the same logic to his own body and mortality: illness, pain, and death are outside his control and therefore not properly his to resent; what remains his is only how he meets them. He also applies it to political and military setbacks during his reign — outcomes he could influence but never guarantee — treating his own conduct, not the result, as the only thing he's ultimately answerable for.

## Practical application
When something is bothering you, run it through the sort explicitly: is this actually within my control, or does it just feel like it should be? A colleague's opinion of you, a diagnosis, a delayed flight, an argument's outcome — these sit in the "not up to me" bin, and the discipline is to notice when you're spending effort as if they didn't. What's left after sorting — your intention, effort, and reaction — is where all your energy is actually allowed to go, and it turns out to be enough, because it's the only thing that was ever actually yours to work with.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of something you're currently anxious about. Sorted honestly, is it in the "up to me" category or the "not up to me" category — and if it's the latter, what would it look like to redirect that same energy into your own response instead?
