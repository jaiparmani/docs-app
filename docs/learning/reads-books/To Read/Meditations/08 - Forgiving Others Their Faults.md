# 08 — Forgiving Others Their Faults

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Marcus returns often to a specific, practical technique for defusing anger at other people: reminding himself, before reacting, that whoever wronged him almost certainly did so out of ignorance rather than deliberate malice. In the Stoic view he inherits, no one knowingly does wrong — people act badly because they mistake something harmful for something good, or because they've never been taught to see clearly, not because they're choosing evil with full understanding of what they're doing. Given that, meeting their fault with anger is both unjust, since they didn't know better, and useless, since anger doesn't actually correct the misunderstanding that caused the behavior in the first place.

## Why it matters
This reframe matters because anger at other people is one of the most common and most corrosive daily disruptions to equanimity, and simply being told to "let it go" rarely works on its own. Marcus's version gives the mind something more specific to do with the irritation than suppress it: understand the other person as mistaken rather than malicious, which changes the emotional register from indignation to something closer to patience, or even pity. It also ties back to the dichotomy of control — you can't control whether someone else acts wrongly, but you can control whether you compound the situation by adding your own reactive anger on top of their original error, which rarely improves anything and often makes you complicit in making a bad moment worse.

## Example from the book
Marcus reminds himself, in one of the journal's most frequently cited passages, to start each morning expecting to encounter difficult people — the meddling, the ungrateful, the arrogant, the deceitful — and to have already decided in advance not to be surprised or destabilized by it, because all of them are acting from a mistaken idea of what's good, the same way he himself has been mistaken before. Elsewhere he reasons that being angry at someone for their fault is a little like being angry at a fig tree for producing figs — it's simply the nature of a person who hasn't yet learned better, and expecting otherwise is the real error. He also repeatedly asks himself what good actually comes from resentment, concluding that the wrongdoer is often harmed most by their own wrongdoing, and doesn't need his anger added on top.

## Practical application
The next time someone frustrates or wrongs you, try inserting Marcus's specific question before reacting: is this person acting from deliberate malice, or from a mistaken idea of what's good, shaped by ignorance you don't fully see? In almost every ordinary case — rudeness, carelessness, thoughtlessness — the honest answer is the latter, and naming it that way changes the shape of the response from retaliation toward something closer to patience. This isn't the same as excusing harmful behavior or refusing to set boundaries; it's a way of keeping your own reaction from adding unnecessary damage on top of theirs.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of someone who recently frustrated or wronged you. If you assume they acted from a mistaken idea of what's good rather than deliberate malice, does your anger toward them change — and if so, what does that tell you about where the anger was actually coming from?
