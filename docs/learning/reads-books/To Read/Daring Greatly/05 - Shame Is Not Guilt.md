# 05 — Shame Is Not Guilt

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Brown draws a sharp line between shame and guilt, building on research from shame scholars including psychologist June Tangney: guilt is the feeling that "I did something bad" — it's about a specific behavior, it stays in proportion to the act, and it usually motivates repair. Shame is the feeling that "I am bad" — it's about global self-worth rather than a specific action, and because there's no discrete behavior to fix, it doesn't point toward repair, it points toward hiding, secrecy, and self-protection. The two feel similar in the moment but function completely differently, and Brown treats confusing them as one of the most consequential mistakes people make in how they talk to themselves and to others, especially children.

## Why it matters
This distinction has real behavioral consequences. Guilt, kept in proportion, is associated with taking responsibility and making amends — it's compatible with growth. Shame is associated with the opposite: research she cites links shame more strongly to addiction, depression, aggression, and disengagement than to any of the corrective behavior people hope it will produce. That means "shaming someone into doing better" — a common instinct in parenting, management, and self-talk — tends to backfire, because shame's actual behavioral output is withdrawal and self-protection, not improvement. Brown's practical reframe is that the goal, when someone (including yourself) does something wrong, is to hold the guilt ("that was a bad choice") without letting it collapse into shame ("I am a bad person").

## Example from the book
Brown discusses this distinction using contrasting internal statements people make after the same kind of mistake: one version stays specific to the behavior ("I made a mistake"), the other generalizes it to identity ("I am a mistake"). She argues the second framing is what shows up disproportionately in her interviews with people describing chronic shame, and that parents, teachers, and managers who default to identity-level language when correcting behavior — even with good intentions — are more likely to produce the hiding and defensiveness shame predicts than the accountability guilt would have produced.

## Practical application
The next time you catch yourself in self-criticism after a mistake, check which sentence you're actually saying internally: is it about the action ("that was the wrong call") or about your worth ("I always mess this up," "I'm not cut out for this")? Rewriting the second into the first — even as a deliberate, slightly awkward exercise — is the practical move Brown's distinction points to. The same applies to feedback you give others: naming the specific behavior keeps the door open to change, while language that lands on their character tends to close it.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Recall the last time you were hard on yourself after a mistake. Was your internal language about the specific action, or did it generalize into a statement about who you are?
