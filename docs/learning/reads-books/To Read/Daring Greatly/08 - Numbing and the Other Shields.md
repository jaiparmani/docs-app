# 08 — Numbing and the Other Shields

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Beyond perfectionism and foreboding joy, Brown catalogs a broader set of everyday strategies people use to avoid the discomfort of vulnerability, which she groups loosely under the idea of an "arsenal" or armory people build up over a lifetime. Numbing is the one she treats as most universal: using distraction, busyness, substances, food, or any other reliable escape to blunt the discomfort of uncertainty and exposure. Alongside numbing she describes patterns like "viking or victim" thinking — treating every interaction as a contest you must either dominate or be dominated in, which forecloses vulnerability by keeping everything adversarial — and a posture of cynicism or performed coolness, where caring visibly about anything is treated as a liability, so detachment gets worn as a kind of protective sophistication.

## Why it matters
The unifying problem with all of these shields is the same one perfectionism has: they don't actually let a person opt out of pain selectively. Brown's key claim about numbing specifically is that emotions can't be selectively muted — you cannot numb vulnerability, grief, or fear without also numbing joy, gratitude, and connection, because the nervous system doesn't have a dial for individual emotions, only a general volume control. That means the trade isn't "less pain for the same amount of good feeling," it's less of everything. The same logic extends to viking-or-victim thinking and cynicism: treating connection as a battlefield or refusing to visibly care does reduce the chance of being hurt, but it reduces the chance of real connection by the same stroke, since both run through the same channel of being open enough to be affected by another person.

## Example from the book
Brown discusses numbing largely through the lens of what she heard across interviews about coping mechanisms people reached for automatically, without necessarily naming them as avoidance — busyness and constant activity came up as one of the most common and most culturally sanctioned forms, precisely because "being busy" reads as virtuous rather than as an escape. She contrasts this with the Wholehearted interviewees in her research, who reported feeling their emotions, including painful ones, more fully rather than less — not because they had less pain to numb, but because they'd stopped trying to selectively dampen it.

## Practical application
Notice your own default numbing behavior — the thing you reach for automatically when a day gets uncomfortable — and instead of treating it as neutral, ask what feeling it's actually interrupting. Separately, notice moments when you default to viking-or-victim framing (treating a disagreement as something to win) or to performed detachment (downplaying that something matters to you), and try naming, even just internally, what you'd have to risk by dropping the posture: usually, it's the discomfort of someone seeing that you actually care about the outcome.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's your most automatic numbing behavior — the one you reach for before you've consciously decided to? What discomfort is it usually standing in for?
