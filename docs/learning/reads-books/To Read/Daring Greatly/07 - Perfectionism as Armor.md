# 07 — Perfectionism as Armor

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Brown draws a firm line between healthy striving and perfectionism, and argues they're often confused because they can look similar from the outside. Healthy striving is self-focused — "how can I improve" — and it's compatible with mistakes, since the goal is progress rather than flawlessness. Perfectionism is other-focused at its core, even though it feels intensely personal: it's the belief that if you do things perfectly and look perfect, you can avoid the pain of blame, judgment, and shame from other people. She describes it as a shield people carry, believing it will protect them from being hurt, when in practice it's heavy, exhausting to maintain, and does the opposite of what it promises — it's usually the very thing standing between a person and being truly seen.

## Why it matters
Because perfectionism is culturally rewarded — a "perfectionist" often reads as diligent, high-standards, admirable — it's one of the hardest armor patterns to recognize in yourself, let alone give up. But Brown's research links perfectionism to worse outcomes on exactly the measures it's supposedly protecting: it correlates with more depression, anxiety, and paralysis, not less, because a standard that requires flawlessness before you're willing to be seen guarantees you'll either avoid starting things or never feel safe finishing them. It also blocks the connection it's meant to preserve — people don't bond with your flawless performance, they bond with the parts of you that are recognizably imperfect and human, which perfectionism is specifically designed to hide.

## Example from the book
Brown writes about her own history with perfectionism, describing herself as a self-identified recovering perfectionist and using her own patterns — over-preparing, over-controlling how she was perceived, treating any visible mistake as catastrophic — as a working example throughout the book rather than only describing the pattern in her interview subjects. She frames this self-disclosure deliberately: writing a book about vulnerability while hiding behind a flawless authorial voice would have contradicted the book's own argument, so she treats her visible imperfections in the writing itself as part of demonstrating what she's describing.

## Practical application
When you notice yourself delaying something until it's "ready" or refusing to share work, an opinion, or an apology until it's airtight, ask whether the standard you're applying is about genuine quality or about pre-empting judgment. A useful test Brown's framing suggests: healthy striving asks "did I do my best," perfectionism asks "what will they think." If the honest answer is the second question, the perfectionism is functioning as armor, not as a quality standard, and the practical move is to lower the bar for "shippable" on purpose and notice what actually happens when something imperfect gets seen.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick something you're currently holding back until it's perfect. If you're honest, is the delay about quality, or about controlling what other people will think of you?
