# 10 — Wholehearted Parenting

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Brown closes the book's argument in the domain she treats as highest stakes: parenting. Her central claim is that children don't learn shame resilience, courage, or how to handle vulnerability from what parents tell them — they learn it from watching what parents do with their own shame and vulnerability. Wholehearted parenting, in her framing, is not the practice of raising perfect, struggle-free children, and it's explicitly not about being a perfect parent. It's about parents being willing to let their children see them daring greatly in their own lives — visibly trying, sometimes failing, and handling both without collapsing into shame — because that modeling teaches the lesson no amount of instruction can.

## Why it matters
This reframes parenting away from outcome management (are my kids succeeding, are they protected from failure) and toward something closer to self-work: the most influential thing a parent does isn't a specific technique applied to the child, it's the parent's own relationship to their own imperfection, playing out in full view. A parent who hides all struggle and only presents a composed, in-control front is — even with the best intentions — modeling the exact armor Brown spends the rest of the book arguing against, teaching a child by example that vulnerability is something to conceal rather than something survivable. Brown's reframing of the core parenting question from "am I a good parent" to "am I a good enough parent, engaged and present" removes the impossible perfectionist standard from the parenting role itself.

## Example from the book
Toward the end of Daring Greatly, Brown includes what she calls a Wholehearted Parenting Manifesto — a direct, personal statement addressed to her own children, laying out what she wants them to internalize: that they are loved and lovable regardless of achievement, that they will learn from watching her, not from what she tells them to do, and that she is committed to letting them see her own courage and struggle rather than hiding it from them. It functions less as an instructional passage than as a concrete demonstration of the book's core argument applied to her own family, in her own words, rather than as advice handed down from a distance.

## Practical application
Instead of asking "how do I get my child to be more resilient or less afraid of failure," ask what your child has actually watched you do the last time you failed at something in front of them — did they see you hide it, minimize it, or handle it openly. The practical move Brown's argument points to is deliberately letting children witness age-appropriate versions of your own struggle and recovery, rather than only presenting them with your successes, since that visible handling is the actual curriculum, regardless of what you separately tell them about resilience.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a recent moment you struggled, failed, or were uncertain in front of a child in your life. What did your handling of that moment actually teach them about what to do with their own future struggles?
