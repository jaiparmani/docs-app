# 04 — Making Threats and Promises Credible

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Alongside commitments, Dixit and Nalebuff treat **threats** ("I will punish you if you don't comply") and **promises** ("I will reward you if you do") as the other two members of the family of "strategic moves." Both face the identical problem: a threat or promise only changes the other side's behavior if it's believed, and it will only be believed if carrying it out is actually in the threatener's own interest *once the moment to act arrives* — not just at the moment the threat was announced. An empty threat is one the target can call, correctly predicting that following through would hurt the threatener too much to be worth it. Making a threat credible, therefore, usually means constraining your own future self in advance, so that when the moment comes, carrying out the threat has become your genuine best option rather than a costly bluff you'd rather not honor.

## Why it matters
This explains why so many threats and promises in ordinary life simply don't work — not because the other side doesn't believe you're angry or well-intentioned, but because they've correctly calculated that following through wouldn't actually serve your own interest when the time comes, so they call your bluff. It reframes "credibility" from a matter of tone or conviction into a structural question: have you actually arranged things so that keeping your word is the rational move for you later, not just a promise made in the present.

## Example from the book
The book returns repeatedly to labor negotiations as a setting where this plays out concretely: a strike threat only carries weight if workers have actually arranged things — a strike fund, public commitments, union rules — that make walking out their genuine best response once bargaining stalls, rather than a threat management can simply wait out, correctly betting that workers can't afford to follow through. The same logic runs the other way: management's promise of a raise contingent on productivity gains is only credible if there's some structure (a contract, a public commitment, a reputational cost to reneging) making it costlier for management to break the promise than to keep it once productivity actually rises.

## Practical application
Take a threat or promise you're currently making, explicitly or implicitly, in a negotiation or relationship. Ask honestly: if the moment came to actually carry it out, would it still be in your interest to do so — or would you quietly back down? If the answer is that you'd back down, the other side likely already suspects that, and the threat or promise isn't doing the work you think it is. If you want it to carry real weight, look for a way to bind your future self — a public statement, a contract, a cost to backing out — rather than relying on willpower alone.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick a threat or promise you're currently relying on to shape someone's behavior. If you strip away your own conviction and just ask what your actual interest would be at the moment it needs to be honored — would you really follow through, or does the other side already know you wouldn't?
