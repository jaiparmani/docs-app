# 06 — Repeated Games and Tit-for-Tat

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A single-shot prisoner's dilemma has no escape — defection is the dominant strategy no matter what. But when the same two parties play the game repeatedly, and each can see how the other played last time, the incentive structure changes completely: cooperation today can be rewarded with cooperation tomorrow, and defection today can be punished with defection tomorrow. Dixit and Nalebuff point to Robert Axelrod's famous computer tournaments, in which political scientists and game theorists submitted competing strategies to play repeated prisoner's dilemma against one another, as evidence for what actually works once the game repeats: the simplest strategy submitted, **tit-for-tat** — cooperate on the first move, then simply mirror whatever the other side did last time — outperformed far more elaborate, devious strategies across repeated tournaments.

## Why it matters
This is the book's answer to the trap laid out in the previous chunk: real, durable cooperation doesn't require altruism, trust, or an enforceable contract — it can emerge from pure self-interest, given two conditions: the interaction repeats, and each side can observe what the other did last time. That's a genuinely different and more optimistic claim than "people should be nicer to each other." It explains why long-running business relationships, alliances, and even rival firms in the same industry over long horizons often settle into stable cooperation without anyone forcing them to, while one-off transactions between strangers don't.

## Example from the book
Axelrod's tournaments are the book's central evidence: entrants submitted strategies ranging from simple to highly complex, including strategies designed to exploit cooperative opponents through occasional betrayal. Tit-for-tat — arguably the shortest, simplest program submitted — won by a wide margin across repeated rounds, because it's forgiving (it returns to cooperation the instant the other side does), it's not exploitable for long (it punishes defection immediately), and it's transparent (the other side can figure out the rule and learn that cooperating is in their own interest too). Dixit and Nalebuff use the result to argue that clarity and predictability of response, not cleverness or unpredictability, is what sustains cooperation over repeated play.

## Practical application
In a relationship where trust is fragile but the interaction is genuinely ongoing — a business partnership, a shared project, a rivalry you can't avoid — adopt an explicit tit-for-tat rule: cooperate first, mirror whatever the other side did last time, and forgive immediately the moment they cooperate again rather than holding a grudge. Critically, make the rule legible to the other side, even by saying it outright — the strategy's power in Axelrod's tournaments came partly from opponents being able to learn what triggered what, which is what made cooperating with it the rational choice for them too.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a relationship where you're currently playing something more complicated or more punitive than tit-for-tat — and would simply mirroring the other person's last move, with immediate forgiveness the moment they cooperate, actually produce a better outcome than whatever strategy you're running now?
