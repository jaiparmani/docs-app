# 01 — Games Are Everywhere

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Dixit and Nalebuff open by drawing a line between two kinds of problems people routinely confuse. In an ordinary decision problem, you're optimizing against a fixed environment — the weather doesn't change its mind because you brought an umbrella. In a **game**, your payoff depends on the choices of another party who is simultaneously trying to anticipate you, and who knows you're trying to anticipate them. That mutual anticipation — "I think that you think that I think" — is what makes something a game in the formal sense, whether it's a corporate pricing decision, a labor negotiation, a poker hand, or a political standoff. The book's opening chapter, a set of short real-world vignettes the authors call their "Ten Tales of Strategy," exists specifically to establish how common this structure is before any formal tools are introduced.

## Why it matters
Most bad strategic decisions aren't failures of intelligence — they're failures of diagnosis. Someone treats a genuinely strategic situation as if it were a plain optimization problem: they pick their "best" price, their "best" offer, their "best" move, based on the world as it currently stands, without asking whether the other side will simply react and change the payoff. A price cut that looks optimal against today's competitor pricing can be a bad move the moment you account for the fact that the competitor will cut back. Recognizing you're in a game — not just facing a fixed situation — is the precondition for every other tool in the book; none of the later chapters (backward induction, credible commitments, mixed strategies) mean anything until you've correctly identified that the other side is a strategic actor, not a fixed feature of the landscape.

## Example from the book
The "Ten Tales of Strategy" chapter deliberately spans very different domains — business rivalries, election contests, card games, military standoffs — to make the point that the underlying logic repeats regardless of the setting. The authors' point in juxtaposing them isn't that these situations are similar on the surface; it's that once you strip away the surface details, the same question is doing the work in each one: what does the other side do in response to what I do, and have I actually accounted for that, or just for the world as it looks right now?

## Practical application
Before your next competitive or negotiating decision, ask explicitly: is the other side going to change their behavior because of what I'm about to do? If the honest answer is yes, you're not solving a decision problem, you're playing a game — and the "obviously correct" move calculated against the current state of the world may be exactly wrong once you factor in the reaction it provokes. Make it a habit to name, out loud, who the other strategic party is and what they're optimizing for before you commit to a move.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a recent decision you treated as pure optimization against a fixed situation — a price, an offer, a plan. Was there actually another party adjusting to you the whole time, and would your decision have changed if you'd modeled their reaction instead of just the world as it looked in that moment?
