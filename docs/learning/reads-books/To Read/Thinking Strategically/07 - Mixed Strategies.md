# 07 — Mixed Strategies

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
In games where an opponent directly benefits from correctly predicting your next move — and you lose exactly as much from being predicted — the rational strategy is often not a single fixed action but a deliberately **randomized mix** of actions, played in specific proportions. This is a **mixed strategy**, and its logic is different from simply "being unpredictable for its own sake": the right mix is the one that makes the opponent indifferent between guessing any of your possible moves, because if any one guess were better for them than the others, they'd exploit it and you'd need to adjust. Any strategy discoverable by a rational opponent — including a fixed "best" move played every time — eventually gets exploited; true equilibrium in these situations requires calculated randomness, not commitment to a single best answer.

## Why it matters
This cuts against a natural instinct to look for *the* optimal move and then play it consistently once found. In games with a pure-conflict element — where your opponent's gain from anticipating you is your loss — consistency is exactly what gets punished. Dixit and Nalebuff's point is that unpredictability isn't a fallback for when you don't have a good plan; a carefully calculated mix, in the right proportions, *is* the plan, and it's the only stable one once a sufficiently attentive opponent is on the other side.

## Example from the book
The book's clearest illustration is the penalty kick in soccer: a kicker who always shoots to the goalkeeper's left becomes trivially exploitable, since the goalkeeper simply learns to dive left every time and the kicker's success rate collapses. The equilibrium instead has the kicker mixing left and right in specific proportions — calibrated so the goalkeeper genuinely can't gain by favoring either side — which is precisely why professional penalty takers and goalkeepers behave close to unpredictably in practice, not out of whimsy but because any detectable pattern would immediately be punished. Dixit and Nalebuff extend the same logic to business and military settings, where a firm or army that always defaults to the same tactic — always underbidding, always advancing on the same flank — hands a pattern-recognizing rival exactly the information needed to counter it every time; deliberately varying tactics, even when one tactic looks marginally better in isolation, protects against being read and exploited over repeated encounters.

## Practical application
In a repeated competitive interaction where the other side has the opportunity to learn your patterns — a negotiation style, a pricing habit, a way you always respond to pushback — deliberately introduce calculated variation into what would otherwise be a predictable choice, even when one option looks like the "best" answer in isolation. The goal isn't randomness for its own sake; it's making sure no single response becomes a pattern a rational, attentive opponent can bank on.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's a move you make so consistently in a competitive or negotiating context that someone paying close attention could predict it every time — and what would it cost you to occasionally do otherwise, on purpose?
