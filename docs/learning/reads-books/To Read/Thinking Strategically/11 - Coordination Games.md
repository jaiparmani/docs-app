# 11 — Coordination Games

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Not every game involves conflicting interests. In a **coordination game**, everyone actually wants the same broad outcome — they just need to land on the *same* choice, and there's often more than one choice that would work equally well if everyone converged on it together: which side of the road to drive on, which keyboard layout to standardize on, which file format to adopt. Dixit and Nalebuff's key point is that multiple equilibria can be simultaneously stable and "rational" in these games, and which one a group actually lands on is often decided by expectation and convention rather than by any underlying payoff advantage of one option over the other. Once everyone expects everyone else to drive on the right, driving on the right becomes individually rational for you too — not because it's inherently better than the left, but because everyone else's expectation makes it so.

## Why it matters
This has a darker mirror image the book uses to sharpen the point: a **bank run**. If enough depositors come to expect that other depositors will withdraw their money, then withdrawing immediately becomes each individual depositor's own rational move — even at a bank that is, on the fundamentals, completely solvent — because the one thing worse than getting your money out is being last in line if the bank actually does run out of cash. The crisis in that case isn't caused by any change in the bank's underlying health; it's caused purely by a shift in what everyone expects everyone else to do, and it becomes self-fulfilling the moment enough people believe it will happen. The same coordination structure that peacefully settles which side of the road to drive on can, with the same mathematics, produce a self-inflicted financial crisis out of shared belief alone.

## Example from the book
Dixit and Nalebuff use standards adoption — keyboard layouts, technical formats, driving conventions — as the benign version of coordination games, where a stable equilibrium is simply whichever convention enough people already expect everyone else to follow, with no need for anyone to prefer it on the merits. The bank run is their example of the same structure turning destructive: the mere fact that a bank run is a coordination equilibrium — sustained purely by the expectation that others will run — means confidence itself, not just solvency, is what keeps a "no-run" equilibrium in place, and a credible public signal (a government guarantee, a visible show of reserves) can be enough to shift expectations back to the stable equilibrium without changing anything about the bank's actual finances.

## Practical application
When a group — a team, an organization — seems stuck failing to adopt something everyone privately agrees would be beneficial (a new tool, a new process, a new norm), consider whether it's genuinely a disagreement about the benefit, or a coordination problem: everyone is individually willing to switch, but nobody wants to be the first to switch while unsure whether others will follow. If it's the latter, the fix isn't more argument for the benefit — it's a credible, public focal point (a firm date, a visible commitment from a respected member) that shifts what everyone expects everyone else to do.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a change your team or group has been individually willing to make for a while, but hasn't — not because anyone disagrees it's better, but because nobody's sure whether everyone else will actually move too? What would function as the public signal that finally shifts everyone's expectation at once?
