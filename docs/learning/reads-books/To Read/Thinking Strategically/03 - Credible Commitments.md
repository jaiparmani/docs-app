# 03 — Credible Commitments

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Conventional wisdom says keeping your options open is always an advantage. Dixit and Nalebuff overturn that: in a strategic setting, sometimes the winning move is to deliberately **destroy your own future options**, because a rational opponent who sees that you can no longer retreat will adjust their own behavior to account for it. This is a **commitment device** — and its power depends entirely on credibility. A commitment that could quietly be reversed isn't a commitment at all; the other side will call the bluff and wait you out. The commitment has to be visible, and genuinely irreversible, before it changes anyone else's calculation.

## Why it matters
This is one of the most counterintuitive ideas in the book precisely because it inverts default intuition. We're trained to protect flexibility — a backup plan, an escape hatch, a way out if things go badly. But in a game against a strategic opponent, the escape hatch you keep is exactly what tells the other side they don't have to yield: they know that if they hold firm, you'll eventually take your softer option instead of following through. Removing that option — publicly, verifiably — is what forces the other side to update their expectations and move first.

## Example from the book
The book's paradigm case for this is Hernán Cortés, who on landing in Mexico had his ships burned (in some tellings of the story, scuttled) so that retreat back across the Atlantic was no longer physically possible for his own men. Dixit and Nalebuff use it as the clean illustration of the mechanism: burning the ships didn't just remove Cortés's own temptation to retreat, it removed the *option* to retreat entirely, which meant his soldiers' resolve to fight was no longer something anyone had to trust his word on — it was a fact anyone could verify by looking at the empty harbor. The commitment worked because it was irreversible and observable, not because Cortés simply announced he wouldn't retreat.

## Practical application
Look at a negotiation or standoff you're currently in where you keep signaling — even subtly — that you have a softer fallback available if the other side holds firm. That fallback is very possibly the reason they aren't moving: they're rationally waiting you out, because they know your stated position isn't your only option. Consider whether visibly and genuinely closing off that fallback (in writing, in front of a third party, through a decision that can't quietly be undone) would change the other side's incentive to concede — and be honest with yourself about whether you're actually willing to burn that particular boat, because a commitment that turns out to be reversible after all is worse than no commitment at all.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's the "ship" you're currently keeping afloat in a negotiation or standoff — the fallback option you haven't closed off — and is its continued existence the actual reason the other side hasn't moved yet?
