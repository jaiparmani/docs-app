# Freakonomics

<small>2 min read</small>

*Steven Levitt and Stephen Dubner*

The book's organizing move is applying economic and statistical reasoning to questions that don't look like economics questions at all: what schoolteachers and sumo wrestlers have in common when incentives create room to cheat, why crack-era street dealers mostly lived with their mothers despite the drug trade's popular image, and — most famously — an argument connecting the legalization of abortion to the drop in crime a generation later. Levitt, the economist supplying the empirical method, and Dubner, the journalist shaping it into narrative, aren't building toward a unified thesis so much as demonstrating a method: treat data as a way to interrogate assumptions everyone already holds, and follow incentives to wherever they actually lead rather than where intuition says they should.

## What it's reputed to do well

This is one of the books most credited with popularizing the "use data to question something surprising" genre that a huge amount of later pop-nonfiction imitates — its reputation is less about any single finding and more about modeling a habit of mind: don't accept the conventional explanation for a social pattern without checking whether the incentives actually support it. The sumo wrestling and schoolteacher cheating chapters, built on relatively clean statistical detective work, are the parts most often cited as the book at its best — legible methodology applied to a question nobody else thought to ask.

## The heads-up

The book's most famous individual claim is also its most contested: the abortion-crime link faced serious methodological pushback from other economists after publication, including a widely cited coding error identified in the original analysis and substantial disagreement about whether the effect holds up under different specifications. That controversy is worth knowing going in, not as a reason to skip the book but as a calibration on how to read it — the value is closer to "here's a compelling case for interrogating data-backed conventional wisdom" than "here are settled findings to cite as fact." Several of the book's other claims have faced smaller versions of the same scrutiny since 2005. Read it for the method and the habit of asking better questions of data, and treat any individual finding as a claim worth checking rather than a conclusion to carry forward unexamined.
