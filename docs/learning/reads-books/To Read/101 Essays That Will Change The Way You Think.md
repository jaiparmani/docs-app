# 101 Essays That Will Change The Way You Think

<small>2 min read</small>

*Brianna Wiest*

This is a collection of short, standalone essays on self-awareness, emotional maturity, and mindset — many of which originated as separate pieces that circulated individually online (on Thought Catalog and similar platforms) before being compiled into a single book. There's no single sustained argument running through it the way a traditional nonfiction book builds one thesis across chapters; instead each essay makes its own smaller point — on self-sabotage, emotional intelligence, the difference between what you want and what you think you should want, how unresolved feelings show up as behavior — and the book's value is in the accumulation of many such observations rather than in one throughline.

## What kind of book this actually is

The honest way to categorize it is as a browsable collection rather than a cover-to-cover argument, closer in structure to a curated anthology of blog posts than to a book like *Think Again* or *Sapiens* that builds one idea across a few hundred pages. That's not incidental to its origin — the essays were written and published individually, for readers encountering one at a time, often shared or read in isolation rather than as part of a sequence, and the compiled book largely preserves that original unit of consumption rather than restructuring the material into a unified argument.

## Why it's plausibly worth the read

The appeal of this format is real and specific: individual essays are short enough to read in a few minutes, self-contained enough to pick up in any order, and aimed at emotionally resonant, practical territory — self-sabotage, boundaries, the gap between busyness and progress — that a lot of readers find genuinely useful as standalone prompts for reflection, distinct from needing an overarching theory to be worth the time. It's a reasonable fit for reading in short sessions rather than sitting down for it start to finish, and several of the individual essays have become popular and widely shared in their own right, which is some evidence that specific pieces land well even independent of the collection as a whole.

## The honest heads-up

The structural tradeoff of any essay-compilation book applies directly here: quality and depth vary considerably essay to essay, since they weren't written as parts of one sustained argument with consistent rigor and pacing, and a reader expecting the kind of built, cumulative case a single-thesis book makes will likely find this format frustrating by comparison. Some essays will land as genuinely sharp; others will read as repetitive of ideas covered elsewhere in the same book, or as more assertion than argument, without much external evidence behind the claims — this is closer to reflective, aphoristic writing than to research-grounded psychology. The realistic way to get value out of it is to treat it as a browsable reference to dip into rather than a book to read straight through expecting a cumulative payoff at the end.
