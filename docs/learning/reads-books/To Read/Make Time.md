# Make Time

<small>2 min read</small>

*Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky*

Knapp and Zeratsky come from Google Ventures, where Knapp created the "design sprint" methodology, and *Make Time* scales that same bias toward small, testable experiments down to the level of a single day. The core system is deliberately narrow: each morning, pick one "Highlight" — the one thing you want to be true you did by the end of the day — then use tactics to "Laser" your focus on it by reducing the pull of infinite-scroll defaults (email, social feeds, news), and close the loop with energy management (sleep, movement, food) so you actually have the capacity to execute. It's explicitly framed as a daily tactical system rather than a philosophy of productivity — the book is structured as a menu of roughly 80-plus short, independent tactics you're meant to sample and keep only what works, not a single argument you're meant to accept wholesale.

## The concrete mechanism, and why it's a different shape than most productivity books

Most productivity books either sell a values-level reframe (find your why, align with your purpose) or a comprehensive system meant to be adopted in full (GTD's full capture-and-review architecture). *Make Time*'s pitch is narrower and more testable: one Highlight a day, chosen deliberately instead of drifting into whatever's loudest, is a low-cost experiment you can run tomorrow morning, and the "Laser" tactics are individually small enough to try and discard without restructuring your whole workflow. That modularity is the book's real design principle, inherited directly from the design-sprint background of its authors — build small, test fast, keep what works — applied to personal time instead of product features. For someone whose actual problem is reactive days eaten by notifications and other people's priorities rather than a lack of an overarching life system, that's a plausibly better-fitted tool than a heavier framework.

## The honest heads-up

The tactic-menu structure is also the book's main weakness: with 80-plus discrete suggestions, a lot of them are minor variations on the same idea (make distraction less convenient, make the important thing more convenient), and the book can read as padded if taken cover to cover rather than sampled. It's also, by its own admission, aimed at individual habits and willpower-adjacent tactics — it has less to say about structural time pressures (a genuinely overloaded calendar, a job with hard external deadlines, caregiving responsibilities) that no amount of Highlight-picking or notification-hiding actually resolves, since those are constraints on time, not distractions from it. And like most attention-management books published in the smartphone era, some of the specific tactics (turning off particular app features, browser extensions) are the most likely parts to feel dated depending on how the underlying apps and platforms have changed since publication — the daily-Highlight and energy-management scaffolding is the part likely to hold up better than the specific tool recommendations.
