# 09 — Fear-Setting: What's the Worst That Could Happen

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Ferriss offers a structured exercise for the vague dread that keeps people from making unconventional choices — quitting a job, starting a muse business, taking a mini-retirement. Rather than trying to talk yourself out of the fear, he has you define it precisely: write out the actual worst-case outcome in specific, concrete detail, then write out what you would concretely do to recover from it if it happened, and separately, what the cost of inaction — of staying exactly where you are for the next six or twelve months — actually is. The vague, undefined version of a fear is almost always worse than the specific, written-down version.

## Why it matters
Undefined fear is what actually paralyzes decisions, not the risk itself — most people avoid unconventional choices because the downside feels catastrophic and unbounded in imagination, even when it's neither, once it's spelled out. Writing the worst case down in specific terms almost always reveals it to be survivable, often reversible, and rarely as bad as the ambient anxiety suggested. Pairing it with the cost of inaction is the second half of the move: it's easy to treat staying put as the safe default, but staying put has its own compounding cost that people rarely account for, because it doesn't feel like a decision at all.

## Example from the book
Ferriss walks through applying this exercise to his own decision to step back from BrainQUICKEN and travel rather than keep running the business conventionally — listing the specific worst outcomes (losing the business, running out of savings, professional embarrassment), a specific recovery plan for each, and the cost of not making the change at all. This exercise, laid out in the book under a chapter built around exactly this "what's the worst that could happen" question, later became the basis for the TED talk he's well known for on the same subject.

## Practical application
Take a decision you've been avoiding because it feels too risky. Write three columns: the specific worst-case outcomes if you do it, what you'd concretely do to recover from each one, and what happens — concretely, not vaguely — if you change nothing for the next six months. Compare the worst case in writing to the worst case in your head; they are rarely the same size.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What decision are you avoiding right now because the worst case feels too big to name — and what does it actually look like, in specific terms, once you write it down?
