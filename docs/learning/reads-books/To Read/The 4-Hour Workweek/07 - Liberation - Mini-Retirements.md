# 07 — Liberation: Mini-Retirements

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Ferriss challenges what he calls the "deferred-life plan" — work for decades, save aggressively, and spend the freedom and travel you actually want at sixty-five — as a bad bet on both ends: it assumes your health and appetite for adventure will be undiminished decades from now, and it front-loads all the difficulty into the years when you have the least freedom to enjoy anything else. His alternative is the mini-retirement: extended periods of travel, learning, or simply disengagement from work taken periodically throughout a working life, rather than a single retirement saved up for the end of it.

## Why it matters
The deferred-life plan treats leisure and work as strictly sequential — first decades of the one, then (hopefully) some of the other — when there's no requirement that they be structured that way. Mini-retirements reframe freedom as something to be distributed across a life rather than banked entirely for its final stretch, which also hedges against the real risk that the banked version never arrives in the form imagined, whether due to health, family circumstances, or the plan simply not working out. It's the "Liberation" payoff of the DEAL framework: elimination and automation aren't ends in themselves, they're what make taking real time away from work logistically possible without everything falling apart.

## Example from the book
Ferriss describes his own extended stays abroad taken during his working years rather than after them — including time spent living in Buenos Aires studying tango and periods spent in other countries pursuing activities like kickboxing training, rather than tourist-style short vacations. These weren't breaks bolted onto a still-running business back home; they were made possible specifically because the elimination and automation steps had already reduced how much of the business required his constant physical presence.

## Practical application
Design one mini-retirement with a real timeframe attached — weeks, not a single long-deferred someday — and work backward from it: what would need to be eliminated or automated in your current work for that time away to be logistically possible without everything breaking. Treat the planning of that trip as a forcing function for doing the elimination and automation work, rather than waiting until the freedom already exists to plan what to do with it.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you had to take a month away from your current work starting next quarter, what would actually break — and is that thing worth fixing regardless of whether you take the trip?
