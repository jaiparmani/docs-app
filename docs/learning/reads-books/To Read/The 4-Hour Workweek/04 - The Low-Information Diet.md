# 04 — The Low-Information Diet

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Ferriss argues that most information people consume — news, industry updates, the constant stream of things that feel important to know — is not actionable and doesn't change any decision the consumer will actually make. He proposes treating information intake the way a nutritionist treats food: as something to be deliberately restricted, not maximized, because the volume consumed has little relationship to the value produced, and a lot of it exists mainly to create the feeling of being informed and diligent while actually just consuming time and generating anxiety.

## Why it matters
This attacks a specific kind of disguised busywork: the habit of reading news, checking updates, and scanning feeds that feels responsible and even virtuous, but rarely leads to a different action than the one you'd have taken anyway. Because it doesn't look like procrastination — it looks like staying informed — it survives most attempts at time-management that only target obviously unproductive activities. Ferriss's point is that "informed" is not the same as "actionable," and that most of what qualifies as current events reaches genuinely important people through other channels regardless — via colleagues, family, or unavoidable second-hand mention — making deliberate avoidance far less risky than it feels.

## Example from the book
Ferriss describes putting himself on a self-imposed news fast, cutting out newspapers and general news consumption for an extended period as an experiment, on the premise that if something genuinely important happened, he would hear about it from other people regardless of whether he'd read it himself. He reports that this held up in practice — nothing that actually mattered to his decisions or his life went unnoticed — while the time and mental space previously spent consuming news was reclaimed for the more targeted work of the book's other steps.

## Practical application
Pick one recurring information habit — news app, industry newsletter, social feed — and go without it for a defined period, a week is enough to test it. Track whether anything you needed to know reached you anyway through people around you, and whether the time reclaimed got used for something with a clearer payoff than staying current did.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Of the information you consumed today, how much of it changed a decision you made — and how much of it just made you feel caught up?
