# 05 — Batching and the End of Constant Interruption

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Ferriss argues that checking email (and, by extension, any similar channel) continuously throughout the day is one of the most expensive habits in a modern workday, not because any single check takes long, but because it fragments attention into pieces too small for real work and trains everyone around you to expect an instant response, which then compounds the interruption problem indefinitely. His fix is batching: process communication in scheduled blocks — a couple of set times a day — rather than reactively, on someone else's schedule.

## Why it matters
Constant availability creates a feedback loop: the faster you respond, the faster people expect you to respond, which pushes response time toward zero and pulls sustained focus toward zero along with it. Batching breaks the loop from your side deliberately rather than waiting for it to break on its own. It also surfaces a fact most people never test: the large majority of messages that feel urgent when they arrive are not actually time-sensitive in the way the sender's tone implies, and holding them for a scheduled block rather than reacting immediately rarely produces a worse outcome than answering instantly would have.

## Example from the book
Ferriss describes setting up an autoresponder that explicitly told correspondents he checked email only at specific times each day, and directed anything genuinely urgent to a different channel. Rather than causing a backlash, this retrained the people who emailed him — most adjusted their expectations within a short period, and the volume of messages requiring truly immediate attention turned out to be far smaller than the volume of messages arriving with an implied sense of urgency attached.

## Practical application
Set two fixed times a day to process email or messages — for instance late morning and late afternoon — and close the inbox the rest of the time. Write a short auto-response or a message to close collaborators explaining the change and naming a channel for genuine emergencies, so the batching doesn't just create silent unavailability. Track for a week how many "urgent" items from the batch turned out to need action before the next scheduled check.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> The last time you dropped focused work to answer a message immediately, would anything meaningfully bad have happened if you'd answered it two hours later instead?
