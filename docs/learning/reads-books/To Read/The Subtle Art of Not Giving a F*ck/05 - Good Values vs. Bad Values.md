# 05 — Good Values vs. Bad Values

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Manson argues that values function as the metrics you use to judge whether your life is going well or badly, so the quality of your values determines the quality of your problems — and since problems never stop coming, choosing better values means choosing better problems to have. He proposes a rough test: good values are reality-based (grounded in something observably true, not fantasy), socially constructive (they improve things for people around you, not just you at their expense), and immediate and controllable (they depend on things you can actually act on, not on circumstances or other people's opinions). Bad values fail all three: they're superstitious, socially destructive, or dependent on things outside your control — like fame, being right, or being liked by everyone.

## Why it matters
This explains why solving a problem often doesn't produce lasting satisfaction: if the underlying value was bad to begin with, solving one problem just clears the way for the next problem generated by the same flawed value. Manson calls this "rock star problems" — a term for complaints that only exist because someone's other, more basic problems got solved, and a new tier of dissatisfaction opened up right behind them. Chasing better circumstances without examining the values generating your sense of success or failure just moves you to a new set of complaints at a higher altitude, not out of complaining altogether.

## Example from the book
Manson tells the story of Dave Mustaine, who was kicked out of Metallica before the band became one of the best-selling rock acts in history, and who went on to found Megadeth, a hugely successful band in its own right — reportedly tens of millions of albums sold. By any external measure, Mustaine achieved massive success. Yet in a later interview he described still feeling like a failure, because his internal metric wasn't "did I build something people love," it was "did I beat Metallica" — a value he could never satisfy no matter how well Megadeth actually did. Manson also recounts his own teenage dream of being a rock guitarist: what he actually wanted wasn't the daily grind of practicing an instrument, it was the fame and adoration of being a rock star — an external, uncontrollable value — and the moment the fantasy met the boring reality of practice, his interest collapsed.

## Practical application
Take a goal you're currently chasing and name the value underneath it explicitly. Run it through the three-part test: is it based on something real, does it help people around you as well as you, and is it something you can actually act on today rather than something that depends on other people's opinions or on chance? If it fails, the goal itself may be fine, but the metric you're using to judge your progress toward it probably needs to change.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a "rock star problem" in your own life — a complaint that only exists because something else went well. What does the complaint reveal about the value underneath it, and is that value one you'd pick on purpose?
