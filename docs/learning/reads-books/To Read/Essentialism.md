# Essentialism

<small>3 min read</small>

*Greg McKeown*

McKeown's argument is a diagnosis before it's a system: most people's actual problem isn't too little time or too few techniques, it's the "undisciplined pursuit of more" — saying yes to too many good opportunities, obligations, and requests because each one, evaluated on its own, seems worth doing. The fix he proposes isn't optimization (do the same amount of stuff, more efficiently) but subtraction: figure out the vital few things that matter disproportionately, and systematically eliminate or say no to nearly everything else, on the premise that "almost everything is non-essential." The mechanism is explicit trade-off thinking — McKeown wants readers to treat every yes as a real cost against other things they could be doing, rather than assuming commitments stack for free — paired with practical discipline around saying no, protecting unstructured thinking time, and building in slack rather than filling every available hour.

## What distinguishes it from adjacent books

This sits on the same shelf as *Make Time* and *The 4-Hour Workweek*, and it's worth being explicit about how it differs, since the three get recommended together often enough to blur. *The 4-Hour Workweek* is fundamentally tactical — automate, outsource, and arbitrage your way to more free time while keeping most of your ambitions intact. *Make Time* is a daily-system book — pick one highlight per day, use specific tactics to protect focus and energy. *Essentialism* argues from elimination rather than tactics: its claim isn't "here are hacks to fit more in" but "the whole premise of fitting more in is the problem," and its solution is fewer commitments made more deliberately rather than the same commitments executed more cleverly. That's a more philosophically ambitious claim than either of the other two books makes, and it's the reason people tend to describe it as changing how they decide what to work on, rather than just changing how they work.

## The fair counter-argument

The book's biggest vulnerability is that ruthless elimination assumes a degree of latitude many readers don't actually have. Saying no to your manager's request, turning down a client, or declining a family obligation is a very different proposition depending on how much power you hold in that relationship — the essentialism framework reads more easily from the vantage point of someone senior enough, or independent enough, to control their own calendar than from an employee, parent, or caregiver whose obligations aren't optional in the way the book's examples often assume. It's also fair to note that "focus on the vital few, cut the rest" is not a new insight — it's a restatement of ideas at least as old as the Pareto principle, and some of the book's critical reception treats it as competently packaged rather than original. And where the book is strongest as philosophy — why to say no — it's comparatively thin as a system for how: unlike a more mechanically detailed productivity book, it offers fewer concrete decision procedures for the genuinely hard cases where two things both look essential and a real trade-off has to get made under uncertainty, leaving a meaningful gap between the diagnosis, which is sharp, and the day-to-day execution, which is left more to the reader's judgment than the book's confident tone might suggest.
