# 04 — Tracking Progress: "How Far I've Come" vs. "How Far I Have to Go"

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The same objective progress — say, 40% done on a project — can be framed two ways: as "I've done 40%" (progress-to-date) or "I have 60% left" (progress-to-go), and which framing motivates more depends on where you are in the pursuit. Early in a goal, small progress-to-date feels encouraging because relative to a starting point of zero, any progress looks like meaningful momentum. Late in a goal, progress-to-go framing works better, because the remaining distance now looks small and finishable, while dwelling on how far you've come stops feeling relevant. The framing that motivates flips somewhere around the midpoint, and using the wrong one for the stage you're in can quietly sap drive that the actual numbers don't justify losing.

## Why it matters
Most tracking tools and habit trackers pick one framing and apply it uniformly across the entire pursuit — a progress bar that always shows percent complete, for instance. That's a mismatch for roughly half of any goal's duration. Understanding that the effective framing changes over time means you can deliberately switch how you narrate your own progress to yourself depending on where you actually are, rather than being stuck with whatever framing your tracker happens to default to, or worse, unconsciously dwelling on the discouraging framing for your current stage without realizing there's an alternative.

## Example from the book
This is close to Fishbach's own research territory, built on studies of loyalty and reward programs — the standard "buy nine coffees, get the tenth free" stamp card. Research on these programs (including the well-known finding that customers given a head start — a ten-stamp card pre-stamped with two stamps, requiring the same eight purchases as a plain eight-stamp card — complete it faster than customers on the unstamped card) shows that visible progress toward a goal accelerates effort as the finish line gets closer, a pattern researchers call the goal-gradient effect. The closer people get, the faster they move, precisely because the remaining gap starts to look small and achievable — the progress-to-go framing naturally takes over near the end. Early on, by contrast, it's the fact of having started — the two free stamps, the visible progress-to-date — that got people meaningfully more engaged than starting from an untouched card, even though the actual remaining work was identical.

## Practical application
For a goal you just started, deliberately track and narrate progress-to-date — "I've already done the first three days," "I've written 1,000 words already" — even if the absolute amount is small, because that framing is what sustains early momentum. Once you're past the midpoint, switch your internal narration to progress-to-go — "only four sessions left," "just 2,000 words to the target" — since that's the framing that will actually accelerate your finishing kick. If you use a tracking app or habit tracker, check whether it's showing you percent-complete or amount-remaining, and know that you may need to do the reframing yourself rather than relying on the tool to switch for you.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> For a goal you're mid-way or further through right now, are you still mentally framing it around how far you've come — and would switching to counting down what's left actually make you move faster?
