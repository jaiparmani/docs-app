# 05 — When Monitoring Backfires: The Licensing Effect

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Tracking progress is generally motivating, but it has a documented failure mode: seeing evidence that you're doing well on a goal can itself become a reason to ease off, because the mind treats the good behavior as a credential that's already been earned and can now be spent. This is the licensing effect (also called moral licensing or self-licensing) — a workout logged, a healthy week recorded, or a savings goal visibly on track can quietly grant yourself permission to skip the next session, order the indulgent meal, or make an unplanned purchase, precisely because the tracker just told you that you're already "the kind of person" who does this.

## Why it matters
This complicates the simple advice to "just track everything" that shows up throughout habit and goal-setting literature, including elsewhere in this book's own chunks. Monitoring is not uniformly good — it depends on whether the record is interpreted as evidence of identity and commitment ("I am someone who does this, so I'll keep doing it") or as a completed credit that offsets a lapse ("I've already banked enough good behavior, so this doesn't count"). The same progress record can produce either read, and which one you land on determines whether tracking accelerates or undermines you.

## Example from the book
Fishbach's own research with Ravi Dhar on self-signaling found this pattern directly: participants who were reminded of prior progress toward a health goal — recalling a recent instance of exercising or eating well — subsequently chose more indulgent, less healthy options than participants who weren't given that reminder. The recalled progress didn't function as encouragement to continue; it functioned as evidence the goal was "under control," which then licensed a departure from it. The effect reversed, notably, when the same information was framed instead as evidence of commitment to the goal rather than as evidence of progress made — reminding people that they cared about the goal (not that they'd already partly achieved it) produced the opposite, goal-reinforcing effect. The content of the reminder mattered less than which question it implicitly answered: "how much have I already done" versus "how much do I actually want this."

## Practical application
When you log progress, notice whether the record is nudging you toward "I've earned a break" or toward "this confirms I'm someone who follows through." If a tracker is starting to function as a permission slip — if you find yourself thinking "I already went to the gym twice this week, so skipping tonight is fine" — try reframing the same entry as evidence of commitment instead of banked credit: not "I've done enough," but "this is who I am, so of course I'm doing it again today." For goals especially prone to this trap (diet, spending, screen time), consider tracking commitment-relevant cues (why the goal matters) alongside or instead of pure completion counts.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a time you used your own recent good behavior as an excuse to slip — what would have changed if you'd read that same track record as proof of who you are, rather than as a balance you'd already paid off?
