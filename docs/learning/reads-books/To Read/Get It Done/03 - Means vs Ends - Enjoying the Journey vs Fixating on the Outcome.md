# 03 — Means vs. Ends: Enjoying the Journey vs. Fixating on the Outcome

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Every goal-directed activity can be experienced two ways: as a means to an end (the run matters because it produces fitness; the study session matters because it produces the exam grade) or as intrinsically enjoyable in its own right, independent of what it produces. Fishbach's research shows this isn't just a matter of temperament — the same activity, identically performed, produces more persistence and more actual enjoyment when it's mentally framed as its own reward than when it's framed purely as instrumental to a distant outcome. Means-framing isn't a nice-to-have on top of the "real" motivation; for long or effortful goals, it often is the thing keeping you in the activity at all.

## Why it matters
Outcome-only framing has a structural weakness: the outcome usually arrives late, arrives once, and is disconnected from most of the individual moments of effort along the way, so it can't reward those moments directly. If the only thing you value is the number on the scale or the diploma at the end, then every single workout and every single study session is, by that framing, worthless in itself — pure cost with no payoff until some distant date. That's an exhausting way to spend months or years, and it's part of why outcome-fixated pursuits are so prone to being abandoned partway through. Treating the process as worth something on its own terms gives you a reward available every day the goal requires effort, not just on the day it's finally achieved.

## Example from the book
Fishbach's experiments on this contrast typically manipulate nothing about the task itself, only the instructions framing it. In one line of studies, participants asked to approach a health-related activity — a workout, a food choice — with attention to how enjoyable or interesting it was performed and persisted noticeably differently than participants given the identical activity but told to focus on the outcome it would produce (weight loss, fitness gains). The means-framed group reported more enjoyment in the moment and, in follow-up measures, greater willingness to repeat the activity later. The task never changed; only the internal question the person was answering while doing it changed — "is this enjoyable right now" versus "is this getting me anywhere" — and that single shift moved both experienced enjoyment and future persistence.

## Practical application
For a goal you're pursuing mainly because of what it produces, deliberately ask, before you start each session, what about the activity itself — not its output — you could pay attention to or make satisfying. That might mean choosing a route you like for a run instead of the fastest one, picking study material you find genuinely interesting within the required subject, or treating a work task as a chance to get better at a skill you enjoy exercising. This isn't about lying to yourself that you love something you don't; it's about noticing that most goal-directed activities have some real, extractable enjoyment in them that outcome-fixation causes you to filter out.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick a goal you're currently pursuing purely for its outcome. Is there anything about the process itself you actually enjoy but have been ignoring because you've only been counting what it produces?
