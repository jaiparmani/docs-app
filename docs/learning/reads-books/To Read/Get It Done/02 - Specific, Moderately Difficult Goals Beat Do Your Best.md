# 02 — Specific, Moderately Difficult Goals Beat "Do Your Best"

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Two separate failure modes undercut goals, and they sit on opposite ends of the same spectrum. On one end is the vague goal — "do your best," "get healthier," "read more" — which fails because it gives the mind no fixed target to measure against, so any amount of effort can be rationalized as good enough. On the other end is the goal set so far beyond current ability that it reads as impossible, which fails because people disengage from targets they've already concluded they can't hit. The sweet spot in between is a goal that is specific (a number, a date, a defined finish line) and moderately difficult — hard enough to require real effort, not so hard that it collapses into "why bother."

## Why it matters
This is a corrective to two popular pieces of folk advice that pull in opposite directions: "just do your best and don't put pressure on yourself" and "set audacious, ten-x stretch goals." Both, taken as general prescriptions, are wrong for the same reason — they abandon the one variable goal-setting research keeps finding matters most, which is specificity paired with a difficulty level the person actually believes is reachable with effort. A goal's power comes less from how inspiring it sounds and more from how precisely it lets you tell, at any moment, whether you're on track.

## Example from the book
Fishbach draws on the well-documented pattern in marathon finishing times: when you plot the distribution of runners' finish times, there's a conspicuous spike of runners finishing just under round-number thresholds — 3:59 rather than 4:01, 4:59 rather than 5:01 — and a corresponding dip just after those marks. Round numbers function as implicit specific goals runners set for themselves without anyone assigning them, and the data show people digging in and finding extra effort in the final stretch specifically to land under the threshold rather than just over it. Runners aiming for a vague "do my best" time show no such clustering; the clustering only appears around the numbers that function as concrete, self-imposed targets. The goal's specificity — a single crossable line — is doing work that a diffuse intention to run well cannot do.

## Practical application
Take a goal you've currently phrased in do-your-best language and force it into a number and a deadline. Not "write more" but "800 words by 9pm four days a week." Not "save money" but "$400 into savings by the last day of the month." Then sanity-check the difficulty: if you're fairly confident you'll hit it without any real effort, tighten it; if you privately suspect it's not achievable no matter what you do, loosen it. The target you want is one where success feels genuinely uncertain but plausible — that uncertainty is what keeps the goal motivating rather than either irrelevant or defeating.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Take your current biggest "do your best" goal and turn it into one number with one deadline. Does naming that number make you want to lower it, or does it reveal you'd already quietly settled for less than you could actually do?
