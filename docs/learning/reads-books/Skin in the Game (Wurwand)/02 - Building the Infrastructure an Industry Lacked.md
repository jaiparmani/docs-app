# 02 — Building the Infrastructure an Industry Lacked

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Dermalogica's known business model wasn't just "make skincare products" — it was education-first: building a professional training and credentialing pathway for skin therapists and estheticians alongside the product line itself, in an industry that didn't have much formal infrastructure of that kind before. The company grew large enough that Unilever acquired a majority stake in 2015, but the structural bet — product plus professional training network — is the more interesting founding decision than any single product launch.

## Why it matters
A lot of founder stories center on a product insight: someone notices an unmet need and builds a better version of an existing thing. This is a different kind of bet — recognizing that an entire industry lacked standardized training and credibility infrastructure, and deciding to build that infrastructure as the core of the business rather than as a marketing add-on to a product line. That's a business-model choice, not a product choice, and it's worth naming as the distinct thing it is rather than folding it into a generic "she built a skincare company" summary.

## Example from the book
Without claiming to know the specific scenes Wurwand uses to tell this part of the story, founder memoirs describing an infrastructure-building bet typically narrate the moment the founder recognized the gap — a training program that didn't exist, a certification nobody offered, a professional community with no formal home — and the early, unglamorous work of building that missing layer before the market rewarded it. Whatever form that narration takes in this specific text isn't something to summarize confidently from a distance.

## Practical application
In whatever field you operate in, ask whether the actual constraint on growth — yours or the field's — is a product gap or an infrastructure gap: missing training, missing credentialing, missing standards, missing shared language. Product gaps get noticed and filled quickly because they're visible. Infrastructure gaps are slower to notice and slower to fill, which is also why filling one can be more durable.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a piece of missing infrastructure in your field — a training path, a credential, a shared standard — that nobody has bothered to build because it isn't as visible as a product gap?
