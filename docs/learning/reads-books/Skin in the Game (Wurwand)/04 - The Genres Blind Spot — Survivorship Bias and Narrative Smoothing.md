# 04 — The Genre's Blind Spot: Survivorship Bias and Narrative Smoothing

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Founder memoirs, written by a founder after the company's success is already established, are survivorship-biased by construction. The founder is narrating from the vantage point of an outcome that worked, and the counterfactual — the version of the story where the same bets didn't pay off — never gets written, because the founder who made those bets and lost doesn't get a book deal. This applies to any book in the genre, this one included, independent of whatever specific claims it makes.

## Why it matters
Lessons framed as "what worked" are structurally hard to distinguish from "what happened to precede an outcome that was also shaped by market timing, luck, and factors the founder didn't control." A founder narrating in hindsight tends to describe choices as more deliberate and their reasoning as more prescient than they likely felt in the moment — not necessarily out of dishonesty, but because memory itself reorganizes around the outcome that actually happened. A reader who takes every "this is why it worked" claim at face value is absorbing a story that's been retrospectively smoothed.

## Example from the book
Rather than claim to know how smoothed or how candid this specific book is — which isn't knowable without close reading — it's more honest to name the genre pattern: founder memoirs vary widely in how much they acknowledge luck and contingency versus how much they attribute outcomes to the founder's judgment and character. *Shoe Dog* is notably candid about naming its own luck; many other books in the genre lean harder into individual-empowerment framing. This book's subtitle — "Everything You Need Is Already Inside You" — leans toward the empowerment end of that spectrum by its framing alone, which is a reasonable thing to flag going in, independent of the book's actual content.

## Practical application
As a reading habit for any founder memoir: for each lesson presented as "why this worked," ask what the same decision would sound like narrated by a founder who made an identical choice and failed. If the lesson only sounds wise in the version where it worked, treat it as a story about this particular outcome rather than a transferable principle.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Of the "lessons" a successful founder attributes to their own judgment, how would you tell the difference between a real insight and a decision that simply happened to land well?
