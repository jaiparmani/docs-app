# Glucose Revolution

<small>3 min read</small>

*Jessie Inchauspé*

Inchauspé's claim is narrower than the marketing around it suggests, and narrower is actually the book's strength: it isn't primarily about average blood sugar or diabetes risk, it's about the shape of the glucose curve after you eat. A spike — a fast, high rise followed by a fast crash — is, in her argument, a distinct physiological event from the same total glucose delivered gradually, and it's the spike specifically that she ties to energy crashes, cravings, skin inflammation, and downstream hormonal effects, in people who are metabolically unremarkable by any standard blood panel. The mechanism she leans on is real and well established: a sharp glucose spike triggers a correspondingly sharp insulin response, which frequently overshoots and drives blood sugar below baseline, and that trough is what registers subjectively as fatigue and hunger about ninety minutes after eating.

## The hacks are more defensible than they sound

The book's most quotable content — eat vegetables before carbs, put vinegar in water before a meal, savory breakfast over sweet, clothe your carbs in fat or fiber rather than eating them alone — reads like a listicle, and it's fair to be skeptical of anything this packageable. But the food-order effect specifically has decent continuous-glucose-monitor evidence behind it: sequencing fiber and protein ahead of refined carbohydrate measurably blunts the subsequent spike, independent of total calories or macros eaten. That's a real, mechanistically sensible finding — fiber slows gastric emptying and forms a physical barrier to glucose absorption — not a wellness-influencer invention. The vinegar claim is thinner but not fabricated; acetic acid's effect on insulin sensitivity has support in smaller trials, even if Inchauspé states it with more confidence than the effect size probably earns.

Where the book oversells is in the leap from "spikes are measurably worse than flat curves in continuous-glucose-monitor data" to "spikes are the hidden cause of" an increasingly long list of complaints — acne, PCOS symptoms, mood, aging skin. Some of these links have real biochemical plausibility (glycation's role in skin aging is genuine chemistry), but the book compresses correlational and mechanistic evidence of very different strengths into a single confident register, so a reader can't easily tell which claims are load-bearing science and which are extrapolation dressed the same way.

## What the format hides

This is popular science built around a personal N-of-1 device — a continuous glucose monitor Inchauspé wore herself — generalized to a universal audience, and that generalization is where the book is most vulnerable. Individual glucose responses to identical meals vary substantially between people (this is itself a well-replicated finding, ironically one that undercuts a one-size-fits-all "eat in this order" prescription). The book gestures at bio-individuality without really grappling with what it means for the specificity of its own advice: the general shape of the recommendations is probably fine for most people, but the confident specificity — this many minutes, this order, this much vinegar — outruns what individual variation would actually predict works for everyone equally.

## What's actually worth keeping

The reframe from "count calories/carbs" to "manage the curve" is a genuinely useful mental model, and it's compatible with existing nutrition science rather than opposed to it — it's a different lens on the same food, not a diet requiring elimination of any food group, which is a real point in its favor next to most books in this genre. The most durable, low-cost takeaways are the food-order effect and pairing carbohydrate with fiber, protein, or fat rather than eating it alone; both are cheap to test on yourself and have real physiological grounding. The parts to hold more loosely are the specific numbered hacks and the broader causal story connecting spikes to skin and hormonal symptoms, where the book's confidence exceeds the evidence it cites for its own claims.
