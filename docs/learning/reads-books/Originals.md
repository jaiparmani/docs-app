# Originals

<small>3 min read</small>

*Adam Grant*

The people who change fields are not, on average, the fastest movers or the biggest risk-takers. Adam Grant's central claim is a correction to the startup-hero image of originality: the most prolific originals hedge. They keep a day job while the side venture gets going. They generate a large volume of mediocre ideas because volume, not some private quality filter, is what correlates with producing a few great ones. And they often procrastinate on purpose, because time spent not-executing is time spent incubating alternatives the first draft foreclosed. Originality, in Grant's telling, is less a personality trait and more a set of exploitable regularities in how good ideas actually get produced and selected.

## The evidence base and its shape

Grant's method throughout is the same: take a counterintuitive finding from organizational psychology, dress it in a vivid case (the Warby Parker founders keeping their old job offers lined up, the creators of Seinfeld nearly shelving the pilot), and generalize outward into a principle. It's an effective structure for readability and a slightly worrying one for rigor, because a well-chosen anecdote can make almost any thesis feel proven. The book is honest that its stories are illustrations rather than data points, but it leans on them harder than the caveat implies — the studies are real and mostly well-cited, but the connective tissue between "here is a correlational finding about risk tolerance in entrepreneurs" and "here is a story about a founder who felt anxious" is doing more narrative work than logical work.

That said, the core empirical claims hold up better than most pop-psychology imports. The finding that entrepreneurs who keep their day jobs are meaningfully less likely to fail is a real, replicated result, not a folk intuition dressed up in a study citation. The idea quantity argument — that Beethoven, Edison, and Picasso produced enormous quantities of forgettable work alongside their masterpieces, and that this ratio was not a personal failing but close to a law of creative output — is one of the more durable findings in creativity research, and Grant states it plainly rather than softening it into inspiration-poster language.

## Where the argument thins

The chapters on groupthink and dissent are the weakest structurally, not because the underlying research is bad but because Grant is reaching for a unifying "originals share these traits" narrative across domains — parenting, political dissent, whistleblowing, product design — that don't obviously share a generative mechanism. A parent raising a child who challenges authority and a product manager killing groupthink in a meeting are both examples of "surfacing dissent," but treating them as instances of the same underlying skill is more rhetorical convenience than demonstrated fact. The book is at its best when it stays close to organizational behavior, where Grant's own research lives, and thinner when it stretches the frame to cover everything adjacent to nonconformity.

There's also a selection problem the book never quite confronts: every original in these pages succeeded. Strategic procrastination, holding many bad ideas, waiting for the right moment — these are also exactly the behaviors of people who never ship anything and call it incubation. Grant gestures at this ("most originals fail more than they succeed") without giving the reader a way to distinguish productive hedging from plain avoidance in the moment it's happening, which is the only moment it actually matters.

## What's worth keeping

The most useful idea in the book is the reframe of originality as a numbers game rather than a talent — it argues, correctly, that the practical lever available to most people is producing more raw material, not waiting for better instincts. The second is the case against brainstorming-as-practiced: Grant marshals decent evidence that group brainstorming sessions, run the way most companies run them, suppress originality relative to people generating ideas independently and pooling them afterward — a finding worth acting on directly rather than filing away as trivia. The parts about "vuja de" and the specific taxonomy of risk-tolerance types are closer to filler; memorable phrasing standing in for an idea that doesn't need six pages once stated.
