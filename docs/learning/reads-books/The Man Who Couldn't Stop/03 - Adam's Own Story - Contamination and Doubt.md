# 03 — Adam's Own Story — Contamination and Doubt

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The personal spine of the book is Adam's own account of developing OCD centered on HIV contamination as a young man. It began, as these fears often do, with something that had a grain of ordinary sense to it — a period of awareness about HIV transmission — and then, through the compulsion-relief loop, grew disconnected from anything resembling proportionate risk. He describes checking and re-checking for cuts or breaks in his skin, avoiding anything that could conceivably carry a trace of infection, and washing in response to contact that posed no realistic danger at all, all while knowing, on some level, that the fear had stopped tracking reality. That gap — being able to recognize a fear as excessive while still being compelled to act on it — is one of the book's most important and most specific observations about how OCD actually feels from the inside.

## Why it matters
Adam's account matters less as a story of dramatic symptoms and more as a demonstration of something clinicians describe but rarely convey experientially: insight without control. Popular imagination often assumes that a person struggling with an irrational fear simply doesn't realize it's irrational — that awareness would fix it. Adam's memoir sections repeatedly show the opposite: he knew, in the moment, that another round of washing wasn't going to make him safer, and did it anyway, because the anxiety was real and immediate even though the reasoning behind it wasn't. That gap between knowing and doing is what makes OCD resistant to being reasoned out of, and it's a large part of why the disorder is properly understood as a specific neurological and behavioral pattern rather than a simple failure of logic or willpower.

## Example from the book
Adam describes the specific texture of his contamination fear — the hyper-attention to any possible break in his skin, the way an entirely ordinary interaction could get reinterpreted as a transmission risk, and the washing rituals that followed. He writes about the isolating effect of this: because the fear centered on something as loaded and stigmatized as HIV in that era, it came with a layer of shame on top of the anxiety itself, making it harder to talk about than a more mundane compulsion like checking a lock. He also traces how the fear didn't stay static — it adapted and found new objects to attach to once old triggers had been exhausted, a pattern he connects back to the general mechanism of the compulsion-relief loop.

## Practical application
Adam's own account works best as a corrective to a common misconception: that someone with a compulsive fear "just needs to know" the fear is unfounded. If you know someone dealing with something like this, his story is a case for not leading with reassurance or logical argument — "you can't possibly have been infected that way" — since that's usually information the person already has and it doesn't touch the loop that's actually driving the behavior. It's a case for patience and for taking the felt reality of the anxiety seriously, even while the specific fear is not evidence-based, and for encouraging professional support rather than trying to talk someone out of a compulsion.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Adam describes knowing his fear was disproportionate while still being compelled to act on it. Have you ever recognized a worry as excessive in the moment and found that recognizing it changed nothing about what you did next?
