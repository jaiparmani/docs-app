# 02 — The Near-Universality of Intrusive Thoughts

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Adam cites research on non-clinical populations — people with no OCD diagnosis and no particular history of anxiety — showing that the overwhelming majority report having had intrusive thoughts of the exact flavor associated with OCD: sudden, unwanted images of harming someone they love, of shouting something obscene in a quiet room, of being contaminated, of a taboo or violent act. These thoughts arrive uninvited, often make no sense in context, and are reported by people who have never acted on them and have no intention of doing so. What separates a passing intrusive thought from the clinical disorder isn't the thought's existence or even its content — it's what the mind does with it next: whether the thought is shrugged off as noise, or seized on as meaningful and answered with a ritual.

## Why it matters
This is the finding that gives the book's title claim — that OCD sits on a spectrum everyone touches — its actual evidentiary weight, rather than leaving it as a rhetorical flourish. It also does real destigmatizing work: people who don't have OCD but who've had a single disturbing intrusive thought sometimes conclude something is wrong with them for having thought it at all, when the data says the thought itself is close to universal. Conversely, for people who do have OCD, it relocates the disorder away from the content of the thought — which sufferers are often ashamed of, precisely because it's violent or taboo — and onto the loop response, which is a more tractable and less morally loaded place to locate the problem.

## Example from the book
Adam discusses survey research in which large majorities of ordinary respondents — with no OCD diagnosis — endorse having had intrusive thoughts of just this kind, often thoughts of harming a child or family member, of contamination, or of a sudden violent or sexual image with no connection to what they were doing at the time. He connects this to his own case: what made his contamination fears different from a stranger's passing hand-washing thought wasn't the content of the thought but the elaborate compulsive apparatus he built around it, and the degree to which it started to govern his day. The book uses this comparison specifically to push back against the idea that the presence of a disturbing thought is itself diagnostic of anything.

## Practical application
The most responsible use of this idea is destigmatizing rather than diagnostic — it's a reason not to panic at an odd or disturbing thought that crosses your mind, and not to read shame into it. It is not license to self-diagnose OCD from having intrusive thoughts, since, per the finding itself, having them is the normal case rather than the exception. The distinguishing feature that actually matters clinically — a compulsive ritual built around the thought, and functional impairment that follows from it — is a different and much narrower thing than the thought's mere occurrence. If a thought like this is arriving frequently and is genuinely distressing, or has led to ritualized responses that are taking up real time or causing real distress, that's worth raising with a clinician rather than assessing from a book summary.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
