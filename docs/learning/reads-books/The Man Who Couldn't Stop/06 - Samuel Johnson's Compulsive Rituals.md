# 06 — Samuel Johnson's Compulsive Rituals

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Adam uses Samuel Johnson — the eighteenth-century writer and lexicographer, extensively documented by his contemporary biographer James Boswell — as a historical case study of behavior that reads, in retrospect, as recognizably compulsive: elaborate touching and counting rituals, a need to cross thresholds in particular ways, retracing steps if a routine was interrupted or performed incorrectly. Boswell's contemporaneous descriptions of these habits, written decades before anyone had a clinical vocabulary for them, give Adam a rare thing in the history of psychiatry — a well-documented, real-time account of compulsive behavior from an era that had no framework for understanding it as anything other than eccentricity.

## Why it matters
Johnson's case does two things for the book's argument. First, it's evidence that whatever is producing OCD's characteristic compulsions isn't a modern phenomenon or an artifact of contemporary life — the loop was operating in exactly recognizable form centuries before it had a name. Second, and more pointedly, it shows how a compulsive pattern gets absorbed into a person's public identity as mere personal quirk when there's no clinical category available to name it as something else. Johnson was regarded, in his own time, as brilliant and eccentric — the rituals were treated as color, not symptom. Adam's point isn't to retroactively diagnose Johnson with certainty, which the book is careful not to overclaim, but to use a well-documented historical figure to make the loop mechanism feel concrete and to show how thoroughly a culture's available vocabulary shapes what gets treated as illness versus what gets treated as personality.

## Example from the book
The specific behaviors Adam draws from Boswell's accounts include Johnson's rituals around doorways and thresholds — needing to pass through in a particular manner, sometimes retracing his steps to redo it correctly — along with touching and counting behaviors performed with a consistency that Boswell, writing as an admiring friend rather than a clinician, simply recorded as one of Johnson's many idiosyncrasies. Adam reads these accounts through the lens of the compulsion-relief loop established earlier in the book, noting how closely the pattern — a felt need to perform an action in exactly the right way, and apparent distress or a need to repeat it if that didn't happen — maps onto compulsions described by OCD patients today.

## Practical application
Johnson's case is a useful historical anchor for the idea that compulsive behavior has always existed and has usually been absorbed into "that's just how they are" rather than recognized as a treatable pattern. It's worth holding onto as a check against the assumption that psychiatric categories are simply discovering something that wasn't there before versus giving a name and a treatment pathway to something that was always present but previously invisible as illness. It's also a caution about retrospective diagnosis generally — the book uses Johnson to illustrate a mechanism, not to assert a definitive clinical verdict on a person who can't be assessed directly.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Johnson's contemporaries saw his rituals as personal eccentricity rather than as a pattern that might have caused him real distress. What compulsive-looking behaviors do we currently wave off as "just how someone is" that might look different with a different vocabulary?
