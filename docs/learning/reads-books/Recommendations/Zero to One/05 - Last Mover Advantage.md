# 05 — Last Mover Advantage

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Standard startup wisdom prizes first mover advantage — get there before anyone else and claim the ground. Thiel argues this is often backward, or at least badly incomplete: being first only matters if that early lead can be converted into something durable. What actually determines long-run success, in his framing, is being the *last* mover — the company that makes the last major development in a given market and then gets to enjoy an extended run of monopoly-like profits afterward, because no one meaningfully challenges the position again. Being first without building anything defensible just means being first to get overtaken.

## Why it matters
Chasing speed into a market can mean winning a race that turns out to matter less than the war — an early entrant proves the concept, takes on the initial risk and cost of educating the market, and then gets displaced by a later, better-resourced, more durable competitor who benefits from everything the first mover already established. The founders who end up with lasting monopoly positions are usually the ones who built something structurally hard to dislodge — proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale, brand — rather than the ones who simply arrived earliest.

## Example from the book
Thiel discusses Amazon, a company he was an early outside investor in, as an example of this patience. Amazon was willing to run at thin or negative margins for years, reinvesting continuously in infrastructure, logistics, and customer trust rather than optimizing for near-term profitability — building the kind of durable operational and scale advantage that's difficult for a faster-moving but shallower competitor to replicate, even one that got to a similar idea earlier. The payoff wasn't being first to sell books online; it was building a position so entrenched that being first stopped mattering to anyone still trying to catch up.

## Practical application
Instead of asking "how do I get to market before anyone else," ask "once I've built this, what will make it hard for someone better-resourced to simply copy and out-execute me a year or two later?" If the honest answer is nothing — no network effect, no proprietary advantage, no structural edge — speed alone won't protect the business, and the real strategic work is figuring out what would make the position defensible once it exists, not just how to reach it first. It's worth revisiting that answer periodically rather than settling it once, since a defensibility story that held up at launch can quietly stop being true as a market matures and competitors catch up on the dimensions that used to be hard to copy.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If a well-funded, capable competitor saw exactly what you're building today and started building the same thing tomorrow, what would stop them from catching up to you within a year or two — and if the answer is "nothing yet," what would you need to build first?
