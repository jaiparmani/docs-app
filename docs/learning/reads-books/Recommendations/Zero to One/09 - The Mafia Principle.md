# 09 — The Mafia Principle

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
This chapter takes its title from the "PayPal Mafia," the nickname later given to the company's early alumni network, many of whom went on to found or lead a striking number of major companies afterward. Thiel's argument is that this outsized alumni success wasn't random — it came from a deliberate hiring and culture philosophy at PayPal: hire people who would genuinely enjoy working closely together day to day, not just people with the strongest individual resumes; favor an intense, in-person, shared working environment over a loose, distributed, contractor-style arrangement; and select for real belief in the mission over people optimizing purely for compensation. He draws a sharp line between an authentic culture — a group of people who actually like, trust, and push each other, working toward a shared and specific mission — and a cosmetic "perks culture" of free food and ping-pong tables, which he argues is often a signal of the *absence* of real cohesion rather than evidence of it: perks are what you offer people who don't otherwise want to be there.

## Why it matters
The specific claim is counterintuitive to a lot of conventional hiring advice, which optimizes almost entirely for individual credentials and skill. Thiel's version adds a second, coequal filter: would the people already on the team actually want to work closely with this specific person, day after day, under pressure. A brilliant individual hire who doesn't fit how the existing team actually works together can cost a young company more than a slightly less credentialed hire who does — because early-stage execution depends heavily on a small group's ability to move fast and trust each other's judgment without friction.

## Example from the book
The book credits the tight, contentious, all-in character of the early PayPal team — engineers and founders working in close physical proximity through the company's most difficult early years — as the actual mechanism behind why so many of its alumni went on to found or lead major companies together or independently afterward. The trust and shared working history built inside that original team became the informal network underneath a large share of subsequent Silicon Valley company formation, which Thiel treats as evidence that the culture itself, not just the individuals' raw talent, was the asset.

## Practical application
When hiring for an early team, weight "would the people already here want to work with this person specifically, closely, under pressure" as heavily as you weight raw skill or credentials. And be suspicious of perks as a culture strategy in their own right — if you find yourself reaching for free snacks or office amenities to make a role more appealing, ask honestly whether that's compensating for a mission or working relationships that aren't compelling enough to want to show up for otherwise.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you removed every perk and every dollar of above-market compensation from your current team or company, who would still genuinely want to be there for the mission and the people — and is that group large enough to actually build what you're trying to build?
