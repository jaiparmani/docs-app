# 08 — Founding Teams and the Question of Equity

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Thiel treats a company's earliest structural choices — who founds it, how ownership is split, how control is organized — as decisions that are almost impossible to correct once made; in his words, a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed later. He separates out three distinct things people can hold in a company that are often conflated: ownership (equity — who has a legal claim on the value created), possession (who actually runs day-to-day operations), and formal control (the board, which has legal authority over the company's major decisions). He argues founders should think about choosing a co-founder with something like the seriousness of choosing a spouse, since misalignment between founders on vision or values is one of the most common causes of failure — more common, in his account, than being beaten by outside competition. On equity specifically, he argues a CEO's ownership stake needs to sit in a particular band: high enough that the CEO is genuinely committed and incentivized, not so high that everyone else on the team feels replaceable or undervalued by comparison.

## Why it matters
Founding conflicts over direction, control, or who's "really" in charge tend to surface only once the company is already under real pressure — a funding crunch, a strategic disagreement, a co-founder wanting to leave — well after the legal and social structure is already locked in and hard to renegotiate cleanly. Thiel's point is that the design work has to happen before that pressure ever arrives, while it's still just an uncomfortable conversation rather than an existential one.

## Example from the book
Thiel uses the phrase "founding matrimony" directly, framing the choice of a co-founder as comparable in seriousness to choosing a life partner, and noting that founder breakups — which he says happen constantly in practice even though they're rarely discussed publicly — can be as damaging to a company as a divorce is to a family. He's also specific about board size, recommending a small board (he suggests three people is often ideal for an early-stage company) on the logic that a larger board is slower to reach agreement and harder to align quickly, which is exactly when a company can least afford delay.

## Practical application
Before starting or joining a founding team, have the explicit and uncomfortable conversations — equity split, vesting schedules, who has final say on what, what happens if someone wants to leave — as early as possible, not after the company already has momentum and the conversation feels riskier to raise. Treat unresolved disagreement on these specific questions as a serious warning sign in its own right, not something growth or good intentions will smooth over later.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you're currently building something with co-founders or a founding team, could you and each of them independently write down the same answer to "who has final say if we disagree" and "what happens to equity if someone leaves in year one" — and if you're not sure, what's actually stopping that conversation from happening now rather than later?
