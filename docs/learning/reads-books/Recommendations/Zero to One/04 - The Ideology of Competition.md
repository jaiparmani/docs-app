# 04 — The Ideology of Competition

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Where the previous chunk argues monopoly beats competition on the merits, this one asks why competition is so hard to walk away from even when it's not serving anyone well. Thiel's answer is that competition isn't just an economic condition, it's an ideology absorbed early — through grades, class rank, sports, and admission to a narrow set of prestigious schools — that trains people to fight intensely over the same narrow, already-defined goals other people also want. He draws this partly from René Girard's theory of mimetic desire (Thiel studied under Girard at Stanford): people don't form their desires independently, they largely imitate what others around them already want, and that convergence on identical objects of desire is exactly what produces rivalry, often wildly disproportionate to what's actually at stake in the underlying prize.

## Why it matters
Founders who've come up through competitive, credential-driven institutions often default to entering crowded, already-validated markets or career paths — not because those are the best opportunities, but because they're legible, socially confirmed as worth wanting, and other capable people are already fighting for them. That instinct is close to the opposite of the 0 to 1 move: it treats the presence of intense competition as a signal a goal is worth pursuing, rather than as a warning that the value has probably already been competed away or is about to be.

## Example from the book
Thiel tells this partly through his own early career: he competed intensely to get into an elite law school and then to clerk for a top judge, treating each step as confirmation he was on the right track because so many other talented people wanted the same thing. When he narrowly missed the final step of that ladder — a Supreme Court clerkship — he describes realizing, looking at the people who had made it through the door he hadn't, that they didn't seem meaningfully happier than he was outside it. The competition itself, not the destination, had become the actual point of the pursuit, and winning it would not have delivered what its intensity had implied it would.

## Practical application
When you notice yourself, or your company, pulled strongly toward a specific goal, separate two possible reasons: you've independently reasoned that it's genuinely valuable, or it's what everyone in your reference group is already fighting over and its apparent value is mostly borrowed from their wanting it too. Treat the second case as a caution flag rather than validation — the presence of intense competition for something is evidence about how many people want it, not evidence about how much value is actually left to capture there.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a goal you're currently pursuing hard. If you strip away the fact that capable people around you also want it, does it still look like the best use of your time — or is a meaningful part of its pull just that others are competing for it too?
