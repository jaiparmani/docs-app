# 01 — The Contrarian Question

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Thiel opens the book with the question he says he used to ask every candidate in job interviews: "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" He treats this as the single filter underneath everything else in the book — genuine, 0 to 1 progress can only happen in the space where the crowd is wrong, because if a promising idea is already something everyone agrees on, it's already being pursued, already priced in, and no longer a source of outsized advantage. Crucially, he distinguishes this from generic contrarianism (disagreeing with the majority just to be different). A good answer has a specific structure: "most people believe X, but the truth is the opposite of X" — a positive, defensible claim, not a reflexive one.

## Why it matters
Most business advice implicitly rewards consensus: validate your idea, get feedback, make sure people like it. Thiel's argument flips that instinct for the specific case of building something genuinely new. If a plan sounds obviously good to everyone who hears it, that's mild evidence against it, not for it — competitors already see the same opportunity and are likely already moving toward it. The companies capable of real 0 to 1 progress are usually the ones acting on a belief that most smart, informed people would currently reject, and that turns out to be correct. This reframes "getting validation" from an unambiguous good into something to be suspicious of, at least for the central thesis of a new venture.

## Example from the book
Thiel offers his own answer as the model case: he argues that most people assume the future of the world will be defined by globalization — countries converging on a shared, roughly Western pattern of development, catching up via the same known playbook — while his actual belief is that technology, not globalization, is what will determine the future, and that assuming convergence without new technology is likely to produce a much darker outcome than people expect (existing resources spread across a much larger population without corresponding gains in how much can be produced from them). He uses this as a worked example of the "X, but actually not-X" structure: the claim isn't just an alternative opinion, it's a specific, defensible reversal of a nearly universal assumption.

## Practical application
Try writing your own answer in the same "most people believe X, but actually not-X" structure, applied to your field, your company, or a decision you're weighing. Then stress-test it against a smart person who'd disagree — if you can't articulate a specific reason the consensus view is wrong, beyond "I have a hunch," you likely have a preference, not a contrarian truth. The test isn't whether the belief is unpopular; it's whether you can defend it on the merits against someone who has genuinely thought it through the other way.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> What do you believe about your own field or industry that most smart, well-informed people you respect would currently disagree with — and if you had to defend it to one of them directly, could you, or would it fall apart on contact?
