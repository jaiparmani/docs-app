# 10 — Sales Is Hidden

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Thiel's argument here is that distribution — sales, marketing, and the mechanics of how a product actually reaches and gets adopted by customers — matters just as much as the product itself, and that technical founders in particular systematically underrate it. Part of why is structural: effective selling is deliberately designed to be invisible. The best salespeople and the best-built distribution systems don't look like "selling" from the outside at all, which makes it easy for people who've never worked in sales to assume it isn't really happening, or isn't really necessary if the product is good enough. He lays out that the right distribution approach depends heavily on price point: very expensive, rare deals need complex, high-touch enterprise sales; mid-sized deals need a dedicated sales team; consumer-priced products rely on marketing and advertising; and free or near-free products need engineered virality. Using the wrong one of these for your actual price point, he argues, is a common and often fatal startup mistake.

## Why it matters
This directly counters the instinct, common among engineering-heavy founding teams, that a great enough product sells itself and distribution is secondary work to figure out later. Thiel states the trade-off plainly: a company with a mediocre product and excellent distribution can beat a company with a superior product and poor distribution, because if you've invented something new but haven't also invented an effective way to actually sell it, you have a bad business — regardless of how good the underlying product is. Distribution isn't a downstream afterthought to product; it's a design problem that has to be solved with the same seriousness as the product itself, from early on.

## Example from the book
Thiel draws on his own experience at PayPal building the company's viral referral mechanism — paying users a small cash incentive to invite others onto the platform — at a moment when the underlying product, while genuinely useful, wasn't going to spread on its own fast enough to matter. That viral loop wasn't a side effect of a good product; it was deliberately engineered as the distribution channel appropriate to a low-price, consumer-facing product, on the logic that virality has to be built into a product's design rather than hoped for after the fact.

## Practical application
For whatever you're building, identify explicitly which of the four distribution modes — complex sales, dedicated sales team, marketing and advertising, or engineered virality — actually fits its price point and complexity, rather than defaulting to whichever one feels most comfortable or most visible to you personally. A technical founder who's never sold anything will often gravitate toward marketing or virality by default even when the product's price point actually requires direct, high-touch sales — check that the mode you've chosen matches the economics, not just your own comfort zone.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Looking at what you're currently building or selling, does its actual price point and complexity match the distribution approach you've been relying on — or have you been defaulting to whichever mode feels most natural to you, regardless of whether it's the one the economics actually call for?
