# 06 — The Power Law of Venture Capital

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Venture returns, Thiel argues, don't follow anything like a normal, bell-curve distribution where most investments cluster around an average outcome. They follow a power law: a small handful of companies in any given fund generate returns so large they dwarf the combined returns of every other investment in the portfolio, while most companies in most funds end up worth close to nothing. The practical consequence is unusual: because the distribution is this skewed, a fund's single best investment needs to be capable, on its own, of returning the entire fund — which means the entire logic of how to select investments has to bend around finding and backing that rare outlier, not around building a smooth, diversified basket of reasonably safe bets.

## Why it matters
This reframes both how investors should behave and, more importantly for founders, what kind of company is actually worth building or backing. A merely solid, respectable business — profitable, growing steadily, unlikely to fail but also unlikely to become huge — is nearly invisible to an investor working under power-law logic, because it can never be the outlier that makes the fund's numbers work, no matter how many of them a portfolio holds. It also means the ordinary investing instinct to diversify broadly across many small, safer bets is close to exactly wrong at the earliest stage of venture investing, where returns are concentrated in a tiny number of category-defining winners rather than spread evenly across the portfolio.

## Example from the book
Thiel discusses his own firm, Founders Fund, and its early investment in Facebook, noting that this single investment alone generated a return larger than the combined returns of every other company in that fund's portfolio — a concrete illustration of the power law in action rather than an even, diversified spread of moderate outcomes across many companies.

## Practical application
When evaluating a bet — an investment, a project, a career move — resist the instinct to ask only "is this a safe, reasonably positive bet." Ask instead whether it could plausibly become the one outcome that dwarfs everything else you could have done with the same time or capital. If the honest answer is that it's a solid, capped-upside bet at best, it may still be worth doing, but it doesn't deserve the same all-in intensity as something with a real, if unlikely, path to being the outlier. This is also a useful check on how you're spending attention day to day, not just capital — power-law thinking applies just as much to which single project inside a busy portfolio of commitments is actually worth your best hours.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Among the handful of bets you're currently making — with your time, your money, or your career — which one, if any, actually has a plausible path to being the outlier that dwarfs all the others, and are you devoting your attention in proportion to that, or spreading it evenly across bets that were never going to be it?
