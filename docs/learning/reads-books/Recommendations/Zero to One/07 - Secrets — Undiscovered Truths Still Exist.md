# 07 — Secrets — Undiscovered Truths Still Exist

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Thiel argues that every great company is built on a secret: an important truth that few people know or believe, but that's nonetheless true and can be found through real effort. He draws a specific line between secrets (true things nobody has discovered yet, but which are discoverable) and mysteries (things that may be genuinely unknowable, where further effort doesn't help). His diagnosis of why so few people go looking for secrets anymore is cultural: a mix of incrementalism (big problems get broken into narrow specialized slices, so no one individual believes they could personally uncover something large), risk aversion, comfortable complacency, and a kind of globalized "flatness" — the sense that because information now travels everywhere instantly, anything worth discovering has probably already been discovered by someone, somewhere. That skepticism becomes self-fulfilling: if you don't believe secrets exist, you stop looking for them.

## Why it matters
Every one of the definite-optimist companies described in chunk 02 is, in this framing, built on some version of a secret its founders found and almost everyone else missed. If your working assumption is that everything valuable has already been found, the only rational move left is to iterate on known, already-validated approaches — 1 to n, not 0 to 1. Believing secrets still exist is less a factual claim and more a precondition for even starting to look for one.

## Example from the book
Thiel frames PayPal's own founding insight as this kind of secret: a belief, not obvious to most other financial-technology people at the time, that ordinary people wanted a fast, trusted way to move money electronically across platforms and borders, outside the slow rails banks and credit card networks then offered. He also splits the hunt for secrets into two distinct categories worth searching separately: secrets about nature (undiscovered scientific or engineering truths still waiting to be found) and secrets about people (things individuals don't know about themselves, or know but won't say out loud) — a category he argues is just as fertile a source of new businesses as technical discovery, and much less crowded, because it requires a different kind of looking.

## Practical application
Pose both questions deliberately rather than waiting for an idea to arrive: "what valuable company or product is nobody building right now" (a secret about nature), and "what do people not know about themselves, or know but won't admit" (a secret about people). Both are productive precisely because most people around you have already concluded there's nothing left to find in either direction, which is exactly why they haven't looked. Treat a dismissive "that's already been tried" or "someone would have done it by now" as a prompt to check the claim yourself rather than as a reason to stop.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of an assumption in your field that "everyone knows" is settled. Have you actually tested whether it's true, or have you just accepted that it must have already been checked by someone else, somewhere, at some point?
