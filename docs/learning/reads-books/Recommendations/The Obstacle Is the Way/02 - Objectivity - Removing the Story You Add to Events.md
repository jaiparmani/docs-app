# 02 — Objectivity — Removing the Story You Add to Events

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The Perception discipline rests on an old Stoic claim, inherited from Epictetus and repeated throughout *Meditations*: events themselves are neutral, and what actually disturbs you is the judgment you layer on top of them — the story about what the event means, whether it's fair, whether it should be happening to you. Holiday's version of this is practical rather than purely philosophical: he treats "the event" and "your narrative about the event" as two separate things you can pry apart, and argues that most of what feels unbearable about a setback is the second one, not the first. Objectivity, in this framing, isn't emotional suppression — it's the discipline of noticing where the raw fact ends and your added commentary begins, and choosing not to automatically believe the commentary.

## Why it matters
This matters because the added story is usually what produces bad decisions, not the event itself. A canceled contract is a fact you can work with; "this proves I'm not cut out for this" is a story that, once believed, changes how you act on the fact. Panic, self-pity, and catastrophizing aren't reactions to obstacles — they're reactions to the narrative version of obstacles, and they actively degrade your ability to do anything useful about the underlying fact. Holiday's point is that this is a skill, not a personality trait: you can train yourself to catch the narrative layer before it hardens into your operating reality.

## Example from the book
Holiday opens this part of the book with a young John D. Rockefeller navigating the Panic of 1857, one of the first major financial crises he witnessed as a bookkeeper at the start of his career. While businesses around him collapsed and people around him panicked, Rockefeller was known for an almost eerie composure — methodical, unemotional, and focused on the facts of the situation rather than the fear circulating around it. That same cold-eyed calm followed him through the later financial panics of his career: while others sold in fear, he was frequently the one positioned to buy, precisely because he was responding to what was actually happening rather than to the emotional weather everyone else was reacting to. Holiday uses Rockefeller not as a portrait of someone who didn't feel pressure, but as a case study in someone who consistently declined to let the added narrative — this is a catastrophe, this is the end — dictate his actions.

## Practical application
Next time something goes wrong, write two separate lines. First, the event, stated as plainly and factually as you can manage — no adjectives, no interpretation, just what happened. Second, the story you've been telling yourself about it — the interpretation, the implication, the verdict you've attached. Then ask, specifically, whether that second line is something you know to be true or something you're assuming under pressure. Often the plain first line is manageable; it's the second line doing all the damage.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Take a recent setback that felt bigger than it probably was. If you strip out every piece of interpretation and just state what factually happened, how does the size of the actual problem compare to how it felt at the time?
