# 06 — Turning the Disaster Into Fuel

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The most demanding form of the Action discipline is using catastrophe itself as raw material — not waiting for a disaster to pass before acting, but treating the wreckage as the actual starting point for the next move. Holiday distinguishes this from simple resilience or "bouncing back." The claim is stronger: a sufficiently disciplined response doesn't just survive a disaster, it uses the disaster's specific consequences — the forced clean slate, the sudden absence of old constraints — as things it couldn't have gotten any other way.

## Example from the book
Holiday's example is Thomas Edison's West Orange laboratory fire in December 1914. A large part of Edison's factory complex burned down in a fire that destroyed years of equipment, records, and materials — by any reasonable measure, a devastating loss late in his career. Accounts of Edison that night describe him watching the fire with a striking lack of despair, reportedly telling one of his sons to go find his mother because she'd never see a fire like it again. In the aftermath, rather than treating the loss purely as a setback to recover from, Edison and his team began rebuilding almost immediately, and much of the operation was up and running again within weeks — in some respects reorganized and more efficient than what the fire had destroyed. The disaster didn't just fail to stop him; the forced clearing of the old setup became part of how the new one got built.

## Why it matters
Most people's default relationship to catastrophe is to treat it purely as loss — something to grieve, recover from, and eventually get back to normal after. Holiday's point is that "back to normal" is often the wrong target, because the disaster has already changed what's possible, for better as well as worse, and there's frequently real material in that change if you're looking for it instead of only for what was lost. This isn't a claim that disasters are secretly good, or that grief is inappropriate — it's a narrower, more practical claim: once the loss has happened, the question of what to build with what's left is still open, and answering it well requires actually looking at the new situation rather than only measuring it against the old one.

## Practical application
After something goes badly wrong in a way you can't undo, once the immediate response is handled, ask a specific question: what does this clear away that I couldn't or wouldn't have cleared away myself? Old commitments, obsolete methods, sunk costs you'd been reluctant to abandon — a disaster sometimes removes these involuntarily. Look for the version of "starting over" that's actually available now, rather than only the version of "getting back to where I was."

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a genuine setback or loss you've experienced. Looking back, was there anything it cleared away — an old constraint, a sunk cost, an outdated plan — that you might not have let go of on your own?
