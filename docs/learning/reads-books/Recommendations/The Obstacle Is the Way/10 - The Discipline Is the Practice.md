# 10 — The Discipline Is the Practice

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The book's closing move is to fold Perception, Action, and Will back into a single loop rather than leave them as three sequential stages you complete once. Holiday's point, by the end, is that the three disciplines aren't chapters of a process you finish and move past — they're a cycle you re-enter every time a new obstacle shows up, for the rest of your life, without ever graduating out of needing to run it. Seeing clearly doesn't get easier permanently just because you saw one thing clearly once; acting persistently doesn't become automatic just because you persisted through one failure. Each new obstacle asks the same three questions again, from the start.

## Why it matters
This matters because it corrects a natural misreading of the whole book: that these are techniques you apply to get past a specific obstacle, after which you're done. Treated that way, the framework becomes something you reach for in a crisis and forget about otherwise. Holiday's actual claim is closer to a discipline in the literal sense — like physical training, it only works as an ongoing practice, not a one-time intervention. The people in the book's case studies weren't cured of needing this after their signature obstacle; they kept running the same loop on the next one, and the one after that.

## Example from the book
Look back across the case studies and the pattern isn't a single moment each — it's a repetition. Rockefeller's composure wasn't limited to the Panic of 1857; it reappeared across every subsequent panic of his long career, because staying objective was something he kept doing, not something he did once and retained automatically. Grant's persistence didn't end at Vicksburg; the same doggedness that generated another attempt after a failed canal or a failed bayou expedition is what carried him through the rest of the war. Edison didn't rebuild only after the 1914 fire; treating setbacks as material to build with was a stance he brought to obstacle after obstacle across a long career of failed experiments and failed ventures before any of his successes. None of them "solved" Perception, Action, or Will once. They kept practicing all three, obstacle after obstacle.

## Practical application
Instead of treating this framework as something you reach for during your next crisis, build it into a standing habit: when any obstacle appears, however minor, briefly run the same three questions — am I seeing this clearly, what's the next actual action, and is there a piece of this I need to accept rather than fight — even on obstacles small enough that you'd normally skip straight to reacting. The point of practicing it on small obstacles is that the discipline is actually available, already warmed up, the next time a large one arrives.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Looking back at everything in this book, which of the three disciplines — Perception, Action, or Will — do you tend to skip past out of habit, and what would it cost you to actually run all three, every time, instead of jumping straight to the one you're most comfortable with?
