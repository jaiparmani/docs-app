# 04 — Persistence Through Failure

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
The Action discipline opens with the least glamorous idea in the book: persistence, applied plainly and repeatedly, even after failed attempts. Holiday's argument is that most obstacles aren't defeated by a single clever insight — they're worn down by a willingness to try, fail, adjust, and try again, treating each failed attempt as one data point rather than a verdict on the whole effort. This is deliberately unromantic. The discipline of Action, as Holiday frames it, has less to do with inspiration and more to do with a refusal to let one failed attempt become the final word.

## Why it matters
The instinct after a failed attempt is to treat it as evidence about the goal itself — "this isn't going to work" — rather than evidence about that one specific approach. That reframing matters because it's the difference between stopping and adjusting. If a failure proves the goal is impossible, quitting is rational. If a failure only proves that one method didn't work, quitting is premature — and the next attempt might succeed for reasons the failed one revealed. Holiday's point is that people default to the first interpretation far more often than the facts actually support, because it's less effortful than generating another attempt.

## Example from the book
Holiday uses Ulysses S. Grant as the central case study here. Before the Civil War, Grant's record was a string of civilian failures — a failed farm, a failed real estate venture, a failed retail store, a resignation from the army under a cloud involving drinking. By most external measures, he was a man who didn't succeed at things. During the war, that same doggedness showed up as his defining trait — most visibly in the Vicksburg campaign, where his first approaches failed outright: attempts to dig a canal to bypass the city's defenses, and a series of expeditions through the surrounding bayous, none of which worked. Rather than treating those failures as proof the campaign was unwinnable, Grant kept generating the next attempt until he found an approach — a bold march south of the city, cutting his own supply lines to do it — that worked. The failures weren't a detour from the eventual success; they were the process that produced it.

## Practical application
After a failed attempt at something that matters, before deciding whether to quit, write down specifically what the failure actually proved. Not "this doesn't work" in general, but what this particular approach ran into, and what that reveals about a different approach worth trying next. Treat the failed attempt as information about the terrain, not a verdict on whether the terrain is passable — and generate the next specific attempt before allowing yourself to decide the goal itself is the problem.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of something you gave up on after it failed once or twice. Looking back, did the failure actually prove the goal was unreachable — or did it just rule out the specific method you happened to try first?
