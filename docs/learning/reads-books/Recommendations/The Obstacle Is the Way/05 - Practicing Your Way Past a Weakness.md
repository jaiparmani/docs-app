# 05 — Practicing Your Way Past a Weakness

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
A second form the Action discipline takes is deliberate, unglamorous practice aimed directly at a real deficit — not working around a weakness, but working on it until it stops being one. Holiday's point is that many obstacles that feel like fixed traits (a bad voice, a bad temperament, a lack of natural talent) are actually just untrained, and that the Stoic response to a weakness is neither to deny it nor to be permanently limited by it, but to treat it as a specific, trainable problem.

## Example from the book
Holiday tells the classical story of Demosthenes, the ancient Athenian who became Greece's greatest orator despite starting with real physical disadvantages: a stammer and a weak voice that made public speaking, the one skill his ambitions required, genuinely difficult for him. Rather than accepting that as a fixed limitation, he built a program of practice around it — famously speaking with pebbles in his mouth to force clearer articulation, and going to the shore to practice projecting his voice over the sound of the waves, simulating the noise and pressure of an actual assembly. He's also said to have shaved half his head at one point specifically so he'd be too embarrassed to go out in public, forcing himself to stay inside and keep working. None of this was talent. It was a specific, repeated, deliberately uncomfortable practice regimen aimed at a specific, named weakness — and it's the reason his name survived as a byword for oratory rather than as a footnote about a man who never spoke in public.

## Why it matters
This matters because "weakness" often gets treated as a permanent identity rather than a current, addressable state — and that framing quietly excuses avoidance. If a weak voice is just who you are, there's nothing to do about it. If it's an untrained skill, there's a program for it, even an unpleasant one. Holiday's use of Demosthenes is a deliberate rebuttal to the idea that some obstacles are simply part of your nature and therefore not yours to work on. Most trainable weaknesses stay weaknesses specifically because they never get treated as trainable.

## Practical application
Name one specific weakness you've been treating as a fixed trait rather than an untrained skill — something you avoid rather than practice. Design one small, repeatable, slightly uncomfortable exercise aimed directly at it, the way Demosthenes used pebbles and ocean waves rather than vague resolutions to "get better at public speaking." The discomfort isn't incidental; it's usually a sign you've found the actual edge of the weakness rather than a comfortable adjacent skill. Make the exercise specific enough that you could describe it to someone else in one sentence — "I practice X by doing Y for Z minutes" — rather than a general intention, since general intentions are exactly what let a weakness stay untrained indefinitely without ever being tested against something concrete.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> What's a weakness you've quietly filed away as "just how I am" rather than as something untrained? What would the pebbles-in-the-mouth version of practicing it actually look like?
