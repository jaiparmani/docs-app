# 09 — The Honest Limits of Holiday's Stoicism

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
It's worth naming plainly, and separately from whether the book is useful: some readers and philosophers who study ancient Stoicism directly have pointed out that Holiday's popularization narrows the philosophy considerably. Ancient Stoicism, as practiced and written about by Epictetus, Seneca, and Marcus Aurelius himself, wasn't only a personal-resilience toolkit — it was also, centrally, an ethical and social philosophy, concerned with justice, duty to other people, and one's obligations to the wider community (the "cosmopolis," in Stoic terms). Marcus Aurelius's own *Meditations* returns repeatedly to the idea that a person is part of a larger whole and owes something to it — a bee that acts against the interest of the hive is acting against its own nature, in an image he uses more than once. Holiday's book, by contrast, is built almost entirely around the individual overcoming individual obstacles, with barely any of that communal or ethical dimension carried forward.

## Why it matters
This isn't a claim that Holiday misrepresents the philosophy outright — the Perception/Action/Will framework and the underlying dichotomy of control are genuinely drawn from the tradition. It's more that the selection is one-directional: the parts of Stoicism that translate cleanly into personal achievement and resilience are foregrounded, and the parts about duty, fairness, and obligation to others are largely left out. The result is a version of Stoicism that reads comfortably as a philosophy for winning — useful for an athlete, a founder, a soldier — but says comparatively little about how a Stoic is supposed to treat other people, or what they owe a community, which was never a minor thread in the original texts.

## Example from the book
This selection shows up structurally, in the case studies themselves. Every major example in the book — Rockefeller navigating financial panics, Grant persisting through military failure, Edison rebuilding after a fire, Earhart converting a demeaning offer into fame — is a story about an individual overcoming a personal or professional obstacle in pursuit of individual achievement. None of the book's central examples are built around communal duty, service, or the ethical obligations to others that occupy a real share of *Meditations* itself. That pattern, repeated across the whole book, is itself the clearest evidence of which half of the tradition made the cut.

## Practical application
Treat this as a reason to read *Meditations* in full rather than only through Holiday's lens, and to notice, while reading it, the passages that don't fit neatly into a personal-achievement framework — the ones about duty, community, and how to treat people who wrong you. Those passages are just as central to the original text as the ones about resilience, even though they're the ones a modern, individually-focused adaptation is most likely to leave on the cutting room floor.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If Stoicism as you've absorbed it so far is mostly about personal resilience and winning, what would it look like to apply the same discipline — clear perception, deliberate action, acceptance of what you can't control — to an obligation you have toward other people rather than to a personal goal?
