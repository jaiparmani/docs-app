# 10 — Rosa Parks, Strong and Weak Ties, and the Montgomery Bus Boycott

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Duhigg's final scale is social — habits that operate across an entire community or movement rather than an individual or an organization. His argument is that social movements spread through two distinct kinds of relationships, borrowing sociologist Mark Granovetter's theory of strong and weak ties. **Strong ties** — close friends, family, people in your immediate circle — get a movement started, because people act on the personal obligation of loyalty to someone they know well. But a movement that stays confined to strong ties stays small. What lets it grow past the founder's own network is **weak ties** — casual acquaintances, friends of friends — who don't act out of personal loyalty but out of a new social habit: peer pressure to conform to what people around them are now doing.

## Why it matters
This explains a pattern that pure ideology or pure charisma can't account for on their own: why some movements stay a small, dedicated group while others reach a tipping point and become self-sustaining across a much larger population that never had any personal connection to the original cause. The strong-tie phase depends on the leader's own relationships and doesn't scale past them. The weak-tie phase is what actually scales, because it stops depending on personal loyalty entirely and starts running on the same mechanism as any other social habit — people doing what the people around them are visibly doing, because that's what belonging now requires.

## Example from the book
Duhigg uses the Montgomery Bus Boycott of 1955 as his central case. When Rosa Parks was arrested for refusing to give up her bus seat, the boycott's fast initial mobilization relied heavily on her extensive personal network — she was active across multiple churches and civic organizations, well known and well liked across parts of Montgomery's Black community that didn't normally interact, which meant word spread through numerous overlapping circles of strong ties almost immediately. But sustaining a boycott for over a year, among tens of thousands of people, most of whom had never met Parks personally and had no direct tie to her at all, required something else: participation became a new social norm within the wider community, reinforced by the visible fact that neighbors and acquaintances were doing it too. Not walking to work or carpooling instead of riding the bus started to carry social cost among people connected only by weak ties — and that peer-pressure dynamic, not personal loyalty to Parks, is what Duhigg credits with keeping the boycott intact long enough to force change.

## Practical application
If you're trying to build support for something — a cause, an initiative, a new habit within a team — recognize that strong ties and weak ties do different jobs and neither substitutes for the other. Use close, trusted relationships to get initial buy-in and early momentum; that's what strong ties are good at. But if you want it to spread past the people who already know and trust you personally, you need to engineer visibility among casual acquaintances so that participation starts to look like the normal, expected thing to do — a weak-tie social habit — rather than staying dependent on your own direct persuasion of every single person involved.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of something you started doing not because a close friend asked you to, but because it seemed like "everyone" around you was already doing it. Who were those people to you, really — and how strong was your actual connection to any of them?
