# 09 — Target, Data Mining, and the Habits We Don't Know We Have

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Most shopping behavior is habitual — people buy roughly the same products, in the same stores, in the same patterns, week after week, which makes them poor targets for advertising trying to change their behavior. But Duhigg points out that a small set of life events reliably break existing shopping habits and force people to build new ones from scratch: moving house, getting married, having a baby. Retailers who can detect one of these habit-resetting moments early get a rare window to establish new brand loyalty before the customer's habits re-solidify around whichever store happened to be there first.

## Why it matters
This reveals a category of behavioral prediction that has nothing to do with what a customer says they want and everything to do with quietly inferring which moment of habit disruption they're in. It's a more unsettling version of the same core mechanism the rest of the book describes — if a company can read the early signals of a habit reset from purchase data alone, it can intervene before the customer has consciously registered that anything in their routine has changed, let alone told anyone about it.

## Example from the book
Duhigg describes how Target's statisticians, led by an analyst named Andrew Pole, built a model to predict which shoppers were likely pregnant based purely on shifts in purchasing patterns — unscented lotion, cotton balls, hand sanitizer, and specific mineral supplements appearing together, often within a fairly narrow, trimester-linked timing window. Customers flagged by the model started receiving mailers with coupons for baby products. The story that made this approach famous, as Duhigg tells it, involves a man who complained to a Minnesota Target store that his teenage daughter had been sent coupons for baby items, only to later learn from his daughter that she was, in fact, pregnant — the algorithm had picked up the shift in her habits before she'd told her own family. After the approach drew public unease, Target reportedly began deliberately interspersing the baby-product offers among unrelated coupons — lawn care next to diapers — so the targeting felt like coincidence rather than surveillance, since people respond far better to a nudge that doesn't feel like it's watching them. It's worth noting that some particulars of this widely retold anecdote have been questioned since the book's publication, though the underlying method — inferring habit disruption from purchase pattern shifts — is well documented on its own.

## Practical application
Turn the lens on yourself rather than only on the retailer: the moments when your own shopping, spending, or routine habits are most in flux — a move, a new job, a new relationship, a health change — are exactly the moments you're most susceptible to picking up a new default without deliberately choosing it. Recognizing that you're mid-reset is itself useful information, because it means whatever choice happens to be convenient or heavily marketed at that moment has an outsized chance of becoming your new default habit, for better or worse.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think back to a major life change — a move, a new job, a new relationship. What new habits, brands, or routines quietly became your default during that window, and would you have chosen them deliberately if you'd been paying attention?
