# 08 — Crisis as an Opportunity: How Organizations Rewrite Their Habits

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Organizations, like individuals, run on institutional habits — established routines and unspoken norms about who talks to whom, who has authority to act, and how information moves. Duhigg's claim is that these organizational habits are usually just as resistant to deliberate change as personal ones are, with one major exception: **a crisis temporarily suspends the resistance.** In the aftermath of a visible failure, people who would normally defend the existing way of doing things become willing, even eager, to accept new routines — which makes a crisis one of the only reliable windows for a leader to rewrite deeply entrenched institutional habits.

## Why it matters
This reframes crisis response as something more than damage control. Most organizations treat a crisis as a problem to be contained and then forgotten as quickly as possible, restoring the prior routines once the immediate danger passes. Duhigg's point is that this wastes the one moment when structural change was actually possible — the discomfort and scrutiny that follow a serious failure are exactly what's needed to overcome the institutional inertia that would otherwise block reform. A leader who understands this treats the crisis not as something to survive back to normal, but as leverage to install the habits that should have existed before the crisis happened.

## Example from the book
Duhigg describes a hospital that suffered a series of serious surgical errors, including a wrong-site surgery, that drew public scrutiny, regulatory fines, and a loss of institutional trust. Rather than quietly tightening a few procedures and moving on, hospital leadership used the crisis as cover to overhaul how surgical teams communicated across departments — instituting mandatory pre-operation checklists and cross-checks that, before the crisis, staff would likely have resisted as bureaucratic overreach on top of an already-functioning system. Because the failure was public and undeniable, the same staff accepted sweeping changes to habits they'd followed for years without complaint, and the hospital's error rates fell as a result. Duhigg's broader argument is that this pattern repeats across very different kinds of institutions: a visible, undeniable failure creates a short window where an organization's habitual resistance to change goes quiet, and whoever is positioned to propose new routines in that window has far more leverage than they would under ordinary, stable conditions.

## Practical application
If you're leading through an organizational failure, resist the instinct to move as fast as possible back to the way things were before. Use the period immediately following the failure — while scrutiny and willingness to change are both unusually high — to install the structural fix you actually believe is needed, even if it's more sweeping than the specific incident technically requires. That same willingness to accept new routines will fade quickly once the crisis passes from memory, so the leverage is real but time-limited.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a failure or crisis you've lived through in a team or organization. Was it used as an opportunity to fix the deeper structural habit that caused it, or did everyone quietly return to the old routine once the immediate pressure passed?
