# 03 — The Craving Brain: Pepsodent and Febreze

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
A cue and a reward aren't enough to lock in a habit on their own — Duhigg argues the loop needs **craving** to close the circuit: an anticipatory wanting of the reward that starts firing the moment the cue appears, before the routine has even happened. Craving is what turns a cue from a neutral piece of information into something that pulls behavior forward. And critically, craving can be manufactured. It doesn't have to arise naturally from a behavior's actual function — advertisers and product designers can build an artificial craving around a sensory signal that has nothing to do with whether the product is actually working.

## Why it matters
This is the mechanism behind an enormous share of consumer habit formation, and understanding it explains why some genuinely useful products fail commercially while some marginally useful ones become daily rituals. A product only becomes habitual if using it produces a craveable sensory signal the brain can attach to — not just at the point of measurable outcome, but immediately, every time. If a product works but produces no felt signal of working, it has nothing for a habit loop to close around, and can lose to a product that manufactures a strong signal even if that signal is disconnected from actual effectiveness.

## Example from the book
Duhigg tells the story of Claude Hopkins, the advertising executive who turned Pepsodent from a failing product into the first mass-market toothbrushing habit in America. Hopkins's ads didn't sell dental health — they sold the "film" on your teeth, describing it in vivid, cue-triggering language, and promised a cooling, tingling sensation from the toothpaste as proof the film was being removed. That tingle, from citric acid and mint oil additives, does nothing to clean teeth — but it gave users an immediate, repeatable sensory reward to crave, and toothbrushing went from a rare habit to a national one within a decade.

He pairs this with Procter & Gamble's Febreze, originally launched as an odor eliminator for smokers, pet owners, and anyone living with persistent smells. It flopped — because people who live with a smell every day become "nose-blind" to it and never feel the trigger to spray. P&G nearly killed the product until researchers visited a cat owner who used Febreze anyway, not to fight odor she couldn't smell, but as a finishing spritz after making the bed, because she genuinely liked how it made a clean room feel complete. P&G re-marketed around that instinct — commercials showing Febreze as the reward at the end of a cleaning routine rather than a fix for a smell — and sales took off.

## Practical application
When you're trying to build a new habit, don't rely on a benefit you can't feel in the moment. Saving money, exercising, or eating well all have real rewards, but those rewards are usually delayed by weeks or months, which gives craving nothing immediate to attach to. Borrow the Febreze logic instead: attach your new routine to an existing craving that already fires reliably (finish a workout, then have the good coffee you look forward to anyway) so the loop has something to crave from day one, rather than waiting for the delayed, real payoff to eventually reinforce it on its own.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a product or app you use out of habit rather than necessity. What's the actual sensory signal you're craving when you reach for it — and is it connected at all to whether it's doing you any good?
