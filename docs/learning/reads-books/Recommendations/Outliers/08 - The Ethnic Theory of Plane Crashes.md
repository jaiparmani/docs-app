# 08 - The Ethnic Theory of Plane Crashes

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Gladwell extends the cultural-legacy argument into the cockpit, examining a cluster of crashes involving Korean Air in the 1990s and asking why the airline's safety record was, for a period, dramatically worse than comparable carriers. His answer draws on the cross-cultural psychology concept of "power distance" — how much a culture accepts and expects unequal distribution of authority between people of different rank. In cultures with high power distance, subordinates are far less likely to directly contradict or challenge a superior, even when the superior is visibly making a dangerous mistake. Gladwell argues this cultural pattern was showing up, quite literally, in the cockpit: first officers and flight engineers, trained in a high-power-distance culture, would hint and hedge rather than tell a captain directly that he was flying the plane into danger.

## Why it matters
This is a striking, uncomfortable extension of the book's thesis into a life-and-death setting: a cultural trait that's largely invisible and unremarkable in everyday politeness norms became, under the specific high-stakes conditions of an airline cockpit, a measurable factor in crash rates. It also matters because of what fixed it — the problem wasn't individual pilot competence, so the fix wasn't firing pilots, it was systemic: retraining an entire airline's communication culture from the ground up.

## Example from the book
Gladwell recounts how Korean Air, after a public reckoning with its safety record, brought in outside aviation consultants and made English the mandatory working language for cockpit crews — not because the pilots didn't speak Korean fluently, but because switching to a second language stripped away the deeply ingrained hierarchical speech patterns embedded in Korean linguistic structure, making it psychologically easier for a junior officer to speak to a captain more directly and assertively. Combined with broader crew resource management training aimed explicitly at getting subordinates to challenge authority when safety was at stake, the airline's crash rate improved dramatically in the years that followed.

## Practical application
In any high-stakes team — a surgical unit, a flight crew, a startup with a strong founder — treat the ease with which a junior person can flag a senior person's mistake as a system property you can design for, not just a matter of individual courage. If you're the senior person, explicitly invite direct challenge rather than assuming an open-door policy is enough to overcome the natural hesitance of hierarchy; if you're building the system, consider whether structural changes — like Korean Air's shift in working language — could lower the psychological cost of speaking up more effectively than a values statement alone could.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a hierarchy you operate inside — at work, at home, in a group you're part of — where you suspect people are hedging or staying quiet about a real problem because directly contradicting someone senior feels culturally off-limits?
