# 04 - The IQ Threshold and the Termites

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Gladwell opens what the book calls "The Trouble with Geniuses" by introducing Chris Langan, a man with a measured IQ around 195 — by some estimates higher than Einstein's — who nonetheless spent much of his adult life working manual labor jobs and never achieved the public recognition his raw cognitive horsepower would predict. Gladwell uses Langan's story to introduce a specific, research-backed claim: IQ correlates with real-world achievement only up to a point, somewhere around 120, after which additional IQ points stop reliably predicting additional success. Beyond that threshold, other factors — the subject of the next chunk — start to matter more than raw intelligence.

## Why it matters
This threshold idea cuts directly against the "smartest person wins" intuition running through a lot of meritocratic thinking. If IQ only matters up to a point, then the gap between a merely very smart, highly successful person and an off-the-charts genius who never gets there isn't explained by intelligence at all — it has to be explained by something else, which is exactly the gap the rest of the chapter, and the following chunk on Langan versus Robert Oppenheimer, tries to fill.

## Example from the book
Gladwell's main evidence is the work of psychologist Lewis Terman, who beginning in 1921 tracked a cohort of California schoolchildren with exceptionally high IQ scores — nicknamed the "Termites" — expecting them to grow into history's next generation of eminent scientists, leaders, and creators. Followed for decades, the Termites as a group did reasonably well in conventional terms, but produced strikingly few standouts of major national or scientific significance, and a nontrivial number ended up in relatively ordinary careers. Gladwell's sharpest detail: two boys tested by Terman's team as children failed to make the IQ cutoff for inclusion in the study — William Shockley and Luis Alvarez — and both went on to win Nobel Prizes, while many of the officially selected "geniuses" never achieved comparable distinction. A study designed to prove that IQ predicts eminence ended up demonstrating close to the opposite, once you look past the threshold.

## Practical application
When you're assessing potential — your own, a hire's, a student's — resist treating a single high test score, credential, or measured aptitude as the whole story once it's already comfortably above a competent baseline. Above that baseline, the more useful diagnostic questions are about practical skills, opportunity, and circumstance — the things the next chunk on Langan and Oppenheimer gets into — rather than whether the number could theoretically go even higher still.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Have you ever assumed someone's raw intelligence (or your own) was the ceiling on what they could achieve — and can you think of a case, like Shockley falling outside Terman's official cutoff, where the measurement missed something the outcome later proved wrong?
