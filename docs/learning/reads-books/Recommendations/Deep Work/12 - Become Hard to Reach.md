# 12 — Become Hard to Reach

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Closing Rule 4, Newport targets email specifically as one of the largest sources of unexamined shallow work, and argues that most professionals default to a "sender-centric" approach — replying to everything, quickly, in order to keep senders satisfied — which, far from reducing the burden, actively trains the people around you to send more low-value, open-ended requests, since fast responsiveness is itself a signal that this channel is a cheap way to get your attention. His proposed inversion has two parts: a "sender filter," some form of stated or structural friction (a note on where to reach you, criteria for what warrants a reply) that shifts a portion of the filtering work onto the sender rather than absorbing all of it yourself; and writing "process-centric" emails that end each exchange with a concrete decision or next step, closing the loop in one message instead of leaving it open for another round.

## Why it matters
Email load isn't fixed; it's substantially shaped by how you've trained the people who send it to you, which means the sender-centric default is partly self-inflicted rather than purely externally imposed. Recognizing this matters because it turns "I get too much email" from a fact you have to absorb into a pattern you can deliberately reshape — both by making it slightly harder to start an open-ended thread with you, and by ending the threads you do engage with decisively rather than letting them drift into extra rounds of back-and-forth that eat into time that would otherwise go to deep work.

## Example from the book
Newport describes his own practice of stating explicitly, on his contact page, that he doesn't guarantee a response to every message and asking correspondents to be selective about what they send — a direct sender filter that shifts some of the burden of deciding what's worth his attention back onto the person initiating contact. He pairs this with the practice of writing process-centric emails: rather than a reply that reopens a question, he advocates closing each message with an explicit proposed resolution — a stated plan of action, with a default outcome ("I'll proceed with X unless I hear otherwise by Friday") — specifically to prevent an open-ended thread from requiring yet another exchange to actually resolve.

## Practical application
Look at the last several email threads you were part of that took more than two replies to resolve, and rewrite the first reply as a process-centric message — one that proposes a specific next step or default decision instead of just answering the question and leaving it open. Separately, consider one small version of a sender filter you could put into practice, even informally: a stated expectation about response time, or a habit of asking senders to be specific about what decision they actually need from you.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Look at your inbox right now. How many open threads are open only because your own last reply left the next step ambiguous rather than because the other person genuinely needed more from you?
