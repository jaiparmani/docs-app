# 10 — The Any-Benefit Mindset vs. the Craftsman Approach

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Opening Rule 3, "Quit Social Media," Newport names the default reasoning most people use to justify keeping any given digital tool: the "any-benefit mindset" — if a tool provides any conceivable benefit at all, however marginal, that's treated as sufficient justification for using it, with the tool's costs (fragmented attention, time, the training toward distraction covered in chunk 08) left out of the calculation entirely. Against this he sets the "craftsman approach to tool selection," borrowed loosely from how skilled tradespeople choose equipment: first identify the small number of core factors that actually determine success in your professional and personal life, then adopt a given tool only if its positive impact on those specific factors substantially outweighs its negative impacts — not merely if it helps at all.

## Why it matters
The any-benefit mindset is why so many tools accumulate in a life without ever being seriously evaluated: almost anything can clear the bar of "provides some benefit," which means the bar itself does no real filtering work. The craftsman approach matters because it replaces an unwinnable question ("does this help even a little?") with a winnable one ("given what actually matters to me, does this clearly earn its cost?") — and it applies well beyond social media, to any app, subscription, or habit that's currently justified only by the fact that it's not useless.

## Example from the book
Newport recounts writer Baratunde Thurston's twenty-five day, near-total break from social media and online infotainment, documented publicly at the time, and undertaken after Thurston felt his always-on digital life had become genuinely burdensome. Newport uses Thurston's own account of the break — finding it more valuable and less costly than expected to be away — as evidence that the perceived indispensability of social media is often unexamined: someone who actually stepped away found the loss much smaller than the any-benefit mindset would have predicted, because the benefits that had justified constant use turned out to be minor once weighed honestly against what was gained by their absence.

## Practical application
Pick one digital tool you use regularly without having consciously chosen it — something you use because "it doesn't hurt" rather than because it clearly serves a defined goal. List the two or three factors that actually determine success in the relevant part of your life (professional output, a specific relationship, a specific skill), and honestly assess whether the tool's impact on those factors is substantial and positive, or merely nonzero. If it's the latter, treat that as grounds to drop it, at least as an experiment.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick the app on your phone you use most out of habit. If you had to justify it using the craftsman test — a substantial, specific benefit to something you've named as actually mattering — rather than "it doesn't really hurt," would it survive?
