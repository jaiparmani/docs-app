# 08 — Don't Take Breaks from Focus, Take Breaks from Distraction

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Opening Rule 2, "Embrace Boredom," Newport diagnoses a habit most people never examine: the default of filling any small gap in attention — a queue, a pause between tasks, a lull in a conversation — with a quick hit of distraction, usually a phone check. He argues this trains the brain to be unable to tolerate boredom, which directly sabotages deep work, since deep work requires sustaining attention through exactly the kind of low-stimulation stretches that a phone-conditioned brain has learned to escape reflexively. His inversion of the usual advice is precise: don't schedule occasional breaks from focus to allow distraction; instead schedule specific, limited windows when distraction is allowed, and treat all other time — even time when you're not actively doing deep work — as the default, protected state.

## Why it matters
This reframes "willpower to resist distraction" as a trainable capacity rather than a fixed trait, and it locates the training in the moments that seem to matter least. Most people assume their ability to focus only gets tested during the deep-work session itself; Newport's claim is that it's actually being shaped continuously, all day, every time a small ordinary boredom is relieved by a glance at a screen. Skip the phone during a two-minute wait in line and you're strengthening the same muscle you'll need an hour later at your desk; reach for it, and you're weakening that same muscle, regardless of whether you were "working" at the time.

## Example from the book
Newport describes his own practice of scheduling internet blocks in advance — deciding ahead of time exactly when browsing or checking messages is permitted — and applying this schedule even outside of dedicated deep-work sessions, not just during them. The point of extending the schedule beyond work hours, he explains, is that the training effect only works if the brain doesn't get to practice reflexive distraction-seeking during the "off" periods either; a rule that only holds during work hours leaves the underlying habit of chasing novelty untouched for most of the day.

## Practical application
Pick a small number of fixed windows during your day when checking your phone or browsing is explicitly allowed, and write them down in advance rather than deciding in the moment. Outside those windows, when you notice the urge to relieve a moment of boredom with a glance at a screen, treat that urge itself as the target of the exercise — sit with it rather than resolving it — since the goal isn't the task you're avoiding, it's rebuilding tolerance for the absence of stimulation that deep work depends on.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> Count how many times today you reached for your phone during a genuinely trivial gap — a queue, an elevator, a pause before a meeting starts. Is that number closer to zero, or closer to a hundred, and what does that number predict about your ability to sit with a hard problem for an hour?
