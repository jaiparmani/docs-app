# 07 — Be Lazy

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Newport closes Rule 1 with a claim that runs against most productivity advice: genuine, unstructured downtime is not wasted time — it's a necessary input to deep work, not a reward you've earned after it. He draws on two lines of research to make the case: unconscious thought theory, which suggests that stepping away from a hard problem lets background mental processes continue working on it without conscious effort, often producing insight that focused, effortful attention couldn't reach directly; and attention restoration theory, which treats directed attention as a finite resource that depletes over a day of concentrated work and can only be replenished by genuinely undirected time, particularly time away from goal-driven tasks and screens, not by more low-grade stimulation.

## Why it matters
This directly undercuts the instinct to treat every spare evening minute as an opportunity for one more small task, "just checking" email or messages "for a minute" before bed. That kind of check looks like rest because it's brief and low-effort, but it keeps directed attention engaged rather than letting it recover, and it re-opens the exact kind of task-switching that produces attention residue (chunk 01) right when the mind most needs to disengage. Newport's point is that protecting evenings and downtime isn't indulgent; it's what makes the next day's deep work possible at all — skipping it doesn't create more capacity for focus, it quietly borrows against tomorrow's.

## Example from the book
Newport describes his own strict evening shutdown ritual: a fixed point in the evening after which he doesn't check email or think about open work problems at all, marked by a specific closing routine he uses to consciously signal to himself that the workday is over. He contrasts this explicitly with the common habit of a "quick check" of messages during evening downtime, arguing that even a glance is enough to reactivate directed attention and open loops that then occupy background cognitive resources for the rest of the evening, defeating the restorative purpose of the break even though it consumed almost no visible time.

## Practical application
Institute a specific shutdown ritual of your own — a fixed phrase, a checklist review, or simply closing your laptop at a set time — that marks the unambiguous end of your workday, and treat it as a real boundary rather than a suggestion. When you notice the urge to "just check" something during protected downtime, recognize it as attention-residue bait rather than harmless rest, and if a hard problem is genuinely unresolved, trust the unconscious thought process to keep working on it without your active attention rather than forcing another round of conscious effort.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> How much of your "downtime" in a typical evening is actually undirected rest, versus low-effort screen time that still keeps your directed attention quietly switched on?
