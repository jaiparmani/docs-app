# 06 — Execute Like a Business

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Newport imports a framework from business strategy — the "4 Disciplines of Execution" (4DX) — and applies it directly to the problem of actually following through on a commitment to deep work, rather than letting it quietly slide once the initial enthusiasm fades. The four disciplines are: focus on the wildly important, meaning pick a small number of deep-work goals rather than trying to protect focus everywhere at once; act on lead measures, tracking the behavior that drives the outcome (hours spent in deep work) rather than the lagging outcome itself (the finished product), since lag measures arrive too late to correct course; keep a compelling scoreboard, a visible record of the lead measure that creates its own motivation to keep the streak going; and create a cadence of accountability, a regular, recurring review of the scoreboard against the goal.

## Why it matters
Most people who decide to "do more deep work" fail not at the decision but at the follow-through — the plan quietly erodes over a few weeks with no mechanism forcing a course correction. 4DX matters here because it's specifically designed for exactly that failure mode in organizations, and Newport's insight is that an individual's relationship to their own deep-work habit has the same structure as a team's relationship to a strategic goal: too many competing priorities, a tendency to judge by results that arrive too late to fix anything, and no natural forcing function to notice drift before it's already happened.

## Example from the book
Newport applies the framework concretely to deep work itself: instead of tracking finished output (a completed paper, a shipped feature) as the measure of progress, he tracks the lead measure directly — hours spent in a state of deep work — using a simple, visible scoreboard, historically a piece of paper with a tally mark added for each deep-work session completed. The visible, growing count of marks becomes its own motivator, distinct from and faster-arriving than the eventual outcome, and a weekly review of the tally against his goal serves as the accountability cadence that catches a slipping habit before it collapses entirely.

## Practical application
Pick one wildly important deep-work goal rather than several. Choose a lead measure you can track daily — hours of deep work, or number of sessions completed — rather than only judging yourself by final output. Put a visible scoreboard somewhere you'll actually see it (a wall calendar, a physical tally, a simple counter), and set a fixed weekly moment to review it against your goal, treating a dip in the count as the trigger to intervene rather than waiting to notice the project has stalled.

> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you tracked only your hours of genuinely undistracted deep work this week, with a visible tally you saw every day, do you think the number would embarrass you or reassure you — and either way, would you have known that without tracking it?
