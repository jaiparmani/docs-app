# 07 — Dollar-Cost Averaging

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Among the formula-based approaches Graham recommends for the defensive investor, dollar-cost averaging is the simplest: invest a fixed sum of money at regular intervals — monthly, say, or quarterly — regardless of what the market is doing that day. Because the sum is fixed, this arithmetic naturally buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when prices are high, without requiring the investor to make any judgment call about whether now is a good or bad time to buy. Graham's interest in it isn't that it produces the mathematically optimal outcome in every scenario — it's that it removes an entire category of decision, the timing decision, from the investor's hands, and that category is exactly where Graham believes most investors do the most damage to themselves.

## Why it matters
This connects directly to the Mr. Market allegory and the broader warning against forecasting: if you accept that you can't reliably predict where prices are headed, and that trying to do so tends to make you buy more when you're excited (prices high) and less when you're scared (prices low) — the opposite of what you'd want — then a mechanical rule that removes your own judgment from the timing question isn't a compromise, it's a genuine advantage. Dollar-cost averaging works less because of clever math and more because it's a pre-commitment device against the investor's own worst instincts, executed automatically before those instincts get a chance to interfere.

## Example from the book
Graham discusses dollar-cost averaging as part of a broader category he calls "formula investing" — mechanical plans that specify in advance what an investor will do, so that decisions aren't made in the emotional heat of a particular market moment. He notes that the approach demands real discipline of a specific kind: it requires continuing to invest through periods when prices are falling and continuing to feel uncomfortable, which is precisely the period when the formula is doing its most useful work, buying more shares at lower prices. The investor who abandons the plan during a downturn — the moment it's hardest to keep going — gives up the exact benefit the formula was designed to capture.

## Practical application
Set up a recurring, automatic investment on a fixed schedule into your diversified defensive holdings, sized so that you can genuinely sustain it through a prolonged downturn without needing to touch the money for other purposes. The value of the plan isn't in any single contribution, it's in your ability to keep making contributions on schedule specifically when the market is unpleasant — so treat "did I keep investing during the last bad stretch" as the real measure of whether the plan is working, not "did I pick a good month to start."

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think back to the last significant market downturn you lived through. Did you keep investing on schedule through it, or did you pause — and if you paused, was that a decision, or a reaction?
