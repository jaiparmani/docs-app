# 02 — Mr. Market

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Graham's most famous device is a parable: imagine you own a small stake in a private business alongside a partner named Mr. Market. Every single day, without fail, Mr. Market shows up and quotes you a price — he'll either buy your stake at that price or sell you more of his, your choice. The catch is that Mr. Market is emotionally unstable. Some days he's euphoric and sees nothing but a bright future, so he names a high price. Other days he's despondent and sees nothing but disaster ahead, so he names a price that's absurdly low. He isn't offended if you ignore him — he'll be back tomorrow with a new quote regardless. The allegory is Graham's stand-in for the stock market itself: a daily source of prices, not a source of truth about what a business is actually worth.

## Why it matters
The natural instinct most people bring to markets is to treat the quoted price as information — if the price is falling, something must be wrong; if it's rising, something must be right. Mr. Market inverts that instinct. His swings tell you about his mood, not about the underlying business, which most of the time hasn't changed nearly as much as his quotes suggest. Once you internalize this, price volatility stops being a signal you're obligated to react to and becomes optional raw material — useful when it hands you a good price, ignorable the rest of the time. This is the psychological foundation underneath the more technical concept of margin of safety: you can only buy below intrinsic value if you're willing to let Mr. Market's panic set the price while your own analysis sets your opinion of value.

## Example from the book
Graham is explicit that the whole value of the parable is in the freedom it grants you: because you're not obligated to trade with Mr. Market on any given day, his manic-depressive quoting is your servant, not your master. He warns that the investor who lets Mr. Market's daily mood become their own — getting optimistic when he's optimistic, panicking when he panics — has surrendered the one advantage a private investor has over the market itself: the ability to simply not participate in a bad quote. The businessperson who owns a real, illiquid private company never gets a daily temptation to sell in a panic, because there's no Mr. Market showing up at their door — Graham's point is that the emotional habits of the illiquid business owner are exactly the habits the stock investor should try to import.

## Practical application
The next time a stock you own drops sharply on no real news about the underlying business, run the Mr. Market frame explicitly: is this a change in the business, or a change in Mr. Market's mood? If nothing about the company's earnings power, competitive position, or balance sheet has actually changed, treat the drop as Mr. Market having a bad day — either ignore his quote entirely, or, if your own analysis says the new price is a genuine bargain relative to value, treat it as an opportunity he's handing you rather than a verdict he's rendering.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Recall the last time a price drop made you anxious enough to consider selling. Looking back, was that decision responding to Mr. Market's mood, or to a real, analyzable change in the business itself?
