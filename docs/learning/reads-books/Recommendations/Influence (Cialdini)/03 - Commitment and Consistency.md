# 03 — Commitment and Consistency

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Once a person makes a stand — especially an active, public, effortful, and freely chosen one — they come under psychological pressure to behave in ways consistent with that stand, even if the original reasons for taking it fade or disappear. Cialdini frames consistency as a valued trait in almost every culture, associated with rationality, honesty, and strength of character, which makes inconsistency feel like a small identity threat people go out of their way to avoid. The practical exploit is the foot-in-the-door technique: get someone to agree to a small, easy request first, and a subsequent, related, much larger request becomes far more likely to succeed — not because the person was persuaded by new arguments, but because refusing the second request would make the first commitment feel meaningless.

## Why it matters
This principle explains why compliance professionals so often engineer a small "yes" before ever asking for the real thing — a signature, a survey answer, a show of interest — because that small commitment reshapes how the person sees themselves ("I'm the kind of person who supports this cause / cares about this issue / bought from this company before"), and the bigger request is then evaluated against that self-image rather than fresh. It also explains why written and public commitments are so much stickier than private, verbal ones: the more effort and visibility a commitment carries, the more identity is riding on staying consistent with it.

## Example from the book
Cialdini cites classic field research (Freedman and Fraser) in which homeowners who agreed to display a small, unobtrusive "Be a Safe Driver" sign or sign a minor petition were, weeks later, dramatically more likely to agree to install a large, unattractive "Drive Carefully" billboard-sized sign on their front lawn than homeowners approached cold — even though the two requests came from unrelated groups. He also draws on research into Chinese POW camps during the Korean War, where captors extracted small, seemingly harmless written or spoken statements from American prisoners — mildly critical comments about the United States, or mild praise of communism — then had the prisoner sign the statement, read it aloud, or see it broadcast or published. Each step was small enough that no prisoner felt he had "collaborated," but the accumulated public record of his own words pulled his self-image gradually further from where he started, producing genuine shifts in attitude that interrogation alone rarely achieved. On the more mundane end, Cialdini describes toy manufacturers who deliberately advertise a specific toy heavily before Christmas, letting stores under-stock it — parents can't find it, and buy a substitute gift instead, having "promised" the child the advertised toy. After Christmas, the manufacturer resumes advertising that same toy directly to children, who then pressure parents to fulfill the original promise, driving a second wave of purchases in January and February.

## Practical application
Cialdini's defensive advice centers on listening for a specific internal signal: a feeling in your stomach that tells you a request is uncomfortable but you're inclined to comply anyway "because you already agreed to something related." When that gut-level discomfort shows up, treat it as data — ask directly whether, knowing what you know now, you'd make the same commitment again from scratch. If the honest answer is no, you're free to refuse the escalation, whatever the earlier small "yes" was. It also helps to be deliberately slower to make small commitments in contexts — sales, fundraising, negotiations — where you suspect the small ask is a first step rather than the actual point.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a time you agreed to something larger mainly because refusing would have felt inconsistent with an earlier, smaller commitment. If you'd been asked for the larger thing cold, with no prior step, would you have said yes?
