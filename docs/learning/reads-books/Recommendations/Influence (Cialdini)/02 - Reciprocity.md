# 02 — Reciprocity

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Cialdini argues reciprocity is close to a human universal: every known culture instills a felt obligation to repay what others provide, and that obligation is powerful enough to compel repayment even when the original gift was unrequested, unwanted, or trivially small. The rule doesn't just cover favors — it covers concessions. If someone makes a small concession to you, you feel pressure to make a concession back, which is what makes the "rejection-then-retreat" technique work: ask for something large first (expecting refusal), then retreat to the smaller request you actually wanted, and the target feels obligated to reciprocate your "concession" by accepting it, even though your final ask may be identical to what a direct request would have gotten refused.

## Why it matters
Because the rule is nearly automatic and applies even to unwanted or unsolicited gifts, it hands enormous leverage to whoever gives first. A gift as small as a free sample, a favor, or a moment of unsolicited generosity creates a debt that people will often repay many times over just to discharge the discomfort of feeling obligated. This is why reciprocity so often appears as the first tool in a compliance professional's kit — it lets them control who owes whom, simply by choosing to go first.

## Example from the book
Cialdini describes members of the Krishna Society (ISKCON) soliciting donations in airports using a two-step method: before asking for money, a member would press a flower, book, or other small item into a traveler's hand as a gift. Many travelers tried to refuse or hand it back, but the giver would insist it was a gift, refusing to take it back — at which point a request for a donation followed, and a striking number of people gave, despite having no interest in the item and often discarding it minutes later. The obligation attached to the receiving, not to any actual desire for the object. Cialdini also recounts a case of reciprocity operating across decades and continents: in 1985, Ethiopia — then suffering one of its worst famines in recorded history — sent a donation of relief aid to Mexico City to help victims of that year's earthquake. When Cialdini investigated why one of the poorest, most crisis-stricken nations on earth would send aid to a country with vastly greater resources, he traced it to 1935, when Mexico had sent assistance to Ethiopia during Italy's invasion. Fifty years and a famine later, the obligation still held.

## Practical application
Cialdini's own advice, consistent with the book's overall "how to say no" framing, is not to refuse all gifts and favors defensively — that's both impractical and unfriendly — but to mentally re-label an unsolicited gift or favor as what it is (a gift or favor) rather than as the opening move of an exchange. If someone offers something free, accept it if you want it, but treat your acceptance and your later decision about their actual request as two separate events rather than one obligation. Concretely: when a salesperson makes a "concession" partway through a negotiation, ask whether you'd want the resulting offer on its own merits, independent of the fact that they moved first.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a free sample, favor, or small gift you've received recently in a sales or fundraising context. Did you end up buying or giving more than you would have if you'd been offered the same final terms with no gift attached first?
