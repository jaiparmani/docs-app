# 07 — Scarcity

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Opportunities, objects, and information seem more valuable to us as they become less available — Cialdini frames this as two separate mechanisms stacked together. First, scarcity acts as a mental shortcut for value: things that are hard to get are frequently good, so difficulty of access becomes a quick proxy for quality even when it isn't actually correlated with anything. Second, and more powerfully, losing a freedom or option triggers psychological reactance — a motivational state where people want the restricted thing more specifically *because* it's being restricted, independent of how much they wanted it beforehand. Limited-time offers, limited quantities, and exclusive access all exploit one or both mechanisms.

## Why it matters
Reactance in particular means scarcity tactics don't just make people buy sooner — they can make people want something *more* than they did before any restriction was introduced, which is a stronger and stranger effect than simple urgency. Cialdini's point is that this makes scarcity one of the least rational of the six principles to be swayed by: the object or opportunity hasn't actually changed in value, only its availability has, and availability alone is doing all the persuasive work.

## Example from the book
Cialdini cites research into what's sometimes called the "Romeo and Juliet effect": studies of dating and young married couples found that the more parents interfered with and disapproved of a couple's relationship, the more intensely in love the couple reported feeling, and that as parental interference rose and fell over time, romantic intensity tracked it — rising when interference increased, cooling when it eased. Cialdini offers this as psychological reactance in a domain most people wouldn't initially connect to sales tactics: the couple wasn't falling more in love because their bond was objectively stronger, but because the relationship had been made scarcer by outside restriction. His signature commercial example is Coca-Cola's 1985 "New Coke" launch, where the company reformulated its flagship drink after blind taste tests showed the new formula was preferred over both the original and Pepsi. The public reaction was a fierce backlash — consumers hoarded the old formula, organized protest campaigns, and demanded its return — even though many of the same people, in blind tests, had preferred the new taste. Cialdini's reading is that the moment the original formula became unavailable, its scarcity generated a surge of demand for it that had nothing to do with the taste-test data; the company reintroduced the original as "Coca-Cola Classic" within a few months.

## Practical application
Cialdini's advice here is unusually specific among the six chapters: scarcity produces a distinct physical arousal — a jolt of urgency — and that arousal itself is the signal to slow down, not speed up. When you notice the emotional spike that comes with "limited time," "only a few left," or "act now," the corrective question isn't "do I want this" (scarcity will always make the answer feel like yes), but "why do I want this — for its own use and enjoyment, or simply because I might not be able to get it." If the honest answer leans toward the second, the scarcity is doing the wanting for you, and the object's actual utility to you hasn't changed at all.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of something you rushed to buy, claim, or decide on mainly because it was framed as limited, exclusive, or about to disappear. Once the deadline or restriction is set aside, does the thing itself still hold the same appeal?
