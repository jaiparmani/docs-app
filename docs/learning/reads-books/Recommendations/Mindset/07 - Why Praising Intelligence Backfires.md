# 07 — Why Praising Intelligence Backfires

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
One of the book's most concrete, research-backed claims is that the specific kind of praise a child receives after success shapes which mindset they build — and the intuitively kind option, praising the child's intelligence, is the one that produces worse outcomes. Praising a child as "smart" for succeeding teaches them that their worth is tied to a fixed trait that a test can reveal; praising the effort, strategy, or process that produced the success teaches them that the trait doing the work is something within their control.

## Why it matters
This is the chapter of the book with the clearest experimental backing, and it matters because it upends the default instinct most well-meaning parents and teachers have. "You're so smart" feels like unambiguous encouragement, but Dweck's account of the research shows it quietly sets a child up to protect that "smart" label going forward — which means avoiding exactly the kind of visible struggle and risk of failure that real learning requires. The intervention isn't complicated (praise the process, not the person) but it runs against a strong cultural habit of complimenting people, especially children, on who they are rather than what they did.

## Example from the book
Dweck describes a study she conducted with Claudia Mueller in which several hundred fifth graders were given a set of problems to solve, then told they'd done well, with one group praised for their intelligence ("you must be smart at this") and another group praised for their effort ("you must have worked hard"). When the children were then offered a choice between an easier task and a harder, more instructive one, the effort-praised group overwhelmingly chose the harder task, while the intelligence-praised group leaned toward the easier one. When both groups were subsequently given a genuinely difficult test and told they'd done poorly, the intelligence-praised children's confidence and performance dropped sharply on a follow-up test, and a meaningful number of them misreported their scores upward when asked to report them to peers — while the effort-praised children's performance held up or improved, and they reported their scores accurately.

## Practical application
Audit the praise you give — to children, but also to employees, teammates, or yourself — for how often it names a trait ("you're so talented," "you're a natural leader") versus a specific action ("the way you kept revising that draft really paid off," "you handled that pushback well"). The trait version feels warmer in the moment but sets up exactly the fragility Dweck's study documented; the process version is slightly less flattering and considerably more useful over time.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think back to praise you received growing up that stuck with you. Was it aimed at a trait you had or something you did — and can you trace any of your current avoidance of certain challenges back to that distinction?
