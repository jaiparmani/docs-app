# 09 — Changing Your Mindset Deliberately

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Dweck closes the book's argument by insisting that mindset itself is not a fixed trait — the belief that ability is changeable is, fittingly, itself something a person can learn to hold, even after years of running on the fixed version. She's careful to frame this as neither instant nor purely a matter of positive thinking: changing a mindset means noticing the fixed-mindset voice in real time, understanding where it's coming from, and consciously choosing the growth-mindset response until that response starts happening on its own.

## Why it matters
Without this chapter, the rest of the book risks reading as a diagnosis with no treatment — useful for identifying the problem but silent on what to actually do about it. Dweck's answer matters because it treats mindset change the same way growth-mindset people already treat every other skill: not as something you either have or don't, but as a capability you build through deliberate, repeated practice, which is a fitting and slightly self-demonstrating way to close the argument.

## Example from the book
Dweck describes structured workshops and programs, including one she developed called Brainology, that teach students directly about how the brain forms new connections through effort and practice — essentially teaching the neuroscience underlying the growth mindset rather than just asserting it. In a study she cites involving seventh graders, students who went through a workshop explaining that intellectual ability grows through effortful learning showed an improved trajectory in their math grades over the following months compared with a control group that received study-skills training without the mindset component, despite starting from comparable performance levels. She uses this to argue that a growth mindset isn't just a nicer attitude — under the right conditions, it's a lever with a measurable downstream effect on real performance.

## Practical application
Dweck's suggested practice is to build a habit of noticing your "fixed-mindset persona" — give it a voice, notice when it shows up (usually right before a challenge, right after a setback, or right when someone else succeeds), and instead of arguing yourself out of the feeling, consciously narrate the growth-mindset alternative out loud or on paper: what would I do right now if I believed this was learnable? Repeating that override, especially in the specific moments the fixed voice tends to appear, is what she argues actually shifts the default over time — not a single decision, but a habit built the same slow way any other skill is.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> If you gave your fixed-mindset voice a name and listened for it this week, when do you think it would show up first — and what's the growth-mindset sentence you'd want ready to answer it with?
