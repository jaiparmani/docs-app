# 05 — Sports Champions: Jordan and McEnroe

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Dweck argues that sports offers an unusually clean test of the mindset theory because talent is so visibly present in elite athletes, which makes it easy to assume raw ability alone explains who ends up a champion. Instead, she finds the same split among gifted athletes as everywhere else: some treat their talent as a starting point that still requires relentless building, and some treat it as an identity that obstacles and losses put at risk — and it's this second group, not the first, that tends to underperform relative to their gifts over a full career.

## Why it matters
Sports makes the mindset difference legible in a way office life often doesn't, because performance is public, immediate, and measured. It's harder to explain away a match loss or a missed shot as bad luck when it happens on television in front of an audience — which means how an athlete narrates that loss to themselves, in the moment, is a direct window into which mindset is running. Dweck uses this visibility to argue that "being a natural" and "having a growth mindset" aren't the same trait, and the second one is what determines whether the first one gets fully used.

## Example from the book
Dweck presents Michael Jordan as a growth-mindset case: famously cut from his high school varsity team as a sophomore, he used the cut as a reason to train harder rather than as proof he lacked what it took, and he was known throughout his career for treating losses and missed shots as material to study rather than as verdicts on his ability. She contrasts this with John McEnroe, an immensely talented player whose on-court behavior — arguing line calls, blaming distractions, berating officials — Dweck reads as a fixed mindset in action: because McEnroe's sense of himself was built on being naturally, effortlessly great, anything that interfered with a win felt like an intolerable injustice rather than a normal part of competition to be absorbed and adjusted to.

## Practical application
Watch how you narrate your own losses, in whatever "sport" you're actually in — a missed deadline, a pitch that didn't land, a hard conversation that went badly. The McEnroe move is to locate the cause outside yourself immediately, before you've actually looked at what happened. The Jordan move is to accept the loss as real and specifically usable, then go find the one or two things inside your control that would change the outcome next time. Practice catching yourself mid-McEnroe and asking the Jordan question instead: what does this particular loss teach me that a win wouldn't have?

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of your most recent real setback. In the first ten minutes after it happened, did your mind go looking for what you could learn, or for who and what else was to blame?
