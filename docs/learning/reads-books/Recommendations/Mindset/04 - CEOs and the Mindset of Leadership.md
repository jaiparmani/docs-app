# 04 — CEOs and the Mindset of Leadership

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Dweck extends the mindset framework into business by arguing that a CEO's fixed or growth orientation doesn't stay personal — it becomes the operating culture of the whole company, because employees learn very quickly what kind of information their leader can tolerate hearing. A fixed-mindset executive tends to need constant proof of their own brilliance and surrounds themselves with people who won't challenge that image; a growth-mindset executive treats the company's problems as shared puzzles to solve and actively invites the kind of criticism a fixed-mindset leader would experience as a threat.

## Why it matters
This matters beyond individual psychology because it scales — one person's mindset, sitting at the top of an organization, shapes whether thousands of employees feel safe surfacing bad news, admitting mistakes, or proposing ideas that contradict leadership. A company run on a fixed mindset can look successful for a long stretch, often riding an earlier genuine win, while quietly losing the ability to adapt because no one inside it is allowed to say something is going wrong.

## Example from the book
Dweck contrasts Lee Iacocca, who led a celebrated turnaround at Chrysler in the early 1980s and became a genuine business celebrity, with Jack Welch at General Electric. Iacocca, in Dweck's account, became consumed with protecting his own reputation as a business genius — he surrounded himself with loyalists, resisted grooming a credible successor, and reportedly discouraged the internal dissent that had helped produce the original turnaround, and Chrysler's performance declined through the years he stayed in the role past his early success. Welch, by contrast, built GE's culture explicitly around candor: he introduced "Work-Out" sessions where employees at every level were expected to challenge managers directly and question inefficient practices to their faces, treating the exposure of problems as the company's most valuable habit rather than a threat to be managed away.

## Practical application
You don't need to run a company to apply this — the same test works for any team or group you lead, including a household. Ask whether the people around you are more likely to bring you bad news early, when it's still fixable, or to sit on it because they're not sure how you'll react. If the honest answer is the second one, that's a fixed-mindset signal coming from you, regardless of your intentions, and the fix starts with visibly rewarding the person who tells you something went wrong.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> When someone on your team brings you a problem or a piece of criticism, what's your first visible reaction — and is it the reaction that makes people more or less likely to bring you the next one?
