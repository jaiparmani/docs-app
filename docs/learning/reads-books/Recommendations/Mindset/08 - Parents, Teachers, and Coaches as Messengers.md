# 08 — Parents, Teachers, and Coaches as Messengers

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Beyond the specific mechanics of praise, Dweck argues that adults in authority positions over children and students constantly transmit a mindset through countless small, often unconscious signals — which students get extra help and which get written off, whose mistakes are treated as normal and whose are treated as concerning, who gets challenged and who gets protected from difficulty. Children absorb these signals as a working theory about their own potential well before they can articulate it, and that theory then governs how hard they're willing to try.

## Why it matters
This reframes low performance as sometimes a mindset problem transmitted from the adult rather than a fixed ceiling in the student — which is a genuinely actionable finding, because it means changing an adult's language and expectations can change a struggling student's trajectory without changing the student's underlying ability at all. It also means the same teacher or coach can be inadvertently running two different experiments on two different kids in the same room, based on assumptions about who has "it" and who doesn't.

## Example from the book
Dweck holds up Marva Collins, a Chicago teacher who founded a school for children other institutions had given up on — many labeled unteachable or learning disabled — and taught them a demanding, classical curriculum on the explicit premise that intelligence in these children was undeveloped, not absent, achieving results that contradicted the labels those children had arrived with. She pairs this with UCLA basketball coach John Wooden, who built one of the most successful programs in college sports history around a philosophy that deliberately separated effort from outcome: Wooden defined success as knowing you'd given full effort to become the best you're capable of being, a standard entirely within a player's control, rather than defining success by the scoreboard, which isn't.

## Practical application
If you're in any kind of teaching, coaching, or mentoring role, listen for the moments where you've quietly sorted people into "has potential" and "doesn't" — and notice how much less effort and patience you extend to the second group, often without deciding to. The Collins and Wooden examples both point the same direction: treat the current skill level as the starting data point, not the verdict, and keep the standard you're measuring people against something they can actually control, like effort and process, rather than something they can't, like a single outcome.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there someone in your life — a student, a junior colleague, even yourself — you've quietly filed under "doesn't have it"? What would you do differently this month if you treated that as an unproven guess instead of a settled fact?
