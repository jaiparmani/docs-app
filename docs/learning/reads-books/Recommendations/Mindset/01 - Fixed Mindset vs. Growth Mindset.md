# 01 — Fixed Mindset vs. Growth Mindset

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Dweck's central claim is that people carry, often without realizing it, one of two basic beliefs about where ability comes from. A **fixed mindset** holds that intelligence, talent, and personality are largely set traits — you have a certain amount of them, and life is mostly about proving that amount is high. A **growth mindset** holds that those same qualities are starting points that develop through effort, strategy, and input from others. Neither belief is a personality type in the usual sense; Dweck presents them as a lens on ability that a person can hold in some domains and not others — someone can have a growth mindset about their career and a fixed one about their marriage.

## Why it matters
The mindset itself is invisible, but Dweck argues it quietly sets the terms for almost every important response a person has to their own life: what counts as success, what counts as failure, whether effort is a sign of virtue or a sign of inadequacy, and whether a setback is information or verdict. Two people with identical ability can end up in very different places because one treats a hard problem as a threat to be avoided and the other treats it as the whole point. This is the foundation the rest of the book builds on — every domain Dweck examines (school, sports, business, relationships, parenting) is really the same underlying belief showing up in a different setting.

## Example from the book
Dweck opens the book by describing the French psychologist Alfred Binet, who built the first modern intelligence test in the early 1900s specifically to identify children who needed extra help — not to rank them permanently. Binet believed strongly that with the right training and practice, intelligence could be substantially increased, and he was frustrated that his test was later co-opted by others to justify sorting children into fixed categories. Dweck uses this origin story deliberately: the tool most associated with the idea of fixed, measurable intelligence was invented by someone who believed the opposite, and that irony sets up the book's argument before it even begins.

## Practical application
Notice the domains where you talk about yourself in fixed terms — "I'm just not a numbers person," "I've never been good at confrontation," "some people are naturally organized and I'm not." These aren't neutral descriptions; each one is a small decision about whether that trait is worth investing effort in. Pick one and rewrite it as a growth-mindset sentence — not "I am bad at X" but "I haven't yet put in the kind of practice that would make me good at X" — and notice whether that changes what you're willing to attempt next.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick one area of your life where you've quietly decided "this is just how I am." Where did that belief come from — a genuine, repeated test of effort, or a single early experience you never went back to check?
