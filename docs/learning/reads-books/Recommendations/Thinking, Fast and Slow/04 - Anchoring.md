# 04 — Anchoring

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
When you're asked to estimate an unknown quantity, any number you were recently exposed to — even one you know is arbitrary or irrelevant — pulls your estimate toward it. This is the **anchoring effect**: your mind doesn't generate an independent estimate and then compare it to the anchor; the anchor gets incorporated into the estimation process itself, biasing the outcome even when you're consciously aware the anchor is meaningless.

## Why it matters
Anchoring is one of the most robust and well-replicated findings in the whole book, and it's also one of the most exploitable — the first number mentioned in a salary negotiation, a real estate listing price, or a "recommended donation" amount measurably moves the final outcome, even though everyone involved would deny being influenced by an arbitrary number. Because the effect works even on people who are explicitly warned about it and even on domain experts, awareness alone doesn't neutralize it — which makes it one of the clearer cases in the book where the practical fix has to be procedural rather than just "try to think more carefully."

## Example from the book
In Kahneman and Tversky's original experiment, participants watched a wheel of fortune, rigged to stop on either 10 or 65, and were then asked to estimate the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Participants who saw the wheel land on 65 gave a median estimate of 45%; those who saw it land on 10 gave a median estimate of 25% — a number they had just watched be generated by a spinning wheel, which they knew was random, still shifted their answer by twenty percentage points. Kahneman also describes real-estate agents who, shown a house with an artificially inflated or deflated listing price, adjusted their "independent" professional valuations toward that anchor, despite insisting the listing price hadn't influenced their judgment.

## Practical application
In any negotiation, generate and commit to your own number *before* hearing the other side's opening offer — once you've heard their anchor, your subsequent counter is already contaminated by it, even if you consciously try to ignore it. When you're on the receiving end of an anchor (a first offer, a suggested price, a headline statistic), deliberately generate a competing anchor of your own before responding, rather than adjusting from theirs. Adjustment from someone else's anchor is a weaker move than starting from your own.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think back to a negotiation or price discussion where the other side spoke first. Did their number end up shaping your counteroffer more than you realized at the time?
