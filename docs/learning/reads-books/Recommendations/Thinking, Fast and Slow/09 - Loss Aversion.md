# 09 — Loss Aversion

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good. This asymmetry — losing $100 produces more emotional pain than gaining $100 produces pleasure — is one of Kahneman and Tversky's most consequential findings, and it means people aren't rational maximizers of expected value; they're built to weight avoiding loss far more heavily than achieving an equivalent gain. Kahneman describes this as one of the few genuinely stable, precisely measurable ratios in behavioral science, distinct from the vaguer "people don't like losing" intuition — the coefficient is consistently around 1.5 to 2.5, replicated across many different kinds of stakes.

## Why it matters
Loss aversion explains an enormous amount of otherwise-puzzling economic behavior: why people reject small favorable bets (a coin flip to win $150 or lose $100 has strictly positive expected value, yet most people decline it), why investors hold onto losing stocks far longer than winning ones hoping to "get back to even," why negotiators fight harder to avoid conceding something than to gain an equivalent amount, and why status quo bias is so powerful — giving something up registers as a loss even when the alternative is objectively better. It is the emotional engine underneath prospect theory (the next chunk) and one of the most practically useful single facts in the whole book.

## Example from the book
Kahneman describes a simple experiment: offer someone a coin-flip bet — heads, they lose $100; tails, they win $150. In expected-value terms this is a clearly good bet to take repeatedly. Most people refuse it anyway, and when asked to name the minimum gain that would make the $100 loss risk acceptable, the typical answer clusters around $200 to $250 — meaning people require roughly double the potential gain to offset an equivalent potential loss before they'll accept the risk at all. Kahneman also cites research on golfers, whose putting accuracy is measurably better on putts to *avoid* a bogey than on equivalent-difficulty putts to *achieve* a birdie — the same stroke, the same distance, performed more carefully when framed as avoiding a loss than as securing a gain.

## Practical application
When you notice yourself strongly resisting a change, a trade, or a decision, ask explicitly whether you're weighing the actual expected outcome or reacting to the framing of something as a "loss" relative to your current position. In negotiations, framing a concession as "what they'd be giving up" rather than "what you'd be gaining" activates the other side's loss aversion and typically produces more resistance than a gain-framed equivalent ask — useful to recognize both when you're deploying it and when it's being used on you.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a decision where you stuck with something worse than the alternative mainly because switching felt like giving something up. Would you have made the same choice if you were starting from scratch with no status quo to lose?
