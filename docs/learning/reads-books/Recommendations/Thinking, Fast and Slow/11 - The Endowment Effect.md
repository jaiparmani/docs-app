# 11 — The Endowment Effect

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Simply owning something makes you value it more than you would if you didn't own it — and specifically, the minimum price you'd accept to sell something you own is reliably higher than the maximum price you'd pay to acquire the identical item if you didn't already have it. Kahneman frames the endowment effect as a direct consequence of loss aversion: giving up something you own registers as a loss, and losses are weighted roughly twice as heavily as equivalent gains, so parting with your mug hurts more than acquiring an identical mug would please you.

## Why it matters
The endowment effect undercuts a basic assumption of classical economics — the Coase theorem's prediction that, absent transaction costs, goods should flow freely to whoever values them most through trade, regardless of who starts out owning them. In practice, ownership itself inflates value, which means markets involving personal property, real estate, and negotiated trades systematically produce more impasses than a purely rational model would predict, because both sides are anchored to their own reference point (what they currently have) rather than a shared, objective valuation.

## Example from the book
In the canonical experiment (run by Kahneman with Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler), half a group of students were randomly given a coffee mug and then asked the minimum price they'd sell it for; the other half, who didn't receive a mug, were asked the maximum they'd pay to buy one. Classical theory predicts these two prices should converge, since the mugs were handed out randomly and ownership shouldn't affect a rational valuation. Instead, the median selling price was roughly twice the median buying price — the exact ratio loss aversion predicts. The effect held up across many replications with different objects and disappeared only for goods explicitly held "for trade" rather than for use or personal ownership, such as tokens or cash meant to be exchanged, which don't trigger the same attachment.

## Practical application
When pricing something you own for sale, or evaluating whether to trade or upgrade a possession, explicitly ask what you'd pay for the exact same item if you didn't already own it — that's a better estimate of its actual value to you than your instinctive asking price, which is inflated by the fact that giving it up feels like a loss. This is especially worth doing before rejecting a fair offer on a house, a car, or a piece of equipment: the reluctance to sell "below what it's worth" is often ownership talking, not an objective assessment.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of something you own that you'd refuse to sell at a price you'd happily pay to buy an identical item. What does that gap tell you about how much ownership itself, rather than the item, is driving your valuation?
