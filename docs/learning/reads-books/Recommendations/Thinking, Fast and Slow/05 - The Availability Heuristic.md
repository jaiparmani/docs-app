# 05 — The Availability Heuristic

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
People estimate how frequent or likely something is by how easily examples of it come to mind — not by consulting actual statistics. Anything that makes examples easier to recall (vividness, recency, emotional intensity, personal relevance, media coverage) inflates your sense of how common that thing is, entirely independent of its real base rate. Kahneman calls this substitution the availability heuristic: a genuinely hard question ("how frequent is this?") gets swapped for an easy one ("how easily can I think of examples?") without you noticing the swap happened.

## Why it matters
The availability heuristic is why perceived risk and actual risk routinely diverge in predictable directions — people overestimate the danger of dramatic, memorable causes of death (plane crashes, shark attacks, terrorism) and underestimate common, unglamorous ones (heart disease, diabetes, car accidents), because the former generate vivid, easily-recalled media coverage and the latter don't. This isn't a knowledge gap that more facts automatically fix — it's a structural bias in how ease-of-recall gets mistaken for frequency, which means it can persist even in people who know the actual statistics.

## Example from the book
Kahneman describes an experiment where subjects were asked whether more English words start with the letter K or have K as the third letter. Most people confidently say "starts with K," because words starting with K (king, kite, kind) are much easier to retrieve from memory than words with K in the third position (ask, bike, lake) — even though, in reality, roughly three times as many words have K as the third letter. He also cites research where people were asked to estimate their own risk of dying from various causes; divorce rates among friends, and the frequency of words versus made-up categories, were all consistently over- or underestimated in the direction that recall ease predicted, not in the direction actual frequency data supported.

## Practical application
When you catch yourself judging something as common, dangerous, or important because examples spring to mind easily, ask explicitly: is this actually common, or just memorable? Recent news coverage, a personal anecdote, or a vivid image are all reasons something is easy to recall — none of them are evidence about actual frequency. For any judgment about risk or prevalence that matters (health decisions, safety concerns, business risk), seek out the actual base rate rather than trusting how readily your mind supplies examples.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Is there a risk you currently worry about disproportionately — something you can picture vividly — that the actual statistics would tell you is far less likely than something more mundane you barely think about?
