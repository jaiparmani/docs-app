# 12 — Framing Effects

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Logically equivalent descriptions of the same facts produce different decisions depending purely on how they're worded — not because people are computing different expected outcomes, but because System 1 reacts to the emotional charge of the specific words used, particularly whether the frame emphasizes gains or losses. Kahneman treats framing as one of the clearest demonstrations that people don't evaluate options against some stable, underlying reality; they evaluate the *description* of the options, and different descriptions of an identical reality can flip the decision entirely.

## Why it matters
Framing effects show up anywhere a choice can be described in more than one true way — medical treatment, public policy, pricing, and everyday persuasion — and they're powerful enough to change decisions among doctors, policymakers, and other domain experts who would insist, if asked, that wording shouldn't matter to a substantive medical or economic judgment. Because the effect works even on people who are told about it in advance, recognizing framing in the moment (rather than trusting that expertise makes you immune) is the only real defense.

## Example from the book
Kahneman describes a study of physicians given statistics about a medical procedure phrased two ways: one group was told the one-month survival rate was 90%; another group was told the one-month mortality rate was 10% — mathematically identical information. Physicians shown the "90% survival" framing recommended the procedure significantly more often than physicians shown the "10% mortality" framing, even though the odds being described were exactly the same. He also cites the classic Tversky/Kahneman "Asian disease" experiment: subjects choosing between a policy that would "save 200 out of 600 people" versus one with "a 1/3 chance of saving all 600 and a 2/3 chance of saving no one" strongly preferred the certain option — but when the identical outcomes were reframed as "400 people will die" versus "a 1/3 chance no one dies and a 2/3 chance all 600 die," preferences reversed toward the gamble, because loss-framed language pushed people toward risk-seeking, exactly as the fourfold pattern predicts.

## Practical application
When facing an important choice — medical, financial, or otherwise — deliberately re-describe the options in the opposite frame before deciding: if you were told a survival rate, calculate and consider the mortality rate, and vice versa; if an option is framed as a loss, restate it as a gain. If your preference changes between the two framings of the identical facts, that's a signal your decision is being driven by wording rather than substance, and it's worth slowing down to identify the actual expected outcome underneath both descriptions.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a recent decision where the way an option was described — rather than the underlying facts — might have swayed you. If you'd heard the same facts phrased the opposite way, would you have chosen differently?
