# 14 — Priming and the Replication Crisis

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Priming is the idea that exposure to a word, image, or concept can unconsciously activate associated ideas and measurably influence unrelated behavior shortly afterward — evidence, Kahneman argued, that System 1's associative machinery runs constantly and silently, coloring judgments and actions you'd swear were unaffected by anything you'd just seen or read. Kahneman devoted an early chapter of the book to priming research as some of the most striking evidence for System 1's reach. It's also, by his own later account, the weakest-supported material in the book.

## Why it matters
This chunk exists as a deliberate caveat, because taking the whole catalog of biases at face value without this caveat would misrepresent where the book's evidence base actually stands today. *Thinking, Fast and Slow* was published in 2011, just before psychology's replication crisis became widely acknowledged — a period in which many published findings, particularly in social priming research, were found not to reproduce reliably when other labs tried to repeat the original experiments with larger samples and stricter methods. Several of the specific priming studies Kahneman cited approvingly turned out to be among the casualties.

## Example from the book
Kahneman's most prominent example was the "Florida effect": a study by John Bargh in which subjects unscrambled sentences containing words associated with old age (Florida, wrinkle, bingo, grey), and were then observed walking measurably more slowly down a hallway afterward than subjects who'd unscrambled neutral sentences — offered as evidence that an abstract concept (aging) could unconsciously prime a physical behavior (walking speed) without any awareness on the subject's part. Subsequent large-scale replication attempts by other labs failed to reproduce this effect under more rigorous conditions. Kahneman, notably, did not defend the finding once the replication failures accumulated. In a 2012 open letter to colleagues in the priming research field, he wrote that he saw a "train wreck looming" for social priming research specifically, and in later public comments acknowledged he had "placed too much faith in underpowered studies" — an unusually candid, on-the-record retraction of confidence from an author revisiting his own bestselling book.

## Practical application
Read the priming-related material in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* — concentrated mainly in the early chapters on associative memory and the "ideomotor effect" — with more skepticism than the rest of the book, and treat it as illustrative of a real phenomenon (System 1 operates outside conscious awareness) rather than as settled science built on any one specific experiment. This doesn't extend to the book's core architecture: loss aversion, anchoring, the planning fallacy, framing effects, and prospect theory itself all rest on far more robust, heavily replicated evidence and have held up considerably better than the priming chapter. The right lesson isn't to discount the whole book — it's to know which specific claims are on solid ground and which aren't, which is exactly the distinction Kahneman himself later drew in public.

## Something to sit with

> Now that you know part of the book's evidence base didn't hold up, does that change how much weight you put on the rest of it — or does Kahneman's own public correction actually make you trust the parts that remain more, not less?
