# 07 — Regression to the Mean

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Any measurement that involves an element of chance will tend to be followed by a measurement closer to the average — not because of any causal force pulling it back, but simply because extreme results are, by definition, unusual, and unusually good or bad luck is unlikely to repeat itself twice in a row. Regression to the mean is a purely statistical fact about variance, but System 1 has no natural feel for statistics — instead, it reaches for a causal story to explain the pattern, and usually gets the causal story backward.

## Why it matters
This is one of the most consistently misread patterns in everyday judgment because it produces an illusion that punishment works better than reward, or that intervention caused an improvement that would have happened anyway. Any time performance, health, or results fluctuate around a stable underlying level, an extreme outcome will tend to be followed by a more average one — regardless of what anyone does in between. Mistaking this statistical regularity for a causal effect leads people to draw the wrong lesson from feedback constantly: crediting whatever they did right before things improved, or blaming whatever they did right before things got worse.

## Example from the book
Kahneman describes his own experience training Israeli Air Force flight instructors, who insisted that praising cadets after a good landing reliably made their next landing worse, while harsh criticism after a bad landing reliably made the next one better — concluding that criticism works and praise backfires. Kahneman recognized this as pure regression to the mean: an unusually good landing is likely to be followed by a more average one regardless of what the instructor says, and an unusually bad landing is likewise likely to be followed by something closer to average — the instructors had built an entire theory of effective pedagogy on a statistical artifact, because a causal story ("my words caused this") is what System 1 reaches for by default, while "that was just noise" is not an intuitive explanation at all.

## Practical application
Before crediting or blaming an intervention for a change in an extreme result — a bad quarter followed by a good one after a new manager arrived, a slump followed by a rebound after a coaching change — ask whether regression to the mean alone would predict the same pattern. This is especially important when evaluating your own decisions after an unusually good or bad outcome: an extreme result, positive or negative, is statistically likely to be followed by something more moderate regardless of what you do differently next time.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a time you praised or blamed something for a change that followed an unusually good or bad result. Could that change have been explained just as well by regression to the mean?
