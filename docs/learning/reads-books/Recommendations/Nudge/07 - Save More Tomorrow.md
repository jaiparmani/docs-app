# 07 — Save More Tomorrow

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Save More Tomorrow (SMarT) is a retirement-savings program Thaler designed with economist Shlomo Benartzi, and it's the book's flagship example of choice architecture built directly on top of the earlier ideas — the Planner/Doer split, loss aversion, status-quo bias — rather than around them. The design problem it solves: telling people to save more of their *current* paycheck runs straight into loss aversion, because a smaller paycheck this month feels like an immediate, visceral loss, even if the saved money is unambiguously good for the saver's future. SMarT sidesteps the conflict by asking employees to commit, in advance, to having a portion of their *future* raises — money they don't have yet and haven't adjusted their spending around — automatically diverted into retirement savings each time a raise takes effect. Take-home pay in dollar terms never goes down; it just grows more slowly than it would have.

## Why it matters
SMarT is worth its own chunk because it's not just a clever idea — it's the clearest illustration in the book of choice architecture built by someone who understood exactly which biases she was working with rather than fighting against. It uses present bias by moving the "cost" of saving into the future, where people are far less loss-averse about the same dollar amount than they are about a cut to money already in hand. It uses status-quo bias by making the increased contribution automatic once enrolled, rather than something the employee has to actively re-authorize at each raise. And it uses the Planner/Doer framework directly: the employee's Planner self makes one commitment, once, at a moment of reflection, and the Doer self never gets a fresh opportunity to veto it later at the moment temptation would normally win. The program is also a direct answer to the "libertarian paternalism" skeptics — nobody is forced to enroll, and anyone can leave at any time — but the design is deliberately not neutral, because the authors built it to correct a bias they'd already documented, not to be agnostic about which direction was better.

## Example from the book
Thaler and Benartzi tested SMarT with employees at a mid-sized manufacturing company who had, in a prior consultation with a financial advisor, been told they should be saving more but declined to raise their contribution rate immediately. Offered the SMarT alternative instead — commit now to a smaller automatic increase timed to future raises — the large majority who were offered the plan joined it, stayed enrolled through multiple raises, and saw their savings rates climb substantially over the next few years, all without a single moment where their take-home pay actually dropped. The employees hadn't changed how loss-averse they were, and they hadn't developed new willpower. The program had simply moved the decision to a moment where loss aversion wasn't working against it, and then let inertia — the same force that normally keeps people from saving more — keep them enrolled instead of working against them.

## Practical application
Look for places in your own life where you can apply the same structure: separate the *commitment* from the *pain*, by tying the commitment to a future event that doesn't touch what you already have. If a raise, bonus, or windfall is coming, decide in advance what fraction of it goes straight to savings or debt paydown before it lands in an account you already think of as "yours" — many employers let you set a percentage-of-raise or percentage-of-bonus contribution rule ahead of time, which recreates the SMarT structure directly. The same logic works outside money: commit your future self to something (an extra half hour of exercise once a project wraps, a screen-time limit that only takes effect next month) rather than asking your present self to give something up right now.

> [!question]- A question to think about
