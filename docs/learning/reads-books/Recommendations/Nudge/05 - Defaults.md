# 05 — Defaults

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Of every tool available to a choice architect, Thaler and Sunstein treat the default option as the single most powerful — the option a person ends up with if they do nothing at all. Because Humans are subject to status-quo bias (a preference for things staying as they are, independent of whether the alternative is better), inertia (simply not getting around to changing a setting), and a tendency to read a default as an implicit recommendation ("if this is what's pre-selected, it's probably what most people should pick, or what the experts think is sensible"), defaults exert influence far beyond what the ease of opting out would predict. In principle, switching from one option to another costs almost nothing — a form field, a checkbox. In practice, that small cost is enough to keep the overwhelming majority of people wherever the default put them.

## Why it matters
This is where the book stops being descriptive psychology and starts being a design lever with measurable, real-world consequences. If defaults were weak — if people freely and thoughtfully opted out whenever the default wasn't quite right for them — then choice architecture wouldn't much matter, because outcomes would converge on what people actually wanted regardless of the starting point. But the evidence Thaler and Sunstein marshal shows the opposite: default rates of enrollment, participation, or opt-in are often astonishingly sticky, holding steady even when the "effort" required to switch is trivially small. That means whoever sets the default isn't offering a neutral starting point — they're making a decision that a large fraction of the population will simply inherit. Setting a default thoughtlessly, or in whatever direction is administratively convenient, has the same real-world effect as mandating that outcome for most people, even though no one was technically forced to do anything.

## Example from the book
The book's central illustration is the design of employer retirement plans. Under a traditional opt-in system, an employee has to actively fill out paperwork to start contributing to a 401(k) — and a substantial share of eligible employees simply never get around to it, not because they've decided saving isn't worthwhile, but because the form sits unfilled. Thaler and Sunstein cite research on companies that flipped the default: instead of "enrolled only if you sign up," new hires became "enrolled automatically unless you opt out." The financial choice available to each employee was identical either way — anyone could end up contributing or not contributing under both systems. But participation rates rose sharply and quickly under automatic enrollment, because the default absorbed all the people who would have kept meaning to sign up and never did. Nothing about their actual preferences changed; only the starting point did.

## Practical application
Audit the defaults you're currently living inside rather than assuming you chose them. Check whether your retirement contribution is set at whatever your employer's default happened to be rather than what you'd pick if you sat down and calculated it fresh. Check app notification settings, subscription auto-renewals, and privacy settings — most of these were never actively chosen by you, they were inherited from whoever built the product and decided what was easiest to ship. And if you're building anything other people will use — a form, a shared doc template, a signup flow — treat the default value as a real decision with real consequences, not an afterthought, because most of your users will never touch it.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Pick one recurring default you're currently living with — a retirement contribution rate, a notification setting, a subscription tier. Did you choose it, or did you just never get around to changing it?
