# 02 — Biases and Blunders

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
Drawing directly on Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics-and-biases research, Thaler and Sunstein catalogue the specific, predictable ways Human judgment departs from the Econ ideal. Anchoring: an irrelevant starting number (even one you know is random) still pulls your subsequent estimate toward it. The availability heuristic: you judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind, so vivid, recent, or media-covered risks feel more probable than they are. Representativeness: you judge probability by resemblance to a mental stereotype rather than by actual base rates. Add to these a persistent optimism bias (most people rate their own risk of divorce, illness, or job loss below the population average) and loss aversion (losses register roughly twice as painfully as equivalent gains feel good). None of these are occasional lapses — they're the default operating mode.

## Why it matters
These biases aren't random noise that cancels out in aggregate; they're systematic, which means they push large populations in the same predictable direction at once. That predictability is exactly what makes choice architecture possible — and necessary. A form designer who understands that people anchor on the first number they see, or that people weight a loss more heavily than an equivalent gain, can either exploit that tendency (as plenty of marketers do) or design around it to help people reach the decision they'd actually endorse on reflection. Ignoring the biases doesn't make institutions neutral; it just means the institution's design accidentally interacts with them instead of deliberately accounting for them.

## Example from the book
The authors describe the anchoring effect using a classic demonstration: ask people to spin a wheel that lands on a number, then ask them to estimate an unrelated quantity — like the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. People who spun a low number gave lower estimates than people who spun a high number, even though everyone in the room could see the wheel had nothing to do with the question. The anchor still moved the answer. Thaler and Sunstein connect this directly to real-world choice architecture: a "suggested donation" amount printed on a form, or a "recommended tip" percentage on a receipt, functions the same way — it's an anchor, and most people's final answer drifts toward it regardless of what they'd have chosen unprompted.

## Practical application
Before accepting a number that's been suggested to you — a recommended tip, a "starting bid," a pre-filled donation amount, a first salary offer in a negotiation — pause and generate your own independent estimate first, before looking at theirs. Writing your number down before you see the anchor is a simple, low-effort way to blunt its pull. It won't eliminate the bias, but the research the authors cite suggests it measurably reduces how far you drift toward a number that was never meant to inform your answer.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Recall the last time a "suggested" or "recommended" number was presented to you before you had to name your own — a tip, a bid, a donation, a salary figure. How much do you think that number moved your final answer, even if you felt sure you were deciding independently?
