# 01 — Humans vs. Econs

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
Thaler and Sunstein open by naming the fictional creature classical economics is built on: the "Econ" — a being with unlimited computational power, perfect self-control, and no systematic biases, who calculates the utility-maximizing choice in every situation from tax law to cereal brands. Real people are not Econs. They're "Humans": bounded in attention, prone to procrastination, swayed by how a question is phrased, and often unable to solve problems that are, in principle, easy. The book's entire project rests on taking that gap seriously instead of assuming it away, because most institutions — retirement plans, medical forms, loan contracts — are still designed as if everyone using them were an Econ.

## Why it matters
This distinction is the hinge the rest of the book swings on. If people were Econs, the design of a choice wouldn't matter — they'd unpack any menu, form, or contract to find the objectively best option regardless of how it was presented, so there'd be nothing for "choice architecture" to do. Because people are Humans, the presentation *is* part of the decision: two menus offering identical options can produce very different outcomes depending on defaults, framing, and complexity. That's not a minor caveat to economic theory — it's the opening the authors use to argue that institutions have no way to be neutral. Someone designs the form, and that design will influence the outcome whether or not the designer intends it to.

## Example from the book
Thaler and Sunstein point to something as mundane as a company cafeteria to make the point concrete: whoever arranges the food — at eye level, at the start of the line, or tucked away at the end — is shaping what people eat, whether or not they ever think of themselves as making a decision at all. An Econ would walk the entire cafeteria, evaluate every item, and choose optimally regardless of layout. A Human, rushed and hungry, disproportionately picks what's first and easiest to see. The layout was never neutral; someone chose it, and that choice already nudged the outcome before a single tray was picked up.

## Practical application
Notice the next time you assume a choice you're facing is a neutral, symmetrical menu — a subscription's default settings, a workplace benefits form, an app's notification preferences. Ask who built the menu and what they made the path of least resistance. If you're the one designing something other people will choose from (a form, a survey, a shared document's default view), notice that "no design decision" isn't an option — you're a choice architect whether you intended to be one or not.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a recent decision you made quickly — a form you filled out, an app setting you left alone, a menu you ordered from. Would an Econ, given unlimited time and attention, have picked differently than you did?
