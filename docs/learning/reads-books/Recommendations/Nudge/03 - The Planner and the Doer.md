# 03 — The Planner and the Doer

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
To explain why willpower fails so predictably, Thaler (building on earlier work he did with Hersh Shefrin) models each person as containing two competing selves: a farsighted "Planner" who wants what's good for you in the long run, and a myopic "Doer" who wants what feels good right now. This isn't a metaphor for mild inner conflict — it's structured like a principal-agent problem inside one skull, where the Planner has to design commitment devices to keep the short-term Doer in line, the same way a manager has to design incentives to keep an employee's interests aligned with the company's. Self-control problems, on this model, aren't a character flaw or a lack of trying; they're what happens whenever the Doer wins a round the Planner would have vetoed given time to reflect.

## Why it matters
This reframes "just have more willpower" as roughly as useful as telling a company "just have better management" — technically true, uselessly vague. If self-control is structurally a conflict between two selves with different time horizons, the fix isn't moral effort, it's designing the environment so the Doer's impulsive choice and the Planner's considered choice point the same direction. This is the psychological foundation underneath most of the book's later policy proposals: a default retirement contribution, an automatic savings escalation, a commitment contract — all of them work by taking a decision away from the in-the-moment Doer and letting the Planner set the terms in advance, when the Planner is actually in charge.

## Example from the book
The authors describe a simple, low-stakes illustration: a bowl of cashews put out before dinner. Nearly everyone at the table would prefer, on reflection, to eat less and save their appetite for the meal — that's the Planner's preference. But once the bowl is in front of them, the in-the-moment Doer keeps reaching for another handful. The fix that actually worked in their account wasn't willpower; it was removing the bowl from the room entirely. The Planner, acting ahead of time, structured the environment so the Doer never got the choice. That small case is the seed of the book's argument that most self-control "failures" are really *choice architecture* failures — the environment let the Doer decide when the Planner should have.

## Practical application
Identify one recurring moment where your in-the-moment self reliably overrides a decision your reflective self would make differently — checking a phone right before sleep, skipping a workout once the couch is an option, overspending once a shopping app is open. Instead of relying on willpower at the moment of temptation, act as your own Planner *before* the moment arrives: remove the phone from the bedroom, delete the app, lay out the workout clothes the night before. You're not negotiating with the Doer — you're taking the decision away from it in advance.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Where in your life is your Planner currently losing to your Doer on a decision you make repeatedly? What would it look like to redesign the environment so the Doer never gets asked in the first place?
