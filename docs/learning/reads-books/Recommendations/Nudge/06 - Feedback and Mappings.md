# 06 — Feedback and Mappings

<small>3 min read</small>

## Core idea
An Econ, facing any option, can instantly and correctly predict how choosing it will feel and what it will lead to — that's what it means to have unlimited computational power. A Human frequently can't. Thaler and Sunstein call the link between a choice and its eventual experience its "mapping," and they argue good choice architecture does two things to help Humans navigate options whose consequences aren't obvious in advance: it improves the mapping (making it easier to predict, before choosing, what an option will actually be like) and it supplies fast, clear feedback (making it easy to tell, after choosing, whether things are going well or badly, so people can course-correct). Where the mapping is poor and feedback is slow or absent, people make worse choices for longer, not because they're careless but because the system gives them nothing useful to learn from.

## Why it matters
This idea explains why some categories of Human error are stubborn even among people who are otherwise careful and well-intentioned. Choosing a health insurance plan, a mortgage structure, or a mutual fund is hard not because people are lazy, but because the mapping from "which option did I pick" to "what will my life actually be like as a result" is genuinely opaque — full of jargon, deferred consequences, and probabilistic outcomes that are hard to translate into a felt sense of "better" or "worse." Compare that to choosing a flavor of ice cream, where the mapping is immediate and the feedback is a spoon away. Thaler and Sunstein's point is that good choice architecture should do the translation work the option itself doesn't do naturally — showing a decision's likely real-world consequence in units people can actually feel, and then closing the loop quickly once they've chosen, so mistakes get corrected instead of compounding silently for years.

## Example from the book
The authors point to a redesign as simple as unit pricing on supermarket shelf tags. A shopper comparing a 14-ounce jar priced at $3.79 against a 20-ounce jar priced at $4.99 has to do real arithmetic mid-aisle to know which is actually cheaper per ounce — most people don't, and pick based on the sticker price alone. Adding a small "price per ounce" figure to the shelf tag doesn't remove any option or restrict any choice; it just improves the mapping, making the true cost of each option instantly legible instead of requiring mental math most shoppers won't do. They contrast this with domains that give almost no timely feedback at all — a 401(k) statement that arrives quarterly, showing a number without context for whether that number represents a good outcome, is a system that makes it very hard for anyone to learn from their own choices in time to fix them.

## Practical application
Before making a decision with a delayed or fuzzy payoff — a subscription plan, an insurance tier, a financial product — look specifically for whether the option is presented in units you can actually feel (cost per month you'll notice, hours you'll actually spend, not just a headline number), and if it isn't, do the translation yourself before committing. Separately, build faster feedback loops into decisions you already own: if a system in your life gives you information too late to act on it — a budget you only review annually, a habit you only assess once a year — shorten the loop, because the value of feedback comes from how quickly you can use it to adjust, not from its eventual accuracy.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of a decision you're currently locked into — a plan, a subscription, a financial product — where you genuinely don't know if it's working out well for you. What would better, faster feedback on that choice actually look like?
