# 05 — Doing More Than Is Expected

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A cluster of items addresses work and character together, on the theory that they're really the same trait applied in different settings: do a little more than what's asked of you, take pride in work quality even when no one will check it closely, admit mistakes immediately rather than letting them surface on their own, and be the same person whether or not anyone is watching. The common thread is a rejection of the minimum-viable version of effort or honesty — doing exactly enough to avoid criticism, or being honest only when dishonesty carries real risk of discovery.

## Why it matters
Most work and most character are performed for an audience of exactly no one at the moment they happen — the quality of a piece of work when the client isn't checking, or the honesty of admitting a mistake before anyone else has noticed it, both happen in private, with no external enforcement mechanism. That's precisely what makes them a real test: it's easy to be diligent and honest when someone's watching, because the incentive structure does the work for you. This cluster is arguing that the real measure of both work ethic and integrity is what you do in the unmonitored cases, because that's the only version of either trait that's actually yours rather than a response to observation.

## Example from the book
The advice here clusters around a few recurring moves: go beyond the minimum of what a task requires, even when no one has asked for more; own up to a mistake as soon as you notice it rather than waiting to see if it's discovered; and hold yourself to the same standard of conduct in private as you do in situations where you're being observed or judged. There's an implicit warning running through several of these items against the specific failure mode of being visibly good and privately careless — the gap between a public and a private self is treated as the thing to eliminate, not manage.

## Practical application
Pick one piece of work you're doing right now that no one is closely checking, and do the version of it you'd do if someone were — not out of fear, but as a deliberate test of whether your standards are actually yours or just a response to supervision. Separately, if there's a mistake you're currently sitting on hoping it won't surface, treat that as the more urgent item.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think about the last piece of work you did that no one closely inspected. Would it have held up to inspection anyway — and if not, what does that gap tell you about where your real standard actually sits?
