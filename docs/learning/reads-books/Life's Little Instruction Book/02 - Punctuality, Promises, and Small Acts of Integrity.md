# 02 — Punctuality, Promises, and Small Acts of Integrity

<small>2 min read</small>

## Core idea
A recurring cluster of items deals with the smallest, least glamorous forms of trustworthiness: showing up when you said you would, returning things you borrowed in the condition you got them, following through on casual promises as seriously as formal ones, and not saying "let's get lunch sometime" unless you mean it. None of these are dramatic acts of honesty — nobody writes a biography chapter about someone who was reliably on time. They're the low-stakes, easily-skipped commitments that most people let slide because the cost of skipping any single one feels negligible.

## Why it matters
Trust doesn't usually break on the big things — most people are honest when honesty is being watched or when the stakes are high enough to concentrate the mind. It erodes on the small, unwatched things, because those are exactly the moments where cutting a corner feels free. Someone who's chronically fifteen minutes late, or who "forgets" to return a borrowed drill, isn't committing any single serious offense, but they're teaching the people around them, one small data point at a time, that their word doesn't fully bind them. This cluster of aphorisms is really making one argument in many forms: reliability is a pattern built from unimportant instances, not a trait you can switch on for the important ones.

## Example from the book
The instructions in this vein tend to be concrete and almost mundane in their specificity — return borrowed items promptly and in good condition, be on time or call ahead if you can't be, don't make a promise you're not fully prepared to keep, follow through on the small commitments as well as the large ones. There's no item that argues for this position abstractly; the book just states the specific behavior and moves on, trusting that stacking enough of these small instructions together makes the underlying principle self-evident without ever spelling it out.

## Practical application
Pick one category of small commitment you're inconsistent about — punctuality, returning borrowed things, following up on things you said you'd do — and treat it for a week as a hard rule rather than a soft intention. Notice how much friction there is in actually doing it every time versus how easy it feels to justify skipping it "just this once." That gap is usually the whole problem.

## Something to sit with
> [!question]- A question to think about
> Think of someone whose word you trust completely. Is that reputation built on a few big moments, or on years of small, unremarkable follow-through you've mostly forgotten the specifics of?
