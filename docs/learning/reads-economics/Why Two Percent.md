# Why Two Percent

If you follow financial news at all, you have absorbed the number without ever being told where it came from. The Federal Reserve targets 2 percent inflation. So do the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan, the Bank of Canada, and most of the developed world's monetary authorities. It is repeated in press conferences with the tone of a physical constant.

It is not one. It is a judgement call that hardened into a global convention, and the interesting question is not whether 2 is correct — economists genuinely disagree — but why the answer should be a small positive number at all, rather than the intuitively obvious zero.

## What You Are Even Measuring

Inflation is the rate at which the general price level rises, which sounds precise and is not. There is no such thing as "the price"; there are millions of prices moving in different directions at different speeds. To get a single number, statistical agencies define a basket of goods and services meant to represent what a typical household buys, price it repeatedly, and track the cost over time.

Every word in that conceals a hard problem. The basket must be weighted, and the weights change as consumption shifts. When beef gets expensive and households buy more chicken, a fixed basket overstates the pain they actually experienced — substitution bias, and correcting for it requires assumptions about how readily people switch. Quality changes are harder. A laptop today costs roughly what one cost fifteen years ago and is not remotely the same product. Statisticians apply hedonic adjustments to strip out the part of a price change attributable to improved quality, which means deciding what a faster processor is worth in currency, and reasonable methodologies disagree.

None of this makes the measure useless, but the published figure carries real uncertainty. In the United States, the government-commissioned Boskin Commission in the 1990s concluded the consumer price index had been overstating true inflation, by an amount discussed at the time as on the order of a percentage point a year. Methodology has changed since and any remaining bias is debated. **The practical consequence is that zero measured inflation might be mild deflation in reality, and nobody can say precisely how mild.**

## The Case Against Zero

Set measurement aside and suppose you could hit any number exactly. Zero still would not be the obvious choice, for two reasons.

The first is about ammunition. Central banks fight recessions mainly by cutting interest rates, and what matters for borrowing and investment is the real rate — roughly the nominal rate minus expected inflation. Nominal rates face a floor at or slightly below zero, because at sufficiently negative rates holding physical cash beats holding a deposit that shrinks. If inflation runs at 2 percent, cutting the nominal rate to zero gives a real rate near minus 2 percent. If inflation runs at zero, the same cut gives zero, and the available stimulus is smaller by exactly the target. **A modest positive target is a permanent stock of policy ammunition, bought at the cost of a slow, predictable erosion of currency value.** The decade after 2008, when several major economies sat at their effective lower bound for years and improvised with asset purchases and forward guidance, is why this argument now carries more weight than it once did.

The second reason is the buffer. Aiming at zero in a world of measurement error and unpredictable shocks means accepting a substantial probability of landing below zero — and deflation is not inflation with the sign flipped.

## Why Below Zero Is Worse Than Above

Start with behaviour. If prices are broadly expected to be lower next year, deferring a purchase becomes profitable. Individually sensible; collectively, deferred purchases are reduced demand, which pushes prices down further, and the expectation validates itself.

Then debt, which is written in nominal terms. A mortgage does not shrink because the price level fell; if anything the borrower's wages fall while the payment stays fixed, so the real burden rises. Households and firms cut spending to service debt, further reducing demand. Irving Fisher described this debt-deflation dynamic while looking at the Great Depression, and it remains the standard account of how a price decline becomes a spiral.

Wages add a third layer. Nominal wages are famously sticky downward — employers find outright pay cuts corrosive to morale in a way a below-inflation raise is not. Mild inflation lets relative wages adjust without anyone's paycheque falling. Under deflation that mechanism is gone and adjustment happens through layoffs instead. Japan's long struggle with persistently low and sometimes negative inflation from the 1990s onward is the reference case for how hard this is to escape once expectations settle.

## Why Not Higher, Then

If a positive target buys room, why not 4 or 5 percent? Because it also has to be low enough to be ignorable. Nobody negotiating a salary or signing a two-year lease at 2 percent does elaborate hedging arithmetic. As the rate climbs, that changes: contracts get indexed, wage bargaining becomes more frequent and contentious, and the price system gets noisier as a signal, because it becomes harder to separate a genuine change in relative scarcity from general drift. Higher inflation also tends to be more volatile, which raises uncertainty in long-horizon planning. The target sits where it does by trying to be high enough to matter to policymakers and low enough not to matter to anyone else.

## The Part That Actually Does the Work

Which brings us to expectations, the mechanism that makes the apparatus function. Inflation is partly self-fulfilling. If firms expect costs to rise 2 percent they set prices accordingly; if workers expect prices to rise 2 percent they bargain for it; and those decisions produce the outcome. **A central bank that is genuinely believed therefore has an instrument that costs nothing to deploy** — a credible commitment anchors the expectations that help deliver the result. One that is not believed must produce the same outcome through actual interest rate pain, expensive in output and jobs.

The number itself has a modest history. New Zealand pioneered explicit inflation targeting around 1990; others adopted the framework over the following decade or so; the Federal Reserve announced a formal 2 percent longer-run objective only in 2012, having operated with an implicit one before. The convergence on 2 was not derived from a model. It reflected practical judgement about where the ammunition and ignorability arguments balance, and spread partly by imitation.

Serious economists argue for a higher target, on the grounds that the lower bound binds more often than anyone expected; others defend the current level, on the grounds that a target you have changed once is a target you might change again. Central banks have adjusted the framing — the Fed's 2020 shift toward tolerating periods above target to offset periods below, the ECB's 2021 move to an explicitly symmetric formulation — while leaving the figure alone.

That reluctance is the tell. If 2 were optimal, the case for defending it would be that it is optimal. The actual case is that it is established, which is not a weaker argument but a different one: the target's real function is to give millions of independent price-setters a common reference point. A coordination point does not need to be the best available option. It needs everyone to believe everyone else is using it. Which is why the number is arbitrary and changing it is not.
