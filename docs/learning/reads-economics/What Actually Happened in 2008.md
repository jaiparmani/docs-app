# What Actually Happened in 2008

<small>6 min read</small>

Most explanations of the 2008 financial crisis fail in one of two directions. Either they collapse into a morality play with a single villain — greedy bankers, reckless borrowers, asleep regulators, take your pick — or they retreat into jargon so dense the reader concludes it was too complicated to have been anyone's fault. The truth is that it was a chain of individually reasonable decisions that combined into something nobody had designed and few could see. That is the less satisfying story and the more useful one, because chains like it are still being built.

## The Assumption Underneath Everything

Start with a belief, because the belief is load-bearing. For a long stretch of postwar history, house prices in the United States had not fallen substantially on a broad national basis. Regional busts happened — oil-town Texas, parts of New England — but the national aggregate held. From this, an assumption got baked into models, contracts, and confidence across the system: house price declines are local, and a portfolio of mortgages spread across many regions is therefore diversified.

If that assumption is true, an enormous amount of what followed is defensible. A mortgage where the borrower's income is shaky is still a reasonably safe asset, because the collateral is a house and houses appreciate; worst case, you foreclose and recover. Pool thousands of such mortgages from different states and the pool is safer still, because they cannot all go bad together. **Almost every subsequent piece of the machine was a rational construction on top of an assumption that turned out to be false.**

## Lending Loosens

Through the boom years, mortgage lending standards degraded. Products proliferated that required little documentation of income, or offered a low teaser rate that reset sharply after two or three years, or let the borrower make payments smaller than the accruing interest. Some borrowers did not understand the terms. Others understood them perfectly and were speculating, planning to refinance or sell before the reset. Both groups were making the same bet as the lender: that prices would keep rising.

What made this degradation possible at scale was a change in who bore the loss. In the older model, a bank made a loan, kept it on its books, and lived with the consequence for thirty years. That is a powerful incentive to check whether the borrower can pay. In the model that came to dominate, the originator made the loan and sold it on within weeks. The originator's income was the fee for making the loan, not the interest over its life. **The party best positioned to judge the borrower's creditworthiness no longer had much stake in the answer.**

## Securitization and the Ratings

The sale-on mechanism was securitization. Mortgages were pooled, and the pool's cash flows were sliced into tranches with different priority: the senior tranche got paid first and absorbed losses last; junior tranches absorbed losses first in exchange for higher yield. This is not inherently sinister. It is a genuinely useful piece of financial engineering that lets investors with different risk appetites hold different parts of the same underlying asset, and it channelled global savings into housing credit that would otherwise have been unavailable.

The problem was that the machinery depended on judging how much protection the senior tranche really had, and that judgement was largely outsourced to credit rating agencies. Many pension funds, insurers, and bank treasuries either cannot or will not hold assets below a certain rating, so a top rating was not merely an opinion — it was the key that unlocked the largest pools of capital in the world. The agencies were paid by the issuers whose securities they rated, a conflict widely noted afterward, though the deeper issue may have been simpler: their models inherited the same regional-diversification assumption as everyone else. If house prices could not fall everywhere at once, senior tranches genuinely were very safe. When correlation went to one, the diversification that justified the rating evaporated.

## Layers, Leverage, and Opacity

On top of this, further layers accumulated. Tranches of mortgage securities were repackaged into new pooled securities, which were themselves tranched. Credit default swaps let institutions take positions on whether these securities would default without owning them, which meant the total exposure riding on a given pool of mortgages could exceed the value of the mortgages themselves. Much of this sat in entities kept off banks' main balance sheets, and much of it was financed with borrowed money, so small percentage losses translated into large hits to equity.

The cumulative effect was opacity. Working out any given institution's true exposure to American housing required tracing through several layers of repackaging and a web of bilateral contracts. Firms did not have that picture about their counterparties, and there is reasonable evidence that many did not have it about themselves.

## When the Funding Stopped

House prices peaked and turned down, delinquencies rose, and the assumption failed. Losses appeared, and because of leverage they were amplified. But the losses alone did not produce the crisis. The mechanism that produced the crisis was funding.

Large financial institutions did not fund themselves primarily with retail deposits. They borrowed short-term in wholesale markets — repurchase agreements, commercial paper — and rolled that borrowing over continuously, often overnight, against long-lived assets. This works as long as lenders keep rolling. Once nobody could tell which securities were worth what, and therefore which institutions were solvent, the rational move for any individual lender was to stop rolling and demand cash. That is a bank run, conducted by institutions rather than queues of depositors, and it can drain a firm in days regardless of whether it was fundamentally solvent.

Bear Stearns went in March 2008, absorbed by JPMorgan Chase in a government-facilitated deal. Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy in September 2008. AIG, which had written enormous volumes of credit protection, required a government rescue in the same month. Interbank lending seized, because the question "is my counterparty good for it tomorrow?" had become genuinely unanswerable. Uncertainty about solvency, not certainty of insolvency, was what froze the system.

The response was extraordinary in scale and largely improvised: emergency central bank liquidity, guarantees, capital injections into banks, and eventually fiscal stimulus. Regulatory reform followed, notably Dodd-Frank in the United States in 2010 and the international Basel III standards, pushing toward higher capital and liquidity requirements, regular stress testing, and more central clearing of derivatives. Whether these reforms are adequate is still argued.

## What Generalizes

Honest accounts disagree about the weighting. Some economists emphasize monetary policy and global capital flows; others regulatory failure, housing policy, ratings conflicts, or compensation structures that rewarded volume. These are live disputes among serious people, and anyone offering a clean single cause is selling something.

But three features generalize past finance entirely. Complexity that outruns comprehension does not eliminate risk, it hides it, and hidden risk is priced as though it were absent. Any structure that separates the person making a decision from the person who bears its consequence will eventually produce decisions that nobody would have made while holding both ends. And the most dangerous assumption in any system is not the one that is wrong, but the one that is wrong and universally shared — because shared assumptions produce correlated failures, and correlated failure is precisely what all the diversification was supposed to protect against.
