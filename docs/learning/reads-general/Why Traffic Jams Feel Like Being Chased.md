# Why Traffic Jams Feel Like Being Chased

<small>6 min read</small>

You are sitting in stationary traffic. Nothing is happening. No one is hurt, nothing is on fire, and the worst realistic outcome is that you arrive somewhere late and mildly embarrassed. And yet your jaw is tight, your heart is going faster than it should be for a person who is technically seated, and there is a hot restless feeling in your chest that wants you to *do* something — swerve, honk, take the exit, anything. Your body has quietly concluded that you are being hunted.

It is not confused, exactly. It is running a very old program with excellent engineering and a badly outdated input filter.

## The Two-Speed Alarm

When your brain flags a threat, it does not send one signal. It sends two, on different timescales, and the difference between them turns out to matter enormously.

The first is nearly instant. Before you have consciously identified what startled you, the sympathetic nervous system fires and the adrenal glands dump adrenaline into your bloodstream. Within seconds, your heart rate and the force of each contraction jump. Blood vessels constrict in some places and dilate in others, shunting circulation away from your skin and gut and toward the large muscles of your legs and arms. Your airways open wider. Your pupils dilate. Stored energy — glucose and fat — is released into the blood so your muscles have fuel available immediately rather than after digestion. Your blood becomes slightly more prone to clotting, which is exactly what you want if the next thirty seconds involve a laceration. Pain perception is blunted. Attention narrows onto the source of the threat, which is thought to be part of why people in emergencies often cannot recall peripheral details.

The second wave is slower and involves a hormonal cascade running from the brain down to the adrenal glands, ending in the release of cortisol over the following minutes. Cortisol is the sustaining signal. It keeps glucose available, keeps the emergency budget open, and continues to deprioritize everything the body considers optional in a crisis: digestion, tissue repair, growth, reproduction, and parts of the immune response.

That last point is the whole design philosophy. The stress response is not really a "danger" response. It is a **resource reallocation** response. It borrows aggressively from the future to fund the next few minutes. Anything that pays off over weeks gets defunded so that anything that pays off in seconds can be maximally funded.

For a short, physical, resolvable problem, this is superb. You sprint, you climb, you fight, you escape — and then the crisis ends. The parasympathetic system takes over, the brake to the accelerator. Heart rate drops, digestion resumes, cortisol falls back toward baseline, and the loan gets repaid. The system was built to be switched on hard and then switched off cleanly.

## The Off Switch Is the Point

Here is the part that gets underappreciated: the elegance of the acute stress response lies less in the activation than in the termination. Turning the alarm on is easy. Turning it off reliably, quickly, and completely is the hard engineering problem, and evolution solved it — for a specific class of threat.

The solution depends on a feature of the ancestral environment that we have quietly demolished. Ancestral threats *resolved*. The predator caught you or it didn't. The rival backed down or he didn't. The fall happened or was avoided. Either way, within minutes, the world delivered an unambiguous signal that the episode was over, and the body stood down.

Now consider what actually triggers your stress response on a normal Tuesday. A deadline eleven days out. An email you've reread four times trying to decide whether it's hostile. An argument with someone you love that ended without ending. A rent increase. A traffic jam. These share almost nothing with a predator except the one thing that matters to your physiology: they are perceived as threats. What they lack is a resolution event. There is no moment when the deadline visibly gives up and wanders off. The threat is real, but it is *diffuse and durable*, and so the off signal never clearly arrives.

The result is not a strong stress response. It is a **low-grade stress response that doesn't end** — which is a fundamentally different thing, not simply more of the same.

## Zebras and Ulcers

This is the framing Robert Sapolsky made famous in *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers*, and it is one of those titles that does most of the explanatory work on its own. A zebra fleeing a lion experiences a stress response far more violent than anything you will feel in traffic. Its physiology goes to a place yours probably never will. And then, a few minutes later, assuming it survives, it is grazing again. The episode is over, biologically as well as narratively. The zebra does not spend the afternoon replaying the chase, or anticipating next week's chase, or wondering whether the lion's body language implied something about its standing in the herd.

We do. Our capacity for abstraction — for modeling futures, rehearsing conversations, simulating outcomes — is arguably the defining human cognitive achievement, and it is wired directly into a threat-detection system that cannot distinguish an imagined predator from a real one. We can activate the full emergency response using nothing but thought, and we can sustain it for weeks, because the imagined threat is always available and never resolves.

The broad scientific consensus is that chronic activation of these systems carries a cost, and that the cost shows up precisely in the places the emergency budget defunds — cardiovascular strain from a system held at elevated pressure, disruption of sleep and digestion, altered immune function, and effects on the brain regions involved in memory and emotional regulation. This is not a claim about you specifically, and none of it is a reason to be alarmed about your own alarm system; it is a description of what happens when a mechanism designed for minutes is asked to run for months.

## We Are Overqualified for a Job That No Longer Exists

The tempting conclusion is that humans are bad at handling stress. The more interesting conclusion is the opposite.

We are *extraordinarily* good at handling stress — one particular kind. Give a person a short, physical, unambiguous crisis and their body will deliver a coordinated whole-organism response of genuinely remarkable sophistication, mobilizing energy, reprioritizing organ systems, sharpening attention, and suppressing pain within seconds, then unwinding all of it cleanly afterward. It is one of the more impressive pieces of machinery in the animal kingdom.

The mismatch is not a deficiency in us. It's that we became so successful at eliminating the threats we're built for that we replaced them almost entirely with threats we're not. Modern life is, by historical standards, spectacularly safe from the acute and spectacularly dense with the chronic. We removed the lions and installed inboxes.

So the tight jaw in traffic is not a malfunction, and it isn't weakness. It is a superbly tuned instrument responding correctly to a signal it was never designed to receive — sounding an alarm built for a threat that ends, about a threat that doesn't.
