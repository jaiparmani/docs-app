# The Actual Reason Rent Keeps Going Up

<small>7 min read</small>

Every expensive city has its own folk explanation for why it became expensive. In one, it's foreign buyers parking money in empty condos. In another, it's Airbnb hollowing out the rental stock. In a third, it's private equity firms buying single-family homes, or landlords who simply decided to charge more because they could. These stories share a satisfying shape: someone is doing something, and if they stopped, prices would fall.

The trouble isn't that these explanations are false. Most of them describe something real. The trouble is that they're almost all downstream of a more basic condition, and they only become severe when that condition is present. A city with room to build absorbs a wave of investor demand or short-term rentals with a shrug — a few thousand more units get built, and the market moves on. A city that cannot build absorbs the same wave entirely through price. The villain in each story is real; the reason the villain matters so much is structural.

## The Only Variable That Can Move

Start with the plainest possible framing. When a city becomes economically desirable — good jobs appear, wages rise, people want to live near other people who are doing interesting things — demand for housing rises fast. It can rise in a year. A tech boom, a new industry cluster, a wave of remote workers choosing somewhere pleasant: these change demand on the timescale of a hiring cycle.

Housing supply cannot respond on that timescale. Even in the friendliest regulatory environment, a building takes years: land assembly, financing, design, permits, construction. In an unfriendly environment, it may never happen at all. And the environment is often unfriendly by design. Single-family zoning makes it illegal to build anything but a detached house across enormous fractions of many American cities. Height limits cap how much can go on a given parcel. Minimum parking requirements force developers to build expensive structured parking whether or not future residents want cars, which can make small and mid-sized projects financially impossible. Discretionary review processes give neighbors repeated opportunities to delay, shrink, or kill a proposal. Environmental review statutes, written for genuinely good reasons, are routinely used as procedural leverage against infill apartments.

Put those two facts together and you get the whole mechanism. Demand moved quickly, quantity cannot move, and so the adjustment has to happen somewhere. It happens in price. **When quantity is prevented from responding, price does all the work.** Rent isn't rising because landlords got greedier; landlords were always trying to charge the maximum the market would bear. What changed is what the market will bear, and that changed because more people are competing for a nearly fixed number of units.

## Why Housing Is Weird Among Goods

Most goods are not like this. If demand for laptops doubles, factories run extra shifts and prices barely move. If demand for coffee rises, more land goes into coffee. Supply is elastic — quantity responds, and price stays roughly anchored to cost.

Housing is unusually inelastic, and for reasons that stack. It's immobile: you cannot ship an apartment from Cleveland to San Francisco, so local shortages stay local. It's durable, meaning the stock adjusts only at the margin — new construction in a typical year is a small percentage of what already exists, so even a construction boom moves the total slowly. Land is genuinely finite in the places people most want to be. And on top of all the physical constraints sits a legal layer that binds harder than the physical one. In much of a desirable city, the binding constraint on building more homes is not engineering or economics. It's that it is illegal.

## What Filtering Means, and Why It Annoys People

Here is the part that reliably generates arguments. New construction in expensive cities tends to be expensive — glass towers with amenity decks, priced for people who already have money. It's natural to conclude that this kind of building doesn't help, and might even hurt.

The economic counterargument is called **filtering**. Housing units age. A building that was luxury in 1975 is ordinary in 2005 and modest by 2035. The overall stock drifts downward in relative price as it ages, which means today's expensive new construction becomes tomorrow's mid-market housing. More immediately, when a well-paid household moves into a new luxury unit, it vacates an older unit somewhere else, which someone else takes, and so on down a chain. Researchers have tried to trace these chains directly, following who moves where after new buildings open, and the general finding is that the effect is real and reaches beyond the top of the market — that adding units at any price point loosens competition for existing ones.

That's the mechanism. How much it matters is where honest disagreement begins.

## Where the Argument Actually Is

It would be dishonest to present this as settled in every respect. Among economists there is broad agreement that supply constraints are a major driver of high housing costs in expensive metros — this is close to consensus, and the evidence for it is strong. Beyond that, there's real debate.

There's debate about magnitude and speed: how much does a given amount of new construction lower rents, over what geography, and how fast? Effects that show up clearly at the neighborhood or metro level may be modest relative to the size of the affordability problem, and they take time. There's debate about demand-side factors — investor purchases, second homes, short-term rentals, and the sheer force of income growth at the top — which some argue deserve more weight than the supply story gives them. There's debate about whether supply alone is sufficient. Even many economists sympathetic to deregulation argue that market-rate construction cannot on its own house the lowest-income households, and that subsidies, vouchers, or public and nonprofit development are necessary complements rather than optional extras. And there are serious arguments about displacement in the short run, where new investment can raise prices in a specific neighborhood even while easing them regionally.

None of these disagreements rescue the folk explanations. They're arguments about how much of the problem the supply story explains, not whether it's the main structure underneath.

## The Trap Is Made of Voters

The most uncomfortable part of this isn't economic at all. It's that the constraint is politically self-reinforcing, and the reinforcement runs through entirely legitimate democratic machinery.

For most homeowners, the house is not just shelter. It's the largest asset they own, often the bulk of their retirement plan, and frequently leveraged. Anything that plausibly lowers local home prices is, from that vantage point, a direct threat to household net worth. You don't need to attribute bad faith to anyone. A person can sincerely want housing to be affordable in the abstract and sincerely oppose the specific building proposed down the street, and those two positions coexist comfortably in most people's heads.

Now add who shows up. Local elections, planning commission hearings, and zoning board meetings draw a fraction of the electorate, and that fraction skews sharply toward older, longer-tenured, homeowning residents. Renters vote less in local races, move more often, and are far less likely to spend a Tuesday evening at a public comment session. The people who would benefit most from more housing are disproportionately people who haven't moved to the city yet and therefore cannot vote there at all. The costs of new construction — construction noise, changed views, more traffic, a different-looking street — are concentrated and immediate. The benefits are diffuse, delayed, and land partly on strangers.

So the honest way to describe the situation is not that the housing market failed. Markets fail when prices stop conveying information, and housing prices are conveying their information loudly and accurately: this place is scarce, bid more. The system is working exactly as designed. It's just that the design gives decisive weight to the people who already got in, and grants no standing whatsoever to the people who would come next. High rent isn't the market's verdict on a city. It's the aggregated preference of everyone who was already in the room when the vote was taken.
